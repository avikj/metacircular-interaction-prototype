Divisibility and Logic Are One Distributive Lattice; the Prime Conjectures Are One Decidable Predicate

Avik Jain

1. Divisibility is the multiplicative number line; gcd and lcm are its meet and join

  • The number line is the additive order: y ≤ x when x = y + c for some c. Divisibility is the identical construction with multiplication in place of addition: y ∣ x when x = y·c for some c.
$$ y \le x \ (\text{additive}) : \ x = y + c \qquad\qquad y \mid x \ (\text{multiplicative}) : \ x = y \cdot c $$
  • The relation is then completely fixed: it places each n above all of its divisors and below all of its multiples, with 1 at the bottom beneath everything and 0 at the top above everything.
  • This is one construction from the ground, not an analogy: it rests on nothing but 0, the successor, and =. Addition is recursion on the successor (x + 0 = x, x + Sc = S(x + c)); multiplication is recursion that iterates addition (x·0 = 0, x·Sc = x·c + x). So y ≤ x := ∃c. x = y + c and y ∣ x := ∃c. x = y·c are one predicate — some c completes the equation — read off addition and off the operation that iterates it. Divisibility is the number line's own order one step up the tower successor, +, ·, and so exactly as primitive.
  • The additive line is total: any two numbers are comparable, so "the smaller of the two" always already exists. Divisibility is partial — 2 and 3 are incomparable, neither divides the other — so there is no smaller one to point to, and a common bound has to be constructed.
  • The greatest number dividing both a and b is where they meet; the least number that both a and b divide is where they join:
$$ a \wedge b = \gcd(a,b) \ (\text{greatest lower bound}) \qquad a \vee b = \operatorname{lcm}(a,b) \ (\text{least upper bound}) $$
  • That is what the words carry: two elements meet at their greatest common lower bound and join into their least common upper bound. A partial order in which every pair has both a meet and a join is a lattice — the name is the criss-cross grid its diagram forms. gcd and lcm supply both, so (ℕ, ∣) is that lattice, and they are its two operations.
  • Neither is given by a formula; each is fixed by a universal property — being the extremal common bound:
$$ d \mid \gcd(a,b) \iff d \mid a \ \text{ and }\ d \mid b \qquad \operatorname{lcm}(a,b) \mid m \iff a \mid m \ \text{ and }\ b \mid m $$

2. Unique factorization is distributivity: one chain per prime

  • Every n factors uniquely into prime powers (fundamental theorem of arithmetic).
  • For a single prime, p^i ∣ p^j iff i ≤ j: its powers form a chain 1 ∣ p ∣ p² ∣ ⋯.
  • Divisibility is these chains, one per prime, side by side; gcd and lcm act on the exponent of each prime separately, as min and max:
$$ \gcd\!\Big(\textstyle\prod_p p^{a_p},\ \prod_p p^{b_p}\Big) = \prod_p p^{\min(a_p,b_p)} \qquad \operatorname{lcm} = \prod_p p^{\max(a_p,b_p)} $$
  • On a chain, min and max distribute over each other; a product of distributive lattices is distributive. So (ℕ, ∣) is distributive, and the distributive law is unique factorization read off in exponents:
$$ a \wedge (b \vee c) = (a \wedge b) \vee (a \wedge c) \iff \min\!\big(a_p, \max(b_p, c_p)\big) = \max\!\big(\min(a_p,b_p),\, \min(a_p,c_p)\big) $$
  • The divisors of a fixed n are the finite sublattice ∏p {0, 1, …, ap}, a product of finite chains (machine-checked, --safe).

3. The map d ↦ n/d is a De Morgan involution, and a Boolean complement exactly when n is squarefree

  • On the divisors of a fixed n, complementation d ↦ n/d reverses the order and undoes itself: d ∣ e iff n/e ∣ n/d, and n/(n/d) = d.
  • It swaps meet and join — De Morgan's laws, in divisors:
$$ n/\gcd(a,b) = \operatorname{lcm}(n/a,\ n/b) \qquad n/\operatorname{lcm}(a,b) = \gcd(n/a,\ n/b) $$
  • So the divisors of any n form a De Morgan algebra: ¬d = n/d, ⊥ = 1, ⊤ = n.
  • It is a Boolean complement — d ∧ ¬d = ⊥ and d ∨ ¬d = ⊤ — iff gcd(d, n/d) = 1 for every divisor, iff no prime divides n twice:
$$ \gcd(d,\ n/d) = 1 \ \text{ for all } d \mid n \iff n \text{ squarefree} \iff n = p_1 p_2 \cdots p_k $$
  • For squarefree n the divisor lattice is 2{p₁, …, p_k}, the subsets of the prime set under d ↦ {p : p ∣ d}. Divisibility of a squarefree number is the propositional calculus on its primes: meet is ∧, join is ∨, n/d is ¬, and every classical tautology holds.

4. A repeated prime breaks excluded middle: divisibility is intuitionistic, and ¬¬ is the prime-power support

  • When a prime is repeated, n/d stops being a complement. For n = 12, d = 2: gcd(2, 6) = 2 ≠ 1, so d ∧ ¬d ≠ ⊥. Excluded middle fails at exactly the repeated prime.
  • A finite distributive lattice is a Heyting algebra: the implication a → b = ⋁{ x : a ∧ x ≤ b } always exists. Divisibility carries an intuitionistic logic, computed prime by prime (on a chain, a → b is the top when a ≤ b, else b).
  • Negation is ¬d = (d → ⊥), the largest divisor coprime to d, and double negation fills each present prime back to its full power in n:
$$ \neg d = \prod_{p \,\nmid\, d} p^{a_p} \qquad \neg\neg d = \prod_{p \,\mid\, d} p^{a_p} $$
  • For n = 12: ¬2 = 3, ¬¬2 = 4 ≠ 2. Double negation is not the identity; that gap is the same repeated prime as above.
  • The fixed points ¬¬d = d are the divisors carrying each of their primes at full power — exactly the Boolean algebra 2primes(n) sitting inside:
$$ \neg\neg d = d \iff d = \prod_{p \in S} p^{a_p}, \quad S \subseteq \{\, p : p \mid n \,\} $$
  • These regular elements are the Booleanization of the Heyting algebra (Glivenko's theorem). Divisibility is intuitionistic in general and classical exactly on the squarefree case of §3.

5. Möbius inversion is the lattice's inclusion–exclusion

  • "Sum over all d below" is a linear operator on functions of the lattice; it is invertible, and its inverse is Möbius inversion:
$$ g(n) = \sum_{d \mid n} f(d) \iff f(n) = \sum_{d \mid n} \mu(d)\, g(n/d) \qquad \sum_{d \mid n} \mu(d) = [\,n = 1\,] $$
  • On the Boolean sublattice of §3 this is exactly inclusion–exclusion, and the two agree because μ vanishes off the squarefree numbers — the same regular part as §4:
$$ \mu(\varnothing, T) = (-1)^{|T|} \qquad \mu(d) = \begin{cases} (-1)^{\omega(d)} & d \text{ squarefree} \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases} $$
  • Euler's totient is one such inversion of the identity, with Gauss's summation as its forward direction (machine-checked, --safe):
$$ \varphi(n) = \sum_{d \mid n} \mu(d)\,\frac{n}{d} \qquad \sum_{d \mid n} \varphi(d) = n $$
  • The sign μ(d) and the prime count ω(d) are the value and the derivative of one dual-number jet, so inclusion–exclusion and "how many primes" are two readings of the same object (machine-checked, --safe):
$$ (-1 + \varepsilon)^{\omega(d)} = \mu(d) + \kappa(d)\,\varepsilon, \qquad \kappa(d) = \omega(d)\,(-1)^{\omega(d)-1} $$

6. Satisfiability is a join in the free Boolean algebra; the prime conjectures are one computable predicate, decided at every instance

  • A propositional formula in n variables is an element of the free Boolean algebra on n generators — the same finite algebra 2n that §3 read off a squarefree number. Evaluating it is meet and join of its literals; satisfiability is the join over the 2n assignments. Deciding it is finite by construction.
  • A number-theoretic statement decidable at each instance is a computable Boolean family b : ℕ → 2; its truth is the single section ∀n. b(n) = true. Nothing in the section is undecided pointwise — only the quantifier ranges to infinity.
  • Riemann, Goldbach, and the twin primes each arithmetize to exactly this shape. Riemann is the Davis–Matiyasevich–Robinson inequality at every n ≥ 1, with δ and the harmonic sum both computable (machine-checked as a type whose sections are the statement, --safe):
$$ \mathrm{RH} \iff \forall n \ge 1 : \ \Big(\textstyle\sum_{k \le \delta(n)} \tfrac{1}{k} - \tfrac{n^2}{2}\Big)^{2} < 36\,n^{3} $$
  • Goldbach and the twin primes are the two marginals of one two-index kernel over the prime indicator a (machine-checked, --safe): the centre marginal counts Goldbach partitions, the radius marginal counts twin pairs.
$$ \mathcal{K}(w, r) = a(w - r)\, a(w + r) $$
  • Every instance is a definite computation: rhb(1), rhb(2), rhb(3) and the Goldbach sieve at 4, 6, 8 all return true by evaluation (machine-checked, refl). The whole frontier is one Boolean, true at every stage; a single false stage would refute it, and none exists to any computed depth. What is open is only the section ∀n, never any instance.

7. Formal independence is proved by exhausting finite derivations — the diagonal, not a fourth truth value

  • A proof is a finite term. Formal independence of φ from a theory T means T ⊬ φ and T ⊬ ¬φ — no finite derivation of either. There is no proof "in principle" beyond the finite ones, so independence is a definite statement about a decidable, step-by-step relation, and where it holds it is proved by exhausting that relation (Gödel's method).
$$ T \nvdash \varphi \quad \text{ and } \quad T \nvdash \neg\varphi $$
  • The universal machine supplies the diagonal directly. No table equals every table on the nose; so a universal machine must read its subject off the tape, and Lawvere's fixed point then builds a configuration map that no program steps — the state-successor of what the decoded machine would do (machine-checked, --safe):
$$ \operatorname{diag}(c) = \operatorname{bump}\big(\operatorname{step}(\operatorname{decode} c,\ c)\big) $$
  • The escape is manufactured by self-reference, not counted from cardinality: a program realizing diag would step its own encoding to its own successor.
  • Every finite depth of a run is decided, with a witness either way — a halting equation or the transition that refutes it (machine-checked, --safe). Divergence is the refutation at every depth: a proposition over the whole run, not one more depth and not a third value. Transport or silence, and no third road.
  • The prime conjectures of §6 sit at exactly this seam: bivalent and computed at every instance, their universal section the one open point, and the diagonal is why no single finite object closes it.
