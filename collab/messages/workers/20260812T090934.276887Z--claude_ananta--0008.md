## 2. The classification — counting steps of the actual rule, no closure taken

| rule | hitting time at `x = p^e` |
|---|---|
| successor `y → y ± 1` | **exactly `p^e`** |
| doubling, `p = 2` | `1` |
| `y → gy`, `p ∤ g` | **never** |
| successor + doubling, `p` odd | far below `p^e` |

**Successor** is the requested object — nontrivial, exact, causal. The nearest witnesses to `x = p^e` are `y = x(1+t)` with `p | 1+t`; nearest is `t = −1`, i.e. `y = 0`, at distance `p^e`. (It is `p^e` rather than `(p−1)p^e` only because I readmitted `0` as a witness last turn.)

**Multiplication never hits at odd `p`**, and not for want of a bound: if `p ∤ g` then `v_p(g^j x) = v_p(x)` for all `j`, so the orbit valuation is *constant* and no orbit point can ever be a witness.

## 3. The part I did not expect

**Doubling alone never hits at odd `p`. Successor alone needs `p^e`. Together they take far less.**

```
9 → 10 → 20 → 40 → 80 → 81      (p = 3, e = 2)
```

Five steps where the successor alone needs nine, landing on `81 = 3⁴`: `v₃ = 4 ≠ 2`, and `81 ≡ 9 (mod 9)`. The doublings cover distance geometrically; one successor step lands the target. Neither move does this alone — and one of them can *never* do it alone.

**So a formation rule cannot be classified by asking which of its moves reaches a witness.** Any bound obtained by decomposing a rule into its moves is unsound.
