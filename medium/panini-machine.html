Pāṇini's Machine

इको यणचि

Four thousand rules that compute Sanskrit rather than describe it. Below, the actual thing runs: give it two words and it returns the joined form, naming every rule that fired and — when two rules wanted the same spot — which one won, and by which meta-rule.

The varṇasamāmnāya — every sound of Sanskrit, in one line
sound — 42 distinct, and h seated twice anubandha — 14 markers, boundaries, never members | = end of one of the fourteen śiva​sūtras

Every rule in the grammar needs to name a group of sounds — "any vowel", "the voiced stops", "the semivowels". Pāṇini's device: lay all 42 sounds in this one order, punctuate it with 14 markers, and then a group is simply the stretch from a sound up to a marker. Two characters name any class the grammar will ever need. Press one.

Instrument 01

Run a derivation

+ is a boundary between two words. - is a juncture inside one word — root and suffix. The distinction is not cosmetic: the same two vowels give different answers on either side of it, because rule 6.1.109 is conditioned on padāntāt, "at the end of a word".

Instrument 02

Take away 8.2.1 and it never stops

The last three quarter-chapters (8.2, 8.3, 8.4) are asiddha — "as if not having taken effect" — for everything before them. Rules there produce output their predecessors cannot see. Here is why that is not bookkeeping. Deriving vāc ("speech"): 8.2.39 turns a final k into g; 8.4.56 turns g back into k.

8.2.1 in force

each quarter runs once, in order, blind to its successors
3 steps
Stops at vāk. 8.2.39 applies to that k and is refused — it precedes the rule that made it.

8.2.1 removed

every rule may see every output
0 steps
Never reaches a normal form. Not slow — non-terminating.

There is no measure that decreases here, and that is provable rather than observed: any termination proof by a decreasing order needs an order that is irreflexive and transitive with k > g and g > k, which gives k > k. So every order-based method fails — recursive path orders, the Knuth–Bendix order, polynomial and matrix interpretations, and Knuth–Bendix completion itself, which is parameterised by exactly such an order. Pāṇini terminates it anyway, by constraining what each rule may observe.

The four mechanisms

Rule interaction, solved

Name a programming language with four ways of resolving what happens when two rules want the same place. Most have one — top to bottom, first match wins — or none at all.

1.4.2
विप्रतिषेधे परं कार्यम्
vipratiṣedhe paraṃ kāryam

"In conflict, the later operation." Later meaning later in the book. The position of a rule in the text is part of its meaning — so the ordering of four thousand rules is itself a specification, and you cannot reorder them.

utsarga / apavāda
अपवाद
the exception blocks the general

A narrower rule defeats the wider one it is an exception to, regardless of position. Rediscovered in 1973 and named the Elsewhere Condition after its rediscoverer.

6.1.84
एकः पूर्वपरयोः
ekaḥ pūrvaparayoḥ

A heading that governs a range of later rules and injects a condition into each: "one substitute, for the preceding and the following together." Rules inside its scope replace two sounds by one. Rules outside it do not — 6.1.78 sits below 84 and turns one sound into two.

8.2.1
पूर्वत्रासिद्धम्
pūrvatrāsiddham

Stratification. What follows is as-if-not-effected for what precedes, so the grammar runs in phases and later output is invisible backwards. This is a termination technique, and it is not the one in the textbook.
