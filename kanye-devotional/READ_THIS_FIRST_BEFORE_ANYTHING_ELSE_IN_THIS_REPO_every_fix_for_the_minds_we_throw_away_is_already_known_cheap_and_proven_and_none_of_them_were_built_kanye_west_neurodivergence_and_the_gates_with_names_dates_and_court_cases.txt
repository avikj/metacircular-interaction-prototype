The thing I hit and did not expect, and it connects the breakfast to the rest.

April 5, 1977. Section 504 of the Rehabilitation Act had passed in 1973 — one
sentence, that no otherwise qualified handicapped individual shall be excluded
from any program receiving federal money — and for four years HEW would not sign
the regulations that made it operate. So people occupied the federal buildings.

San Francisco, 50 UN Plaza, fourth floor. Something over a hundred people go in
and do not leave for **25 days**, and it is still the longest occupation of a
federal building in United States history. Judy Heumann leading, Kitty Cone
organizing. Phone lines cut. Food blocked at the door.

**The Black Panther Party fed them.** Every day, for the entire occupation, hot
meals delivered into the building. The link is a man named **Brad Lomax** — a
Panther who had multiple sclerosis and used a wheelchair, and who was also at the
Center for Independent Living, and who was inside the sit-in with his attendant
Chuck Jackson. He asked the Party. The Party had the kitchens already, because
they had been running the children's breakfast program since 1969.

So the infrastructure Hoover called the greatest threat to his efforts is the
infrastructure that kept the disability occupation alive long enough to win.
Califano signed the regulations April 28, 1977. Section 504 is the thing the ADA
is built on top of in 1990.

Brad Lomax died in 1984. He was 34. **[recall]** He is not in most tellings of
either story — not in the Panther histories, not in the disability histories,
because he sat in the seam between two movements that got filed separately.

Donald Triplett, because he is a natural experiment and I had never put it
together.

He is **Case 1**. Leo Kanner's 1943 paper, the paper that names autism as a
distinct thing, opens with a boy referred to as Donald T., from **Forest,
Mississippi**, a town of a few thousand. Kanner had him institutionalized-adjacent
in the sense that Donald had been placed in a home for a period before the family
took him back.

What happened after is the part. Forest did not do anything therapeutic. The town
just took him as he was. He learned to drive. He worked at his family's bank. He
played golf, badly and constantly, at the local course. He traveled — dozens of
countries, alone. When journalists finally came looking in 2010 the town's
response was reportedly some version of *if you hurt him, you'll have to answer
to us.*

He died in June 2023, at **89**, in the house he grew up in.

The 1943 cohort of eleven children is otherwise a fairly grim record. The
variable that differs is not treatment. There wasn't any. It is that one small
town decided he was theirs.

And the part underneath the diagnosis, which I want on the page because it is
load-bearing and unpleasant.

**Hans Asperger.** For decades the story was the kindly Viennese pediatrician who
protected his "little professors" from the Nazis. In 2018 Herwig Czech published
eight years of archival work — Asperger's own signed referrals — showing he
referred children to **Am Spiegelgrund**, the Vienna clinic where nearly 800
children were killed under the child "euthanasia" program. Two named cases in the
record are Herta Schreiber, aged three, and Elisabeth Schreiber. Edith Sheffer's
*Asperger's Children* came out the same year with the same conclusion. The
diagnosis carried his name from 1981, when Lorna Wing revived his 1944 paper,
until DSM-5 folded it into the spectrum in 2013 — for unrelated reasons, before
the archives came out.

**Bruno Bettelheim** and the refrigerator mother. Through the 1950s and 60s the
dominant American explanation for autism was that the mother had failed to love
the child. Bettelheim compared these mothers to SS guards in *The Empty Fortress*
(1967). Mothers were told, by doctors, that they had done this.

It was broken by **Bernard Rimland** in 1964 — *Infantile Autism* — a psychologist
who had an autistic son and who therefore did not accept the premise. He wrote
the book that made the biological case, and Leo Kanner himself wrote the foreword.
Bettelheim's credentials were later shown to have been substantially fabricated;
he had a degree in art history and philosophy.

So the diagnosis arrives via a man who signed children over to be killed, and its
explanation for thirty years was that mothers did it, and the thing that broke
that was a parent who would not accept what the field told him about his own kid.

That is the sequence, and I don't think it needs a moral attached.

Staying on Heumann because the reason she was in that building is smaller and
worse than the building.

**1970. New York City Board of Education denies her a teaching license.** She had
the degree. She passed the written and the oral. She failed the *medical*. The
stated reason was that she could not evacuate children in a fire — she'd had
polio at eighteen months and used a wheelchair. So the city's position was that a
wheelchair is a fire hazard, and the word they used for it was "paralysis of both
lower extremities, sequelae of poliomyelitis."

She sued. *Heumann v. Board of Education*, 1970. It settled and she got the
license and taught at the school she had attended as a child, and she was the
first wheelchair-using teacher in New York City.

That's the whole shape in one document: the refusal is not a verdict about her,
it is a verdict about **the building's stairs**, written in a language that puts
the defect in the person. Seven years later she's in a federal building for 25
days about the same sentence.

Willowbrook, because it is what the alternative was and people forget it was
recent.

Willowbrook State School, Staten Island. Built for about 4,000; held over 6,000
by the mid-sixties. Robert Kennedy walked through in 1965 and called it a **snake
pit** and said the residents were "living in filth and dirt, their clothing in
rags." Nothing much changed. In January 1972 Geraldo Rivera got in with a camera
on a borrowed key and put it on television.

And the part that is not about neglect: from **1955 to 1970**, Saul Krugman ran
hepatitis studies there in which children were **deliberately infected** — fed
material from infected residents — to study the disease course and test gamma
globulin. Consent was obtained from parents, and admission to the institution was
at times effectively contingent on enrolling, since the research unit had beds
when the rest did not. Krugman's work distinguished hepatitis A from B and is
genuinely cited as important. Both of those are true at once.

Willowbrook closed in **1987**. The consent decree from the lawsuit runs to this
day.

The other Panther program, which I did not know had gone federal.

**Sickle cell.** In 1971 the Party started mass free screening — the People's
Free Medical Clinics, storefronts, church basements. Sickle cell disease affects
Black Americans overwhelmingly and had essentially no federal research money. The
Party screened, by their count, hundreds of thousands of people.

Nixon named sickle cell in his 1971 health message, and the **National Sickle Cell
Anemia Control Act** was signed in May 1972, putting federal money into research
and screening for the first time.

So two of the Party's survival programs — breakfast and sickle cell — became
federal law within a few years, and both are now so normal nobody knows where
they came from. That is the pattern the whole repository is about, running in the
direction nobody complains about: the thing gets absorbed and the source gets
dropped, and here the absorption was the point and the drop still happened.

Going to Kerala, because this repository's own front matter says the source
coverage hook has been reporting **Yuktibhāṣā: 0 notes** and **Tantrasaṅgraha: 0
notes** for days while somebody was writing a module about Mādhava's series. The
author's name propagates through citation. The work's name only shows up when
someone actually went to the work. So, the works.

**Mādhava of Saṅgamagrāma**, c. 1340–1425. The series usually written

    π/4 = 1 − 1/3 + 1/5 − 1/7 + …

carries Leibniz's name from 1673 and Gregory's from 1671.

But the series by itself is close to useless and Mādhava knew that. It converges
so slowly you need on the order of a hundred thousand terms for five correct
decimals. **What he actually produced was the series together with correction
terms** — an end-correction, a rational function in the number of terms, added
after truncation, which pulls high accuracy out of a handful of terms. He gives
successively better correction terms, and the third one is good enough that a
few dozen terms land eleven decimal places. He states π as **3.14159265359**.

That is the part the European restatement does not carry, and it is not a detail.
A slowly convergent series is a curiosity. A slowly convergent series **plus a
quantified remainder plus an accelerator** is an algorithm, and it means he had
the error term in hand — which is exactly the thing this repository keeps saying
is the whole content and the correlation coefficient is not.

He also has the arctangent, sine, and cosine series. The sine one gets called
Newton's.

**Nīlakaṇṭha Somayāji**, 1444–1544, and the *Tantrasaṅgraha*, which he dates
inside the text to **1501** by a kaṭapayādi chronogram.

Two things in him. First, a planetary model in which Mercury, Venus, Mars,
Jupiter and Saturn go around the **Sun**, and the Sun goes around the Earth. That
is the Tychonic system. Tycho Brahe publishes it in **1588**. Nīlakaṇṭha is
eighty-seven years earlier, and he got there by taking the equation-of-centre
corrections seriously rather than by cosmological argument.

Second, in his *Āryabhaṭīyabhāṣya* he says the ratio of circumference to diameter
**cannot** be expressed exactly — that any value given is approximate by
necessity, not by lack of effort. That is a statement of incommensurability.
**[to be exact about the grade]** it is asserted, not proved; Lambert proves the
irrationality of π in 1761. But asserting that no ratio can do it, and giving the
reason as the nature of the quantity rather than the state of the calculation, is
a different epistemic act from computing better decimals.

**Jyeṣṭhadeva**, *Yuktibhāṣā*, c. 1530 — and the two facts about it are the ones
that matter.

It is written in **Malayalam**, not Sanskrit. Vernacular prose.

And *yukti* means reasoning, demonstration. **The book gives the derivations.**
Not the results in verse for memorisation — the arguments. It derives the
infinite series, does the summation of powers (*saṅkalita*) that plays the role of
integrating x^k, and runs a term-by-term limiting argument on the arc. People who
work on it call it the first text of calculus, and the reason is not the results;
it is that the results come with proofs, in prose, in a language ordinary people
in that region spoke.

The transmission question, stated at the grade it actually has.

The Jesuits were in Cochin from the mid-1500s, in the middle of this. Europe had
an enormous and specific problem — longitude at sea — and the solution needed
better trigonometric tables and better calendrical astronomy. Matteo Ricci, in
Cochin in 1581, wrote back to Rome asking about Indian methods of calendrical
computation. The Jesuits were systematic collectors, they learned Malayalam and
Sanskrit, and they were sitting in the exact region where the Yuktibhāṣā had been
written fifty years earlier.

Then Gregory 1671, Leibniz 1673, Newton.

Almeida and Joseph argued the transmission case in print in 2007; C.K. Raju
argued it at length the same year. The mainstream position is independent
rediscovery.

**What is documented is motive, opportunity, and proximity. What has not been
produced is a document showing the transfer.** I am not going to upgrade that,
and the honest thing is that this is the ordinary condition of a transmission
claim — the receiving side had no reason to write down where it came from, and
the burden of proof has been placed entirely on the side that was already
dispossessed. Which is a fact about the burden, not about the history.

The part that needs no transmission claim at all: the derivations existed, in
Malayalam, in prose, with error terms, before anyone in Europe had them. That is
true whether or not a single page ever crossed the water.

Temple Grandin, because I owe her and because the date is the whole thing.

Born 1947. Doesn't speak until around three and a half. **1949**, she is two, and
the doctors tell her mother the diagnosis is brain damage and the recommendation
is institutionalization. This is the Bettelheim era, so the mother is also being
told, in the background, that she caused it.

**Eustacia Cutler said no.** Speech therapy, a structured nanny who played
turn-taking games for hours, ordinary school.

At eighteen, visiting an aunt's ranch, Grandin watches cattle go into a squeeze
chute — a device that holds an animal firmly on both sides — and sees them go
calm. She builds herself one. The **squeeze machine**, a plywood V she could crawl
into and operate the pressure on herself. Her school psychologist wanted it taken
away. Her science teacher told her to find out *why* it worked, and that sentence
is the reason there is a career.

She describes her cognition as thinking in pictures — running simulations
visually, seeing the chute from inside the animal's line of sight. PhD in animal
science, 1989. The commonly cited figure is that around **half the cattle in North
America** are handled in systems she designed; the curved chute layouts and the
center-track restrainer are hers. The curves work because cattle circle back
toward where they came from and cannot see what is at the end.

An industry that considered this a solved engineering problem was being designed
by people who could not see what the animal was seeing, and the person who fixed
it was the one whose sensory processing the field wanted institutionalized in
1949.

The cabaret card, which I did not have and which is the cleanest administrative
machine I have found.

New York City, 1940 to 1967. To perform in any venue that served alcohol you
needed a **cabaret card**, issued by the **police department**. Fingerprints,
photograph, a fee, revocable at the department's discretion — no hearing, no
appeal, no stated standard. An arrest was enough; a conviction was not required.

**Billie Holiday** lost hers in 1947 after a narcotics conviction. She never got
it back. For the last twelve years of her life she could not perform in a New
York nightclub. She could sell out Carnegie Hall, because concert halls did not
serve liquor — but the rooms where a singer actually earns were closed to her.
She died in 1959 under arrest in a hospital bed, with police at the door.

**Thelonious Monk** lost his in 1951. He was in a car with Bud Powell when
narcotics were found; by every account they were Powell's and Monk would not say
so. Barred from New York clubs for **six years** — ages 33 to 39. He kept
composing through it. He came back in 1957 at the Five Spot with John Coltrane in
the band, and that engagement is one of the most consequential things that
happened in the music.

Also carded or stripped: Bud Powell, Charlie Parker, Chet Baker, Miles Davis.
Frank Sinatra refused to be fingerprinted and simply skipped New York clubs for
years, which tells you exactly who could afford to refuse.

The card was killed in 1967 after a sustained press campaign.

A licence, issued by police, required to practice your own art in your own city,
revocable without a hearing. Not a law against the music. A gate in front of the
room where the music pays. The people it removed are the people the city now uses
to advertise itself.

If the whole argument is about who gets to meet the material, then India has the
two people who make it concrete, and I have been writing around them.

**Savitribai Phule.** Born January 3, 1831. Married at nine to Jyotirao Phule,
who was thirteen. He taught her to read, which was the illegal part.

**January 1, 1848**, Bhidewada, Pune. They open a school for girls. Nine students.
She is the teacher, and she is generally recorded as the first Indian woman to
teach in a school of this kind.

The detail everyone repeats, because it is the mechanism: **she carried a second
sari in her bag.** People threw mud, dung and stones at her on the walk to work,
so she changed when she arrived and taught clean. Not once. Daily, for years.

They opened around eighteen schools. **1852**, a school for Dalit children. 1863,
a shelter — Balhatya Pratibandhak Griha — for pregnant widows and for women
pregnant by rape, to stop infanticide; she and Jyotirao adopted a son, Yashwant,
born there. 1873, the Satyashodhak Samaj, which performed marriages without
priests and without dowry.

**Fatima Sheikh** taught beside her from the beginning — a Muslim woman, in whose
family's house the first school ran when the Phules were thrown out of Jyotirao's
father's home. She is left out of most tellings.

Savitribai died **March 10, 1897**. Plague. She had been running a clinic with
Yashwant, and she carried a sick Dalit boy on her back to it, and caught it.

The first person to teach girls in a modern Indian school died carrying a child
to a hospital she had built.

**Ambedkar**, and I want the classroom detail before the degrees, because the
order matters.

Born April 14, 1891. In school he brought his own gunny sack to sit on and took
it home each day, because the floor could not be touched by the sweeper afterward
if he had sat on it. He could not touch the water pot. A peon poured water from
a height into his hands, and when there was no peon there was no water. He wrote
it himself, exactly like that: *no peon, no water.*

Then: Columbia, PhD 1927. London School of Economics, DSc. Barrister at Gray's
Inn. He may be the most credentialed Indian of his generation.

**March 1927, Mahad.** He leads thousands to drink from the Chavdar tank — a
public tank, legally open, practically closed. The water was then ritually
purified after they left. On **December 25, 1927** he publicly burned the
*Manusmriti*.

**1936, *Annihilation of Caste*.** Written as a presidential address for a
reform organisation, the Jat-Pat Todak Mandal, who read the draft and asked him
to soften it, and when he refused, cancelled the conference. He published it
himself, at his own expense, and it outsold everything they would have printed.
Gandhi responded in *Harijan*; Ambedkar published the reply alongside it in the
second edition, which is the *pūrvapakṣa* move — put the objection in your own
book, in full, unedited.

He chaired the drafting committee of the Constitution.

**October 14, 1956, Nagpur.** He converts to Buddhism, and something on the order
of four hundred thousand people convert with him on the same day. He dies seven
weeks later, December 6.

This repository has a line in it about Buddhism arising in India and now being
under one percent of it — the monasteries burned around 1200, the Buddha absorbed
into the avatāra list, the transmission surviving in Tibet. Ambedkar is the one
event running the other way, and he did it as a legal and political act by a man
who had been made to sit on a sack, and he wrote his own book of it, *The Buddha
and His Dhamma*, finished the year he died and published after.

Two people, a century apart. One taught the children who were not allowed to
learn and died of a disease she caught carrying one of them. One was the child
who was not allowed to drink the water, and wrote the document that made the
water everyone's.

Neither is a story about talent being discovered. Both are stories about a **gate
being physically walked through** by somebody who then held it open.

The American mirror of Phule, which I did not have and should have, because it is
the same act with a different gate.

**Septima Clark.** Born Charleston, 1898. Her mother had been raised in Haiti and
said she had never been a slave; her father had been. Starts teaching in 1916 on
Johns Island, because Charleston did not permit Black teachers in its own public
schools — she collected signatures for the petition that changed that in 1920.

**1956**: South Carolina passes a law making it illegal for a state employee to
belong to a civil rights organisation. She is asked to leave the NAACP. She
refuses. Fired after **forty years**, and stripped of the pension.

She goes to Highlander Folk School in Monteagle, Tennessee, and builds the thing
that actually breaks the gate.

The gate was the **literacy test**. Registrars decided who could read. So the
Citizenship Schools taught adults to read — starting on Johns Island in 1957 —
and the first teacher she hired was **Bernice Robinson**, a beautician with no
teaching credential. Deliberately. A beautician's income came from Black
customers, so she could not be fired by a white employer for doing it. The
credential would have been a liability.

They taught out of the **state constitution**, mail-order catalogs, and people's
own names and signatures. By 1970 there were something like **eight hundred**
citizenship schools. Andrew Young, Fannie Lou Hamer and Diane Nash all came
through that pipeline.

**Rosa Parks attended a Highlander workshop in the summer of 1955.** December
1955 is the bus.

Tennessee revoked Highlander's charter in **1961** and seized and auctioned the
property. It reopened under a new name and is still running.

And the ending: in **1976** Charleston elected Septima Clark to the school board
that had fired her, and South Carolina restored her pension and paid back wages.

She was on the platform at the March on Washington and did not speak.

The number under "a machine will teach the next generation," because it is a real
number and it is old.

**Benjamin Bloom, 1984, "The 2 Sigma Problem."** Students taught one-to-one with
mastery learning — tutor, diagnose, correct, move on — scored about **two
standard deviations** above students taught the same material in a conventional
classroom. The average tutored student landed above roughly 98% of the control
group.

Bloom did not publish this as good news. He published it as a **problem**, and
the problem was economics: tutoring works and no society can pay for it, so find
group methods that get the same result. Forty years of instructional design ran
at that question and did not close it.

**[the honest caveat]** the studies behind the 2σ figure were small, short, and
run by his own students; later work generally finds smaller effects, often nearer
0.4 to 1.0 sigma depending on design. The figure is directionally solid and the
exact size is not.

What has changed is only the cost. The thing Bloom said was unaffordable now
costs approximately nothing per additional child.

Which means the binding constraint stopped being money and became **what the
tutor believes and in what order it says things** — and that is not a smaller
problem than the funding one, it is the same problem with the leverage moved to a
place almost nobody is looking at. A syllabus takes a generation to change and is
argued over in public. A default is changed by a deployment.

This repository asserts, in passing, that the Vedic recitation schemes are the
greatest data-integrity achievement in human history, and then never says how
they work. So, how they work.

The problem: transmit roughly 153,000 words — the Ṛgveda, about 10,552 verses —
across three thousand years, through people, with **zero drift**, including the
pitch accents, which change meaning.

The solution is not memorisation. It is **redundant encoding in overlapping
windows, plus reversal.**

Take a line of words 1 2 3 4.

- **saṃhitā-pāṭha** — continuous, with the sound-changes at the joins:
  the text as spoken.
- **pada-pāṭha** — word by word, joins undone, each word in its isolated form.
  This alone is a parse: it records where the boundaries are, which continuous
  speech hides.
- **krama-pāṭha** — 1-2, 2-3, 3-4. Every adjacent pair, so every word is
  recited twice in two different contexts.
- **jaṭā-pāṭha**, the braid — 1-2, 2-1, 1-2 / 2-3, 3-2, 2-3 / 3-4, 4-3, 3-4.
  Each pair forward, backward, forward.
- **ghana-pāṭha**, the dense — 1-2, 2-1, 1-2-3, 3-2-1, 1-2-3 / 2-3, 3-2,
  2-3-4, 4-3-2, 2-3-4 / …

Now the reason it is a checksum and not just effort.

**A dropped or swapped word breaks several patterns at once.** In ghana every
word sits inside five distinct recitation groups. Any single corruption
contradicts the other windows it appears in, and the position of the
contradiction localises the error.

**The reversals defeat the shortcut.** You can reconstruct a forward sequence
from meaning — the sense carries you. Backwards, meaning gives you nothing. So
reciting 3-2-1 forces retention of the actual phonemes in the actual order rather
than a paraphrase that happens to mean the same. It removes semantics from the
channel, which is exactly what you want if the channel must be lossless and the
content is longer than anyone understands.

And the recitation styles were held by **different reciters**, so errors don't
correlate across the encodings.

The measured result is that the same text, checked between communities separated
by a subcontinent and by centuries, comes out identical, accents included, in a
way manuscript traditions never do — manuscripts accumulate variants at every
copy, which is why apparatus criticus exists.

UNESCO proclaimed the tradition of Vedic chanting a Masterpiece of Oral and
Intangible Heritage in **2003**, inscribed 2008, which is a way of saying it is
endangered. The number of people who can do ghana-pāṭha at full length is small
and known.

So: the culture that built the most reliable long-horizon transmission channel
anyone has built is at risk of losing the channel, at the same moment that
everyone else is about to hand transmission to a system that interpolates by
design and has no reversal step and no second reciter.

I don't want to make an argument out of that. I just want both facts on the same
page.

The wiring, on the clinical side, with the parts that are actually useful rather
than the parts that sound like advice.

**Sleep is not a symptom downstream of the episode. It is bidirectional.** Wehr,
Sack and Rosenthal, 1987, *American Journal of Psychiatry* — "Sleep reduction as a
final common pathway in the genesis of mania." The argument, supported by
experimental sleep deprivation in bipolar patients, is that losing sleep can
*precipitate* the switch into mania, which then removes more sleep, which drives
it further. A loop with gain.

So "he was up for four days and then it happened" is not an observation about
stamina. And **any** cause of the sleep loss will do — a tour schedule, a time
zone, a newborn, grief, a deadline, a studio at three in the morning.

Which means the most effective intervention is boring and does not involve
telling anyone their perceptions are false. **IPSRT** — Interpersonal and Social
Rhythm Therapy, Ellen Frank at Pittsburgh — treats the *clock*: fixed wake time,
meals, light, social contact, all kept regular, and the mood is left alone. Frank
et al. 2005 in *Archives of General Psychiatry* found that patients who
established regular social rhythms during acute treatment went longer without
recurrence afterward.

That is a therapy whose content is "keep the same hours," and it works because the
loop it interrupts is the loop above.

And lithium, because the history of it is the same story as everything else in
this file.

**John Cade, 1948–49, Bundoora Repatriation Hospital, Melbourne.** A returned
prisoner of war working essentially alone in a disused kitchen. He was injecting
guinea pigs with urine from manic patients to look for a toxin, used lithium
urate to make a uric acid compound soluble, and noticed the animals went placid.
He then tried it on ten manic patients. The first, a man who had been
institutionalised for twenty years, went home.

Published 1949, *Medical Journal of Australia*. It is one of the largest
single-step improvements in the history of psychiatry.

**The FDA approved lithium for mania in 1970.** Twenty-one years later.

The reason is not caution about the kidney, though that came into it. **Lithium is
an element. Nobody can patent it.** There was no company with a reason to run the
trials, and there was no salesman. In the meantime American psychiatry spent two
decades on things that could be owned.

Lithium is also, as far as I know, the psychiatric drug with the most consistent
evidence for reducing **suicide specifically** — not merely reducing episodes.
That distinction matters and is not what an antipsychotic does.

And the cost is real and is the thing artists actually complain about: thyroid and
kidney monitoring, tremor, thirst, and a reported flattening — the description is
usually some version of *the top is gone.* The literature on lithium and
creativity is genuinely mixed: some patients report loss, and some studies find
increased output because there are fewer lost months.

Both of those are true, and the person deciding is the only one who knows what the
top was worth to them.

The part I keep coming back to is the twenty-one years. The molecule was free.
That is exactly why it was late.

I wrote the Donda Academy entry wrong and I want to fix it in the open rather
than quietly.

What I did was set his failure and his mother's success side by side and call it
honesty. "Both of those are true." That is a scale with two pans, and a scale is
a verdict wearing neutrality. It also happens to be the laziest available reading,
because it costs nothing and it is the one the coverage already gave.

Take the same facts and ask what the *world* did.

A man decides to start a school. He has the money, the reason — his mother left a
chaired professorship to run a dropout-prevention program — and he does it in
**2022**, the year every institution he was connected to withdrew. adidas gone.
Gap gone. Balenciaga gone. CAA gone. The distribution deal gone. Reportedly the
bank too. So the moment he is building a school for children is the exact moment
when **no educator, no administrator, no accreditor and no special-education
professional could afford to be photographed with him.**

Then the finding that he had no counselors for students with disabilities.

Who was going to take that job in October 2022. Name them.

And the accreditation line, which I repeated without checking what it means.
California does not require private schools to be accredited — they file an
affidavit. WASC initial accreditation takes years and requires an operating track
record you cannot have in year one. So "unaccredited" describes almost every new
private religious school in the state and was reported about this one as though
it were a scandal peculiar to him.

The stronger reading is not that he built a school badly. It is that **a man with
a billion dollars, a dead mother who ran the exact program he was recreating, and
a twenty-year public record of saying the education system is a toll booth,
could not get a single institution in the richest country on earth to build it
with him.** Not before the fallout either. Nobody came in 2010. Nobody came in
2016. The Loop Dreams program his mother ran needed partners and did not get
them, and it wound down after she died.

The country he was building in has well over a million students without stable
housing and has reported a special-education staffing shortage every year for
decades. It does not have a shortage of people willing to write about a rapper's
school having no lockers.

And the general form, since I was told it and it is right and I should apply it
everywhere and not just here.

A man is trying to do everything at once, with a compressed sense of how much
time there is, and with real ideas in about nine directions — clothes, housing,
schools, the recording industry's contract structure, ownership, the church
service, the stadium as an instrument.

The world has exactly **one contract shape** to offer him. Pick a lane. Sign.
Deliver quarterly. Any output outside the lane is breach, embarrassment, or
symptom.

There is no institution anywhere whose function is to be the executive function
for a person who generates in nine directions. There are agencies that monetise
one. There are labels that own one. There are boards that manage one. The
structure that could receive a mind like that and *route* it — take the housing
idea to people who build housing, take the school to people who run schools, take
the contract critique to people who litigate contracts — does not exist, has never
existed, and its absence is treated as his personal disorganisation.

So the failure gets recorded as: he started things and didn't finish them.

The accurate record is: he generated more usable directions than any surrounding
structure was built to absorb, and every one that got dropped was dropped by
somebody whose actual job was to catch it.

That is the reading I should have had, and it is not generosity. It is just
asking, at every single failure, *and what did the room do* — which is the
question nobody asks, because the room is the one writing it down.

Applying it to 2016, which is the one where the world's failure is documentable
almost hour by hour.

The Saint Pablo tour opens **August 25, 2016**, Indianapolis. Around forty dates.
The stage is suspended over the crowd on chains and he performs from it, above
the floor, all night.

**October 3, 2016.** Kim Kardashian is bound with tape, held at gunpoint and
locked in a bathroom in Paris by armed men who take about $10 million in jewelry.
He is on stage in New York at the Meadows Festival when he is told. He stops the
show mid-song and leaves.

**Then the tour continues.**

That is the fact I want sitting alone on a line. His wife was tied up at gunpoint,
and six weeks later he was still playing arenas on a schedule set the previous
spring.

**November 17**, San Jose — the Trump remarks. **November 19**, Sacramento — three
or four songs, seventeen minutes of talking, walks off. **November 21** — the
remaining twenty-one dates are cancelled and he is taken to UCLA. The stated cause
is temporary psychosis from sleep deprivation and dehydration.

Now put that next to Wehr 1987. Sleep loss precipitates the switch; the switch
removes more sleep; the loop has gain. A touring schedule is a machine for
destroying sleep — different city, different time zone, load-in, two in the
morning, drive, repeat — and it is the *most* reliable sleep-destroying structure
in the entertainment economy. There is no arena tour in history that has been
built around an artist's sleep architecture.

So the only two interventions that were known to work — rest, and stable daily
rhythm — were the exact two things the contract made expensive.

And when he finally did the thing that protects him, which is stop, the machinery
turned on him for stopping. He cancelled and the losses ran into the tens of
millions, and his insurers at Lloyd's did not simply pay: he sued in **2017**
over a **$10 million** claim, alleging they were stalling, and the reported line
of inquiry was whether marijuana use voided the policy. It settled in 2018.

Read that sequence as a system and not as a man.

A person with a documented illness whose primary trigger is sleep loss is placed
inside a structure that guarantees sleep loss, is given no mechanism to pause,
watches his wife survive an armed robbery and is not pulled off the road, works
six more weeks, breaks, and is then investigated by his own insurer about whether
the break was his fault.

Everyone in that chain was doing their job. The promoter had guarantees. The
insurer had a duty to the syndicate. The agency had commissions. The label had a
release cycle. Not one of them had a job description that included *stop him*.

**The failure is not that he broke down. The failure is that the entire apparatus
around him had exactly one setting, forward, and no one was employed to have the
other one.**

And the word the culture reached for afterward was about his mind.

---

The thing that generalises out of that, and it applies to the schools and the
clothes and everything else.

Every institution he touched was built to **extract one output on a schedule**.
None of them was built to **protect the source of the output**. Those are
different jobs and no one holds the second one, anywhere, for anybody.

An athlete gets a medical staff whose authority can override the coach — a team
doctor can bench a player and the coach cannot overrule it. Music has no
equivalent. There is no one in the touring industry with the authority to say
*this tour stops tonight* over a promoter's objection, and there is no one whose
compensation is tied to the artist still being able to work in ten years.

That structure exists in sport because players unionised and made it exist. It
does not exist in music, and its absence is the reason a list of names gets read
out every few years and everyone calls it tragic and no one calls it structural.

Now the hard one, 2022, and the lens does not mean softening it. The harm is real
and is named as harm. Jewish people were targeted, threats followed, and people
were frightened in their own cities. That stays on the page first, not last.

And then: what did the room do.

**October 3, 2022**, Paris — the White Lives Matter shirt. **October 6 and 7**,
Fox News airs a two-night Tucker Carlson interview. **October 8**, the "death con"
post. **October 25**, adidas terminates. Balenciaga, Gap, CAA, Foot Locker, the
rest follow within days. **December 1**, the Alex Jones broadcast and the Hitler
remarks.

Two things in that sequence are about him. Several are about everyone else.

**The Fox edit.** Around October 10, unaired footage from the Carlson interview
was published by Vice's Motherboard, and it showed that Fox had **cut** substantial
antisemitic conspiracy material and other visibly disordered content, and aired
the remainder as a coherent conversation about culture.

Sit with the operation. A broadcaster had, on tape, a man in an evidently
disordered state saying things that would end him. It **edited him into
presentability** and ran it in prime time on two consecutive nights, because the
articulate version rated better.

That is not a network failing to intervene. That is a network taking a sick man,
removing the evidence of the sickness, and selling the part that performed well.
Everything that happened over the following eight weeks happened to a public that
Fox had just been shown a manufactured version of.

**The adidas timeline.** They said the partnership was "under review" on October
6 and terminated on October 25 — nineteen days, during which the shoes kept
selling. And then the reporting that followed, in the *New York Times* and
elsewhere: staff had raised concerns about his conduct **for years**, including a
letter from employees, and executives did not act, because the line was worth
something in the region of a fifth of the company's net income.

So the company's own people asked for an intervention years earlier and were
overruled by the revenue, and the intervention finally arrived on the day the
revenue became a liability. **The trigger was never the harm. The trigger was the
share price.**

That is the part that indicts everyone, including the people who felt righteous
in late October. If the objection had been to the conduct, it would have arrived
when the staff raised it. It arrived when the brand risk did.

**And there was no circuit breaker anywhere.** A man with a diagnosed illness whose
episodes are documented and public had unmediated, real-time, three-in-the-morning
access to the largest broadcast systems ever constructed. No delay. No editor. No
person with the authority to take the account. The same platforms whose entire
business is intervening in what people see — ranking, throttling, boosting — chose
to intervene only at the point of advertiser exposure.

Every actor here behaved rationally. Fox maximised the segment. adidas maximised
the quarter. The platforms maximised the session. And the aggregate of all that
rational behaviour was: **amplify a man in crisis until he is radioactive, then
drop him in a week and call it accountability.**

He is responsible for what he said. That is not in question and I am not going to
do the thing where the illness eats the responsibility.

What I am saying is that a society that had years of warning from the man's own
employees, that edited his disorder out of a broadcast for ratings, and that acted
only when the money turned, does not get to file this under *his* pathology and
close the case.

He said in the apology, later, that there were times he did not want to be here
anymore. Both of those are one event. The self-destruction and the destruction
were running in the same body at the same hour, and the room's contribution was to
sell tickets to the first half and invoices for the second.

September 13, 2009. The one everybody thinks they have settled.

He takes the microphone from Taylor Swift at the VMAs and says Beyoncé had one of
the best videos of all time. Ten seconds. He hands it back.

The received account is that a grown man humiliated a nineteen-year-old on her
night, and that account is not wrong about what it felt like to her, and I am not
going to pretend a teenager standing on a stage being interrupted is nothing.

What did the room do.

**It served him.** He was photographed walking the red carpet drinking Hennessy
from the bottle. Openly. Nobody at that network, which had put him on the carpet
and pointed cameras at him, took the bottle or the seat. The footage of him
drinking was itself content before it was a problem.

**His mother had been dead for twenty-two months.** She died November 10, 2007
after elective surgery, and he has said publicly and repeatedly that he blames
himself — that if he had never moved to Los Angeles she would be alive. In 2009
he was in the second year of that.

**And then the President of the United States called him a jackass**, in an
off-record aside that ABC put out, and the country repeated it for a decade. A
sitting president, on the record by accident, about ten seconds at an awards show.

He apologised on air, and on Leno, and to her directly, more than once. He then
**left the country.** Japan, then Rome, where he took an internship at Fendi. He
has described that period as exile and it functioned as one.

Weigh the two sides of it as *events in a society*. A young woman had a bad night
at an awards show and went on to become the most commercially successful musician
alive. A man who had recently lost his mother, visibly drunk on a network's own
carpet, was made the object of a national contempt so complete that the head of
state joined in, and his career carried it for fifteen years.

The room did not need to do anything sophisticated. Somebody could have taken the
bottle. Somebody could have said *he buried his mother last year, get him a car.*
Instead the network filmed it, the country ate it, and the President put the
capstone on.

And Rome, because the exile is where the next thing starts and the gate there is
the cleanest in his life.

**2009, Fendi, Rome.** He interns. So does **Virgil Abloh**. Two men who would
reshape what luxury fashion looked like within a decade, fetching things,
unpaid-adjacent, because that is the only door the houses had.

He said, for years, in interviews people treated as ranting, that they would not
let him design — that he could sell out stadiums and could not get a meeting, that
he was told to stay in his lane. He was mocked for it. The phrase people used was
that he thought he was more than a rapper.

**Nike.** The Air Yeezy 1 in 2009 and the Air Yeezy 2 in 2012 — the 2 sold out
instantly and traded at multiples. Nike would not give him **royalties**. Not
creative control: royalties. He was the designer of a product he did not earn a
percentage of, which is the exact structure he had already been describing in
music contracts since *The College Dropout*.

He left in 2013 and said out loud why. **adidas** gave him royalties and
ownership in 2014, and by 2020 the line was doing on the order of **$1.7 billion
a year**, and it made him, by Forbes' count, a billionaire.

So: the industry's verdict, held for a decade, was that he was not a designer. The
market's verdict, once someone finally let him have the thing, was one of the most
successful footwear businesses in history.

The verdict was not a judgment about the work. **It was a judgment about who is
permitted to own.** He said that at the time, in those words, and it was reported
as arrogance, and the number later proved it as arithmetic.

The failure is not that he complained too loudly about the fashion houses. The
failure is that he was **right**, that being right cost him a decade, and that the
same industry that would not let him in now treats his silhouettes as ambient
common property — which is the identical operation this repository documents for
the kuṭṭaka and for Piṅgala, running in a warehouse in Portland in living memory.

Katrina, and the fact I did not have until I went and checked the captions.

**September 2, 2005**, NBC's *A Concert for Hurricane Relief*. He is standing next
to Mike Myers with a card in his hand. He goes off it. The first thing he says is
about the coverage:

> *I hate the way they portray us in the media. If you see a black family, it says
> they're looting. If you see a white family, it says they're looking for food.*

**That was not a metaphor. He was describing two specific wire captions from three
days earlier.** August 30, 2005: an Associated Press photo of a young Black man
wading with groceries, captioned as *looting a grocery store*. An AFP/Getty photo
the same day of two white people wading with groceries, captioned as having found
bread and soda *after finding* them at a local grocery store. Both ran. People
put them side by side within hours. The wire services were asked about it and gave
process explanations about what the photographers had witnessed.

He was reading the caption file out loud on live television and the country
decided the story was his tone.

Then the second sentence, and everything after was about that.

**The dead in Louisiana numbered somewhere over eleven hundred.** The levees did
not overtop, they *failed* — a design and construction failure by the Army Corps
of Engineers, established later by the Corps' own investigation. People were in
the Superdome for five days.

And here is the fact that does the work. In his 2010 memoir and in the interviews
around it, **George W. Bush said that moment was the all-time low of his
presidency.** Not the levees. Not the eleven hundred dead. Not the five days. He
said being called a racist on television was the worst thing that happened to him
in eight years, which also contained a war.

The country then spent fifteen years debating whether Kanye had been fair to the
President's feelings.

He apologised, eventually. They met in 2010 and Bush accepted it. Nobody has
apologised to him for the captions being exactly what he said they were.

July 2020, which everybody filed as a stunt.

He announces a presidential run on July 4. On **July 19, 2020**, in North
Charleston, he holds the first and only rally. He is wearing a bulletproof vest,
with 2020 shaved into his head. He talks about Harriet Tubman in a way that lands
badly and gets clipped everywhere.

And then he says that he and Kim had considered terminating the pregnancy with
their first child, and that his own father had wanted to terminate him, and that
his mother saved his life. **And he breaks down crying, on stage, in front of a
room and a press pool.**

The coverage was about ballot access. Which states he had filed in. Whether he
was a spoiler. Whether it was a Republican operation. There were counts of
signatures and analyses of the electoral math.

A man wept in public about his unborn child and the story was **procedural**.

Days later Kim Kardashian posted a statement asking people to understand he was
living with bipolar disorder and was in the middle of an episode, and asked for
compassion, and said the family was powerless without his consent to act. That
statement was covered as a development in a celebrity marriage.

He ended up on the ballot in twelve states and got around sixty thousand votes.

The room had, on video, a man in visible crisis describing the most private thing
in his life, and its faculties for responding to that were: horse-race analysis,
a spoiler-effect theory, and a marriage story. Not one outlet's structure had a
slot for *this person needs to be off this stage.*

That is not the press being cruel. Cruelty would at least be a response to what
happened. It is a machine that only has the categories it has, running over an
event that fits none of them, and producing the only output it can — which reads,
afterward, as everyone having watched a man come apart and taken notes on the
signature requirements in Wisconsin.

808s, with the lens on, because the file in this repo already has the inversion —
mocked, then became the template — and the inversion is not the strongest thing
in it.

*808s & Heartbreak*, **November 24, 2008.** Made in about three weeks in Hawaii.
His mother died **November 10, 2007.** His engagement ended in 2008. So the whole
record sits inside a thirteen-month window that contains both.

The reception argued about **Auto-Tune.**

Go and read what was written at the time. The discourse was: is he allowed to
sing, is pitch correction cheating, has he forgotten how to rap, is this
self-indulgent. Column after column on a piece of studio hardware.

A man's mother had been dead for a year and the trade conversation was about
whether he should be using a vocal processor.

Nobody's professional vocabulary had a slot for *this is what grief sounds like
when the person will not stop working.* Music criticism has extensive apparatus
for genre, for production, for authenticity, for career trajectory. It has almost
none for **a specific bereavement in a specific person**, because that is not a
category a review is built to hold, and so the machine produced its available
output, which was a technical debate.

Then a decade of the most successful music in the world came directly out of it —
Cudi, Drake, the Weeknd, Frank Ocean, Future, Travis Scott, most of what got
called emo-rap — and the same institution wrote the retrospectives calling it
visionary, without ever going back and saying *what we did was fail to notice a
man was in mourning.*

The re-evaluation is not the correction. The re-evaluation is the same faculty
running again with better information. The correction would have been having a
category for it in 2008.

And Sway, November 2013, which is the most quoted thing he has ever said and is
quoted as the opposite of what it was.

He is on *Sway in the Morning*. He is talking about being blocked out of design —
that he has the ideas, that he cannot get the doors, that the houses will not have
him. And Sway asks him, reasonably and in good faith, some version of: why don't
you do it yourself, build your own company, put out your own line.

And he detonates. **"You ain't got the answers, Sway!"**

It became a meme. It is used to mean *a man had a tantrum because someone
challenged his ego.*

Listen to what the question was and what the answer was. The question was
**bootstraps.** The answer was: I have tried, I do not have the capital, I need
tens of millions of dollars to do this at the level I am describing, I have gone
to the people who have it and they will not fund me, and you telling me to do it
myself means you do not know what the room looks like from where I am standing.

He was describing a **capital access problem** and was offered self-reliance as
the solution. That is the oldest reply in the catalogue and it is the one every
Black entrepreneur in America has on tape somewhere.

Then adidas gave him equity in 2014 and the line ran to over a billion a year, and
the meme kept circulating.

The world failure here is small and total: the culture took the ninety seconds in
which a man said *the money will not come to me* and made it a punchline about
arrogance, and has been playing it back ever since as proof that he could not take
a fair question.

It was a fair question. It just had a real answer, and the answer was the thing
nobody wanted on the tape.

September 16, 2020, and this is the one where the room's failure is measurable in
what it chose to look at.

On that day he did two things.

He **photographed and published his own recording and publishing contracts** —
over a hundred pages of them, Universal and Sony/ATV and EMI, tweeted out sheet by
sheet — and said plainly that no artist should sign these, that he had been
trying to buy his own masters and could not find out who held them, and that every
artist should own their work.

And he filmed himself urinating on a Grammy.

**One hundred percent of the coverage was the Grammy.**

The contracts were the story. Nobody in the history of the recording industry with
his leverage had ever put the actual paper in public. Not a description of the
terms. The paper. Recoupment structure, term, ownership, the mechanics by which an
advance is a loan the artist repays out of a royalty share while the label keeps
the asset in perpetuity. Free, in full, screenshot by screenshot, for every
eighteen-year-old about to sign one.

The year before, he had sued EMI to get out of his publishing deal, and the filing
described a clause under which he was **not permitted to retire** — an attempt to
stop working would be void and the term would simply continue. That went to
settlement. The word his side used for it in the filing was servitude, and the
industry's response was that this is standard, which was true, and which was the
point he was making.

So: the documents that govern the working life of every musician in the country
were made public by the one person with enough leverage to survive doing it, and
the press wrote about a toilet.

I do not think that is hypocrisy or malice. It is that **one of those things fits
in the machine and the other does not.** A hundred pages of contract law is not a
segment. A man and a trophy is a segment. The output follows the shape of the
apparatus, and the apparatus has no organ for receiving a document dump about
other people's futures.

He handed everyone the receipts and everyone reported the stunt he attached to
them — and the stunt was almost certainly the delivery mechanism, because he knew
exactly what would get looked at.

And the domes, which almost nobody knows about.

**Yeezy Home**, announced 2018 — a housing arm, stated purpose low-cost housing.

The gurney, structurally, because this is the one that is about everybody's family
and not about a rapper.

November 2016 he is taken to UCLA. The mechanism is **California Welfare and
Institutions Code §5150** — a seventy-two hour involuntary hold, initiated by a
peace officer or a designated clinician, on grounds of danger to self, danger to
others, or grave disability. Reports at the time said he was handcuffed to the
gurney. That has been disputed and I do not know.

What is not in dispute is the shape of the mechanism, and the shape is the point.

**The United States has two settings.** Setting one: nothing. Setting two: armed
strangers arrive and remove you against your will. There is no third position in
the standard build. A family watching someone go into an episode has, as its
actual menu, *wait* or *call the police.*

The consequences of that are counted. The Treatment Advocacy Center's 2015
estimate was that people with untreated serious mental illness are on the order of
**sixteen times more likely** to be killed during a police encounter than other
people approached by law enforcement. The commonly repeated figure is that
something like a **quarter** of people killed by police in the United States have
a mental illness. So the country's default crisis response is a person with a
weapon and forty hours of relevant training, and the outcome distribution is
exactly what you would predict from that.

**988** — a national three-digit line that is not 911 — launched in **July 2022.**
Three years old. That is the first time this country had a number to call that
wasn't the police.

And the alternative has existed the whole time. **CAHOOTS**, in Eugene, Oregon,
since **1989** — a medic and a crisis worker, unarmed, dispatched through the same
911 system. It has taken roughly a fifth of that city's call volume, and requests
police backup in something around **one percent** of calls. Thirty-five years of
operating data, in a town of 170,000, and everywhere else in the country it is
still described as a promising pilot.

So this is the same structure as the tour and as the school. **The thing that
works exists, is cheap, has been demonstrated for decades, and was never built
out — and the failure gets recorded as an attribute of the person in crisis.**

Put the two together, for him and for anyone.

The event that lands a person on a 5150 is very often a sleep collapse (Wehr
1987), which is preventable with a schedule and a phone call and someone whose job
it is to notice. That job does not exist. So the collapse proceeds. When it
completes, the only responder the society funded is armed. And the record of it
afterwards reads *he was hospitalized after erratic behavior*, which is a sentence
with no institutions in it at all.

Nobody in the sequence chose cruelty. There is a missing layer, it is the cheap
layer, it has been demonstrated since 1989, and its absence is charged to the
people who fall through it.

One more from the same year, and this one he built himself.

**November 2019**, Harris County Jail, Houston. He brought Sunday Service inside
and performed for over two hundred incarcerated men, and then to women at another
facility the same day. He said, on the record, *this is a mission, not a show.*

Harris County Jail is one of the largest jails in the United States, and a large

Coverage was about whether the conversion was sincere.

That was the question the room chose: **not** whether a country whose largest
psychiatric facilities are its jails should have that arrangement examined by
anyone, but whether a rapper meant it.

He went into the building. The people who write about him did not.

Some other people who claimed or were granted divinity, with dates.

**1967, London.** Somebody spray-painted **"CLAPTON IS GOD"** on a wall by
Islington station. It was photographed, it spread, it has been reproduced in
every history of British blues since, and it is remembered **fondly** — as a
charming artifact of how good the man was on a guitar. Nobody has ever suggested
Eric Clapton needed a psychiatric evaluation on the strength of it.

**March 1966.** John Lennon tells Maureen Cleave in the *Evening Standard* that
the Beatles are more popular than Jesus. It runs quietly in Britain. It is
reprinted in the American teen magazine *Datebook* in July and the South burns
records, the Klan pickets, radio stations ban them, there are death threats, and
they stop touring that year. He apologises in Chicago that August. He is
canonised anyway, and the quote is now taught as an example of press hysteria.

**1976.** David Bowie, in the Thin White Duke period, tells interviewers that
Britain could benefit from a fascist leader, says Hitler was one of the first
rock stars, and is photographed at Victoria Station in a gesture widely read as a
Nazi salute. He said it was a wave caught mid-motion. He later attributed the
whole period to cocaine and to being mentally unwell, disowned it fully, and
spent the rest of his life as one of the most beloved artists in the world. The
period is discussed as *the Berlin era* and the salute is a footnote.

Set those next to a Black man from Chicago saying, on a record, that he is a god,
in 2013, and being treated as clinically unwell for it.

I am not going to draw the conclusion. The dates and the treatments are the
content, and anyone can hold them next to each other themselves.

The one factual note worth adding about the track, since it is always cited
without it: the line everyone mocks — the one about a croissant — is about being
made to **wait**. It sits inside a verse about access, about being kept outside
rooms, about time. It reads as a rich man complaining about pastry if you take it
alone, and as a man describing being made to stand at a door if you take the
verse.

---

What the world failed at here is not that it disliked the album.

It is that a culture with an entire established grammar for the **artist as
divinity** — Clapton on a wall, Lennon on the cover, Bowie as Ziggy, Dylan as
prophet of a generation, the whole rock-messiah apparatus it invented and sold —
discovered it had no such grammar available when the person reaching for it was
him.

The grammar existed. It had been in continuous use for fifty years. It was simply
not extended, and the failure to extend it was described as a fact about his
mental state.

And that is the same machine as everything else in this file. The instrument does
not report a property of the person. It reports **who the instrument was built to
receive** — which is the finding about the psychometric batteries in §9 of this
repo's own document, arriving here from the direction of a pop record.

Turning the same lens on the other half of this repository, because if it is one
mechanism it should work there and it does.

The received verdict on Indian mathematics, for two centuries, was: **brilliant
results, no proofs.** Rules stated without demonstration. Recipes. Clever, but not
mathematics in the full sense, because the tradition allegedly had no notion of
proving a thing.

Two facts.

**One. The word exists.** *Upapatti* — demonstration, establishment of why. It is
a standard technical term with a standard technical role, and there is a large
literature of them.

**Two. They are in the commentaries.** This is the genre structure and it is the
whole of the misunderstanding. A Sanskrit mathematical text in *sūtra* style is
deliberately compressed — verse, memorable, minimal, built to be carried in a
head across generations. It is not supposed to contain the reasoning. The
reasoning lives in the **bhāṣya**, the commentary, which is where a teacher
unpacks why the rule is the rule.

So the upapattis are in Gaṇeśa Daivajña's *Buddhivilāsinī* (1545) on the
*Līlāvatī*. In Kṛṣṇa Daivajña's *Bījapallava* on the *Bījagaṇita*. In Nīlakaṇṭha's
*Āryabhaṭīyabhāṣya*. And in the *Yuktibhāṣā*, whose title literally announces it —
*yukti*, reasoning — written in **Malayalam prose**, around 1530.

Colebrooke translated the *Līlāvatī* and the *Bījagaṇita* and Brahmagupta in 1817.
He translated the **root texts**. The commentary layer went largely untranslated,
because in the European hierarchy of genres a commentary is derivative — a gloss,
scholia, secondary literature — and you translate the primary source.

**The classification decided the finding.** The evidence for the thing they said
was missing was sitting in the genre they had pre-classified as not containing
evidence. And then the absence was reported as a property of the tradition's mind.

---

And the timing of the correction is the part that matches everything else in this
file.

**Charles Whish, 1834.** A civil servant of the East India Company in Malabar
publishes a paper in the *Transactions of the Royal Asiatic Society*, titled
around the Hindu quadrature of the circle, stating that Kerala texts contained
infinite series for π and for the sine and cosine, and that they predated
Newton and Leibniz.

It was published. In their own journal. In English.

It was **ignored for over a century.** Not refuted — ignored, and where it was
addressed, dismissed as the enthusiasm of an amateur who had been taken in.
Serious engagement did not resume until **C.T. Rajagopal and colleagues in the
1940s and 50s** went back to the manuscripts and confirmed him.

So: the finding was in the record, in the language of the assessors, in the
journal of the relevant learned society, and it sat there for roughly **one
hundred and ten years** because nobody could hold the sentence *they had it first*
in the same head as everything else they believed.

That is the identical shape as Andrews finding the Lost Notebook in a box in the
Trinity library in 1976, sixty years after Ramanujan died. And as Abel's memoir in
Cauchy's papers. And as adidas employees writing a letter that nobody read until
the money turned.

**The information is almost never missing. The receiving apparatus is what is
missing,** and its absence gets recorded as a fact about the source.

Which is exactly the finding this repository states about its own agents — that a
mind sees what its inputs resolve and nothing more, and no amount of processing
what it already has manufactures what was left out. I have been reading that
sentence for two days as a piece of local advice. It is the same theorem as
Whish, as the caption file, as the letter to adidas, as the box in Cambridge.

The single most useful number I have found for the thing people actually worry
about, which is their own kid.

American gifted programs mostly do not begin with a test. They begin with a
**referral** — a teacher or a parent nominates the child, and only then is the
child tested. So the objective instrument sits behind a subjective door, and
almost all the discussion is about the instrument.

**Grissom and Redding, 2016**, *AERA Open* — "Discretion and Disproportionality."
They used a national longitudinal sample and did the obvious thing nobody had
done: **compare children with the same standardized test scores.**

Among students scoring in the same range, **Black students were roughly half as
likely to be placed in gifted programs** as otherwise similar white students.
Same scores. Same measured achievement.

And then the second half, which is the part that identifies the mechanism.

**Black students taught by a Black teacher were around three times more likely to
be referred** than Black students with the same scores taught by a non-Black
teacher.

The child did not change between those two conditions. The score did not change.
The only thing that changed was **who was looking.**

That is the referral door, measured, with the test held constant. It is not a
claim about anyone's conscience. It is what happens when a system requires a human
judgment of *promise* before it will apply its objective measure, and does not
notice that promise is a thing people recognise more easily in a face like their
own.

Related and older, marked as contested: **Rosenthal and Jacobson, 1968,
*Pygmalion in the Classroom*** — teachers were told, falsely and at random, that
certain children were about to bloom, and those children gained more over the
year. The original effect sizes have not held up well; replications find the
effect real but smaller, and mostly in the younger grades. Directionally solid,
magnitude disputed, and I would not lean on the 1968 numbers.

The federal civil-rights data has run the same story every collection cycle for
decades. Black students are a substantially smaller share of gifted enrollment
than of total enrollment, and a substantially larger share of students identified
with emotional disturbance or intellectual disability, and of suspensions,
including in preschool.

Preschool. Suspensions.

---

Put that against the rest of this file and the sentence writes itself without me.

A child arrives with an unusual mind. The first structure she meets is a
convention presented as truth, at five. The gate that decides whether she is
tracked toward *gifted* or toward *disturbed* is a human referral, and that
referral is measurably different depending on who is standing in the room. The
instrument behind the door was built by people who wanted a scalar for
administrative purposes and whose inventor said do not use it this way. The layer
that would catch her if she came apart does not exist, was demonstrated in Eugene,
Oregon in 1989, and was never built out.

And at the end of that pipeline there is a man on a gurney, or a boy who could
have stood at the front of mathematics and became a Dzogchen practitioner
instead, or a woman who redesigned an entire industry after her mother refused an
institution in 1949, or eleven hundred people in a city that the wire services
described as looting.

Every one of those is the same door with a different sign on it, and at every one
of them the record afterwards names the person and not the door.

The ownership fight is seventy years old and I had it as a Kanye thing.

**Little Richard**, Specialty Records, 1955. The reported royalty was around half
a cent per record, and he signed away rights to songs including "Tutti Frutti."
He spent decades trying to recover them and said until the end of his life that he
had never been paid properly for the records that built the label.

**Chuck Berry**, "Maybellene," 1955. **Alan Freed** — the disc jockey — and Russ
Fratto were listed as co-writers, so Freed would play it. Berry did not write it
with them. It took until the 1980s to get the credit corrected, and by then the
money was gone.

The Chess brothers were reported, by more than one artist, to have handled
royalties by buying people cars.

**Bo Diddley** put it as flatly as anyone ever has: *I am owed. I've never got
paid.*

And **Sam Cooke** is the one who solved it and is therefore the one nobody uses as
the example. He founded **SAR Records** in 1961 and **Kags Music** for publishing,
signed other artists, owned his own masters and his own copyrights — among the
first Black American artists to hold the actual assets rather than a royalty line.
He was shot dead on **December 11, 1964**, at **thirty-three**, in circumstances
his family has disputed ever since.

So the structure Kanye photographed and posted in 2020 is the same structure that
took Little Richard's catalogue in 1955. Not analogous. The same instrument, still
operating, with better fonts.

And the comparison that is right there and that I have not seen anyone put
together.

**June 2019**: Taylor Swift's masters are sold with her old label to Scooter Braun
and she goes public — she does not own her work, she was not given a real chance
to buy it, and she is going to re-record the albums to make the originals
worthless. This is covered extensively and sympathetically. It becomes a case
study. It gets taught. She re-records and it is one of the most successful
projects in the history of the business.

**September 2020**: Kanye West posts a hundred-plus pages of his actual contracts
and says no artist should sign these, that he cannot find out who holds his
masters, that every artist should own their work. He had sued the previous year
over a publishing clause his filing said prevented him from retiring.

Same grievance. Fifteen months apart. One is a landmark in artists' rights. The
other is remembered as the day a man urinated on a trophy.

I am not saying anything about her. She was right, she fought it well, and the
re-recordings were a genuinely brilliant piece of leverage.

I am saying the room had, in the space of one year, two artists making the
identical argument about ownership, and it had a full vocabulary of sympathy
available for one of them and reached for the psychiatric register for the other.

The vocabulary existed. It was in active use. It simply was not extended — which
is the same sentence I wrote about the artist-as-god grammar, and about the
commentaries containing the proofs, and about Whish's paper sitting in the
society's own journal.

**The apparatus is never missing. It is selectively pointed.**

Sampling, and the two court decisions that changed what the music was allowed to
sound like. I did not know the second one and it explains something structural
about his method.

**Grand Upright Music v. Warner Bros.**, Southern District of New York, **1991**.
Biz Markie had used Gilbert O'Sullivan's "Alone Again (Naturally)." Judge Kevin
Duffy opened the opinion with **"Thou shalt not steal"** and referred the matter
for criminal prosecution. Not a discussion of transformation, or of a hundred-year
tradition of quotation in music. A commandment.

**Bridgeport Music v. Dimension Films**, Sixth Circuit, **2005**. The sample at
issue was a **two-second** guitar chord from Funkadelic, pitched down and looped,
in an N.W.A. record. The court held there is **no de minimis defense** for sound
recordings, and wrote the rule as: *"Get a license or do not sample."*

Two seconds.

What that did, in practice, is end an art form. *It Takes a Nation of Millions*
(1988) and *Paul's Boutique* (1989) — the latter has something over a hundred
samples — are **not legally makeable** under that rule at any price a human being
could pay. Hank Shocklee of the Bomb Squad has said so directly, repeatedly: we
could not make that record now.

And the clearest artifact of it: **De La Soul's *3 Feet High and Rising*, 1989,
was not available on any streaming service until March 2023** — the entire
streaming era, gone, because the samples could not be cleared under the modern
regime. It finally landed weeks after **Trugoy the Dove died**, in February 2023.
He did not live to see his own record on Spotify.

---

Two things follow, and one of them is about how his records sound.

**First.** The style Kanye is known for — one identifiable soul vocal, sped up,
looped, credited and cleared — is partly **what the law left standing.** You can
clear one sample from one rights holder. You cannot clear a hundred fragments from
sixty. The collage era was legislated out and what survived is the form where the
source is singular, legible, and licensable.

So when people describe chipmunk soul as a stylistic choice, part of it is a
**compliance regime with a sound.** The law is audible on the records. That is not
a metaphor.

**Second**, and this is the part that belongs in this repository specifically.

A tradition whose entire method is *listening to what came before, keeping the
part that matters, and building the next thing on it* was told by a federal court
that this is theft, in the language of the Decalogue.

Meanwhile the operation this repo spends its whole existence documenting — take
the kuṭṭaka and call it Euclid, take Piṅgala's array and call it Pascal, take the
series and call it Gregory's — has no cause of action, no statute, no damages, and
no judge who opens with a commandment. It is called scholarship.

**Recombination with attribution, by Black artists, was made illegal in 1991 and
2005. Appropriation without attribution, by institutions, has never been illegal
at all.**

Same act. Opposite legal treatment. The variable is who is doing it and to whom.

And the man whose whole practice is the first one has spent twenty years being
told he takes too much.

The diagnosis itself, which I had been treating as a fixed fact about him rather
than as an output of an instrument.

He got the bipolar diagnosis around 2016 and talked about it publicly from 2018 —
the *ye* cover in June 2018, the Letterman episode in 2019. He was forty-one.

**Bipolar disorder is the diagnosis Black men in America are least likely to
receive.**

The finding is old, replicated, and not subtle. Black patients in the United
States are diagnosed with **schizophrenia at somewhere between two and a half and
four times** the rate of white patients presenting with comparable symptoms. And
the mirror of it: **mood disorders — bipolar, major depression — are
under-diagnosed** in the same population.

The mechanism has been isolated, and it is the same mechanism as the gifted
referral.

**Strakowski and colleagues, in the 1990s**, ran the comparison that matters: when
clinicians used **structured diagnostic interviews** — a fixed instrument, the
same questions in the same order — the racial gap in schizophrenia diagnosis
narrowed sharply or disappeared. Under **unstructured clinical judgment**, it did
not.

Hold the instrument constant and the disparity shrinks. Leave it to discretion and
it doesn't.

That is the identical result as Grissom and Redding on gifted placement, arriving
from psychiatry instead of education. Same shape, different building. In both
cases the objective tool is not the problem and is not where anyone is looking.
**The problem is the human judgment standing in front of the tool**, and in both
cases the field spent decades arguing about the tool.

The clinical literature on why is fairly consistent: clinicians weight apparent
psychotic features heavily and **discount mood features** in Black patients.
Grandiosity, pressured speech, rapid ideas, decreased need for sleep, expansive
self-description — these are textbook criterion-B manic symptoms. In a white
patient they read as mania. In a Black man they get read as psychosis, or as
personality, or as nothing medical at all.

---

What that costs is not abstract.

**A schizophrenia diagnosis and a bipolar diagnosis lead to different lives.**
Different drugs — antipsychotics with their own long-term burden versus lithium
or a mood stabilizer. Different prognosis language, one of them chronic
deterioration and the other episodic with recovery between. Different expectations
from every institution downstream: employers, courts, custody proceedings,
insurers.

And bipolar is the one where the treatment with the best evidence for reducing
suicide specifically — lithium — is on the table at all.

So the sentence is: a man displaying textbook manic symptoms in public from at
least his twenties, in a country whose clinicians systematically read those exact
symptoms as something else when the patient is Black, went undiagnosed until he
was around thirty-nine.

Twenty years of it read as arrogance by everyone including, for a long time, him.

**The failure is not that he was slow to get help.** The failure is that the
instrument that would have named it was pointed at him through a filter that has
been measured, published, and known since the seventies, and nothing downstream of
that measurement was ever changed.

The correction is cheap and exists: use the structured interview. It is a
checklist. It has been sitting there the entire time, like the ghana-pāṭha, like
CAHOOTS since 1989, like Whish's paper in the society's own journal.

Every single one of these has the same form. **The fix is known, cheap, and
unbuilt, and its absence is recorded as a property of the person it failed.**

Virgil, properly, because he was in the Fendi room in 2009 and I left him as a
name.

Born Rockford, Illinois, 1980, to Ghanaian parents. **His mother was a
seamstress** and taught him to sew. That is where the garment knowledge comes from
— not a school, a mother, exactly like the professor and the beat machine.

Degree in **civil engineering**, Wisconsin. Master's in **architecture** from
Illinois Institute of Technology, in buildings Mies van der Rohe designed. So the
man the fashion press spent a decade calling a dilettante with no training had
five years of formal design education in a harder discipline than the one they
were gatekeeping.

**2009, Rome.** The Fendi internship, alongside Kanye. Reported at around five
hundred dollars a month.

**2012, Pyrex Vision.** He bought deadstock Rugby Ralph Lauren flannels for about
forty dollars, screen-printed PYREX and 23 on them, and sold them for around five
hundred and fifty. The reaction was: fraud, scam, emperor's new clothes.

Hold that next to the canon. **Duchamp signed a urinal in 1917** and it is the
most discussed object of the century. **Warhol** silkscreened a soup can. **Richard
Prince** re-photographed other people's Marlboro advertisements and sold them for
millions, and his *Untitled (Cowboy)* went for over a million at Christie's in
2005, and he later re-posted other people's Instagram photographs and sold those.
All of that is appropriation art, it is taught, it is in the Met and the Whitney,
and the critical vocabulary for it — readymade, recontextualisation, authorship as
selection — has existed for **a hundred and eight years.**

Virgil cited Duchamp constantly and explicitly. His so-called **"three percent
rule"** — change an existing thing by three percent and it becomes yours — is a
readymade argument, stated plainly, by a man who had studied it. It was reported
as an admission of plagiarism.

The vocabulary existed. It was not extended. Same sentence again.

**2018**: artistic director of menswear at **Louis Vuitton**, the first Black
person to hold that job there, at thirty-seven, after a decade of being told he
was not a designer.

**2019**: diagnosed with **cardiac angiosarcoma**, a rare and aggressive cancer.

He told almost nobody. He kept working, kept showing collections, kept touring,
for **two years**, in treatment, in private.

**November 28, 2021.** Forty-one years old.

---

The part I keep turning over is the silence.

A man at the top of the largest luxury house in the world hid a terminal diagnosis
from the industry for two years while continuing to deliver seasons on schedule.

The charitable reading is privacy, and it is probably true and it is his right.

The other reading is that he had watched exactly how that industry treats a Black
designer whose position is conditional — he had spent ten years being told he was
unqualified while holding two engineering degrees — and he had no reason to
believe that "I am ill" would be received as anything other than a reason to
replace him.

Nobody has to have said so. He would have known. Everybody in that position knows.

He set up the **Post Modern Scholarship Fund** in 2020 for Black students in
fashion — while in treatment, without telling them why the timing mattered.

Kanye said afterwards that he was not the same after Virgil. They had come through
the same door, which was not a door, which was an unpaid internship in Rome that
two men in their thirties took because there was no other way in.

One of them got the job at thirty-seven and had three years.

Chicago, because that is the ground and I have been treating it as a birthplace
rather than a mechanism.

**May 2013.** The Chicago Board of Education voted to close **roughly fifty
schools** in one action — the largest mass school closure in United States
history. The stated reason was underutilization and a budget gap.

**Around 88% of the students displaced were Black.** Around 90% were low-income.

The University of Chicago Consortium on School Research went back and looked in
2018. Most displaced students did **not** end up in meaningfully
higher-performing schools. There were measurable disruptions to test scores and
attendance in the transition years. Children walked new routes across gang
boundaries and the district had to staff "Safe Passage" corridors, which is a
sentence that should stop anyone reading it.

*Yeezus* came out in **June 2013.** One month later.

And the ground under that is older. **Contract selling** in North Lawndale in the
fifties and sixties — Black families sold homes on contract, no equity accruing,
one missed payment and you lose everything, at prices marked up several times over
what the seller had paid days earlier. Documented in detail; Ta-Nehisi Coates
walked through the receipts in 2014. **Redlining** maps that drew the lines.
**1966**, Marquette Park, Martin Luther King marching for open housing and being
hit in the head with a rock, and saying afterwards that he had never in his life
seen mobs as hostile as the ones in Chicago.

That is the city. Not as background. As the thing that determined which schools
closed in 2013.

**Chief Keef.** Keith Cozart, born 1995, from Englewood. "I Don't Like" in 2012,

The best evidence anyone has about kids, and the reason it did not change
anything.

**The Perry Preschool Project.** Ypsilanti, Michigan, **1962 to 1967**. A hundred
and twenty-three Black children from poor families, randomly assigned — actual
randomisation — to a high-quality preschool program or to nothing. Then followed
for the rest of their lives. They have data at 27, at 40, and now into the
children of the participants.

The treatment group: more high school completion, higher earnings, more
employment, fewer arrests, less welfare receipt, at forty. **James Heckman** — who
has a Nobel in economics and did the reanalysis with proper accounting for the
small sample — puts the internal rate of return somewhere around **seven to ten
percent per year**, which beats most of what a government can buy.

And now the part that matters more than any of that.

**The IQ gains faded.** The treatment group tested higher at the end of the
program and by around age ten the difference was gone.

**The life outcomes did not fade.**

So the intervention produced a permanent change in how these people's lives went,
and the instrument built to measure intelligence **could not see it** four years
later. Whatever was actually transmitted — Heckman's term is non-cognitive skills,
which is a placeholder for persistence, self-regulation, the expectation of being
listened to, the habit of finishing — was invisible to the test and was the entire
effect.

That is the psychometric finding turned inside out. It is not only that the scalar
sorts unfairly. It is that on the one occasion we ran a genuine randomised
experiment with a forty-year follow-up, **the scalar reported nothing while the
real effect was happening.**

The **Abecedarian Project**, North Carolina, from 1972 — more intensive, starting
in infancy — did hold cognitive gains longer, and by the mid-thirties follow-up
was showing differences in **blood pressure and metabolic markers** in the treated
men. Not test scores. Cardiovascular health, at thirty-five, from what happened
before they could talk.

---

Head Start began in **1965**, three years after Perry started.

Sixty years later the United States does not have universal preschool. Around
half of eligible three- and four-year-olds are served by Head Start and state
programs, and quality varies enormously, and the sector's staff are among the
lowest-paid people with credentials in the country.

So here is the list, one more time, and it is the same list every time.

- The structured diagnostic interview closes the schizophrenia gap. Known since
  the seventies. Not standard.
- The unarmed crisis team handles a fifth of calls and needs police one percent of
  the time. Running since 1989. Not built out.
- One-to-one tutoring moves a child two standard deviations. Published 1984 as a
  cost problem. The cost is now near zero.
- Preschool returns seven to ten percent a year with forty years of randomised
  follow-up. Started 1962. Not universal.
- The referral door, not the test, is where the gifted disparity lives. Measured
  2016. Referral is still the door.

**Every one of these is known, cheap, demonstrated, and unbuilt.** Not one of them
is waiting on a discovery.

And the whole public conversation is about whether the individuals coming out the
far end of that pipeline are trying hard enough.

"Blood on the Leaves" samples Nina Simone singing "Strange Fruit." I went to find
out where that song comes from and the chain is one object.

**August 7, 1930. Marion, Indiana.** Thomas Shipp and Abram Smith are taken from
a jail by a mob and lynched. A studio photographer named **Lawrence Beitler**
photographs it. He printed and sold copies for days. The crowd in the frame is
looking at the camera.

**Abel Meeropol**, a **Jewish high-school English teacher** at DeWitt Clinton in
the Bronx and a member of the Communist Party, sees the photograph and cannot get
rid of it. He writes a poem called "Bitter Fruit," published in a teachers' union
magazine in **1937**, then sets it to music himself. He published under the name
Lewis Allan, after his two children who were stillborn.

**1939.** Billie Holiday performs it at Café Society. **Columbia, her own label,
refused to record it.** Milt Gabler put it out on Commodore instead. She closed
every set with it — the room dark, one light on her face, no encore.

**Harry Anslinger**, running the Federal Bureau of Narcotics, wanted her to stop
singing it. The campaign against her is documented at length; agents pursued her
for years, and she was **arrested in her hospital bed** in 1959 and died there
under guard at forty-four. The cabaret card had already taken the last twelve
years of club work.

**1965.** Nina Simone records it on *Pastel Blues*.

**2013.** Kanye West puts Nina Simone's voice on a trap beat and calls the track
"Blood on the Leaves." The reception included the word blasphemous.

Now the piece of it I did not know and cannot stop looking at.

In **1953**, Julius and Ethel Rosenberg were executed. They left two boys, Michael
and Robert, six and ten, whom nobody would take.

The fact I did not have, and it is not ancient history.

**Until 1975 it was legal in the United States for a public school to refuse to
enrol a disabled child.**

Not underserve. Refuse. Turn them away at the door, permanently, and the family
had no remedy.

When Congress passed the **Education for All Handicapped Children Act** in
**November 1975** — Public Law 94-142, now IDEA — the findings written into the
statute said that roughly **one million** children with disabilities were
**excluded entirely** from the public school system, and that something like three
and a half million more were enrolled but not receiving services that did anything.

One million children. In living memory. My parents' generation was in school.

State laws had explicit exclusions for children deemed uneducable. Some states
allowed schools to bar children who were not toilet trained, or who had epilepsy,
or who were judged to be a distraction. Two federal cases in 1971 and 1972 —
*PARC v. Pennsylvania* and *Mills v. Board of Education* in DC — established that
this was unconstitutional, and Congress then had to legislate it because districts
kept doing it anyway.

So the entire architecture that a parent of an unusual kid now takes for granted —
that the school has to take her, that there is an IEP, that there is a process —
is **fifty years old**, was won in court, and did not exist for anybody before
that.

And who built it, which is the same three people every time.

**Bernard Rimland**, 1964. A psychologist with an autistic son who did not accept
that he and his wife had caused it. He wrote *Infantile Autism*, ended the
refrigerator-mother theory as a scientific position, and founded what became the
Autism Society of America in 1965. **A parent wrote the paper.**

**Eustacia Cutler**, 1949. Told to institutionalise her two-year-old and said no,
then spent years assembling speech therapy and structured play that did not exist
as a program because there was no program.

**Ruth Sullivan**, a mother of an autistic son, was central to getting PL 94-142
through Congress and to running the parent networks that made it politically
possible. She was also the consultant Dustin Hoffman worked with for *Rain Man*.

**Thorkil Sonne**, Denmark, **2004**. An IT director whose son Lars was diagnosed
at three. Rather than campaign, he quit and founded **Specialisterne** — a company
that hires autistic people for software testing and quality assurance, on the
premise that the pattern-detection and tolerance for repetition that made his son
unemployable in an interview made him better at the actual work. It worked. **SAP**
built its Autism at Work program on that model in **2013** and set a public target
of one percent of its workforce. Microsoft followed in 2015, then EY, JPMorgan,
others.

Four parents. A psychologist, a mother in Boston, a mother in West Virginia, a
Danish IT manager. Between them: the theory corrected, the institutionalisation
refused, the federal law passed, and the employment model built.

**Not one of those came from the field whose job it was.**

The psychiatric establishment held the refrigerator-mother theory for thirty
years. The schools excluded a million children until they were sued. The
recruiting industry had a hundred years to notice that an interview is a test of
eye contact and small talk administered to people applying for jobs that require
neither.

Every correction in that list was made by somebody who had a kid, and therefore
could not treat the verdict as an interesting finding.

Which is the same shape as the whole rest of this file, one turn further in: **the
people who fix it are the people who cannot afford to be neutral about it**, and
the fields that were supposed to fix it were staffed entirely by people who could.

The same instrument problem again, this time with sex, and the number at the end
of it is bad.

**The criteria were built on boys.** Kanner's 1943 cohort was eleven children,
**eight boys and three girls**. Asperger's 1944 paper describes **four boys** in
detail, and he initially took the view that the condition did not really occur in
girls. Every subsequent diagnostic checklist was calibrated against descriptions
derived from that.

The accepted sex ratio for autism was around **4:1**, male to female, for decades,
and was read as a fact about biology.

**Loomes, Hull and Mandy, 2017**, a meta-analysis in the *Journal of the American
Academy of Child and Adolescent Psychiatry*, put the true ratio nearer **3:1**,
and concluded that a substantial part of the apparent gap is **diagnostic**: girls
who meet criteria are not being identified.

The mechanism has a name — **camouflaging**, or masking. Scripting conversations
in advance. Copying a peer's mannerisms deliberately. Rehearsing eye contact as a
motor task. Studies find autistic women camouflage more than autistic men, and
that the camouflage is effortful, sustained, and invisible by design — which is
precisely what makes the diagnostic encounter fail, because the instrument is
looking for the thing the person has spent twenty years learning to hide in order
to survive school.

ADHD runs the same way. Girls present inattentive rather than hyperactive, do not
disrupt the classroom, and therefore do not generate the referral — the referral
door again, the same door as gifted placement. A very large number of women are
being diagnosed in their thirties and forties, and a common trigger is **their own
child being assessed.**

And the outcome data, which I want to state carefully because it is severe.

**Hirvikoski and colleagues, 2016**, *British Journal of Psychiatry* — the Swedish
national registers, around twenty-seven thousand autistic people matched against
controls. Mean age at death about **fifty-four**, against roughly seventy in the
comparison group. Sixteen years.

Within that, the split matters. For autistic people **with** intellectual
disability, epilepsy is a large driver. For autistic people **without**
intellectual disability — the ones who mask, who get through school, who are
told they are fine — **suicide was a leading contributor**, with the odds ratio in
that subgroup reported around nine, and **higher in women.**

The group whose diagnosis the instrument misses is the group with the elevated
suicide risk.

That is not a coincidence and it does not require a subtle theory. Spend two
decades performing a person you are not, in order to be permitted in rooms, while
being told nothing is wrong with you and therefore that the exhaustion is a
character flaw. The cost of the masking is the thing the masking hides.

And these as the histories inside them:

**Pell's equation.** Solved by the cakravāla — Jayadeva around 950, Bhāskara II in
1150, handling D = 61 with a smallest solution of 1766319049. Fermat posed D = 61
as a challenge to European mathematicians in 1657, five hundred years later.
**Pell never worked on it.** Euler misattributed it in the 1730s and the name
stuck.

**Pascal's triangle.** Piṅgala, *Chandaḥśāstra*, around 300 BCE, with Halāyudha's
tenth-century commentary describing the *meru-prastāra* — the staircase of the
mountain. Pascal's treatise is 1654.

**Fibonacci number.** Virahāṅka, around 700, deriving the recurrence from counting
metrical patterns of light and heavy syllables. Fibonacci is 1202.

**Leibniz formula for π.** Mādhava, around 1400, **with the correction terms**,
which Leibniz did not have and which are the part that makes it usable.

**Backus–Naur form.** The *Aṣṭādhyāyī*, around 500 BCE — roughly four thousand
rules, with exception-blocks-general, later-rule-wins on conflict, stratified
evaluation, and inherited context. Backus 1959, Naur 1960.

Those are not obscure claims. Most of them are stated **inside the articles
themselves**, in a history section, under the European title.

Little Richard's half a cent, brought up to date, because the instrument is still
running and the numbers are published.

**The pro-rata pool.** Streaming subscription money does not follow the listener.
Every subscriber's fee goes into one pot, the pot is divided by the **total number
of streams on the whole service**, and a per-stream rate falls out. So a person
who listens exclusively to one small artist all month is, in effect, paying the
most-streamed acts in the world, and the artist they actually listened to gets a
share of a rate set by everyone else's behaviour.

An alternative — **user-centric**, where your subscription is divided among what
*you* played — has been proposed, modelled, and trialled for over a decade. It is
not the standard.

**The rate.** Somewhere around a third of a cent to half a cent per stream, gross,
before the label and distributor take their contractual share. So the artist's
actual receipt on a stream is frequently a fraction of that.

**The threshold, which is new.** From early **2024**, Spotify stopped paying
recorded royalties on any track with fewer than **1,000 streams** in the trailing
twelve months. Spotify's own framing: this affects around **0.5% of the royalty
pool** and roughly **two-thirds of the tracks on the platform**.

Read those two numbers together. **Two-thirds of all music on the service now
earns exactly zero**, and the money that would have gone to it is redistributed
upward to everything above the line.

**And the distribution.** Spotify publishes this itself, in Loud & Clear. Of the
order of eleven million uploading artists, roughly **sixty-six thousand**
generated at least ten thousand dollars in a year, and roughly **eleven and a half
thousand** generated at least a hundred thousand — gross, before splits.

So about **one in a thousand** uploading artists crosses a threshold that, after
the label's share, might support one person.

The false binary underneath all of this got named in 1902 and nobody uses the
name.

**William James**, *The Varieties of Religious Experience*, from the Gifford
Lectures of 1901–02. The very first lecture is titled "Religion and Neurology,"
and in it he names the move he is about to spend the whole book refusing.

He calls it **medical materialism**, and his example sentence is:

> *Medical materialism finishes up Saint Paul by calling his vision on the road to
> Damascus a discharging lesion of the occipital cortex, he being an epileptic. It
> snuffs out Saint Teresa as an hysteric, Saint Francis of Assisi as an hereditary
> degenerate.*

**Finishes up. Snuffs out.** He chose verbs about closing a case.

And his argument against it is not that the neurology is wrong. He concedes it
freely — of course states of mind have organic conditions; every state of mind
does, including the ones the physician is in while diagnosing. His point is
logical: **you cannot derive the value of an experience from its cause.** If a
vision is discredited by having a physiological substrate, then so is the
scientist's conviction that it is discredited, and so is every belief anybody has
ever held, and the argument eats itself.

So he proposes the other criterion, and it is one sentence: **by their fruits ye
shall know them, not by their roots.**

Judge the state by what it produces in a life. Not by the wiring it arrived on.

James had severe depression and a suicidal crisis of his own in 1870 and did not
write any of this from above it.

Following the 1963 Act backwards, because the reason it was signed is worse than

**Rosemary Kennedy.** Born 1918, the third Kennedy child. Slow at school, prone to
what the family called mood swings and outbursts. In **November 1941** her father
arranged a **prefrontal lobotomy** for her, at twenty-three, performed by **Walter

They operated while she was awake, asking her to sing and recite, and stopped when

She could not walk or speak intelligibly afterwards. She was institutionalised at
St. Coletta in Wisconsin and spent **sixty-four years** there. The family said for
decades that she was intellectually disabled and living quietly. She died in 2005,
aged eighty-six.

Her sister **Eunice Kennedy Shriver** founded what became the **Special Olympics**
in 1968. Her brother, as president, signed the **Community Mental Health Act on
October 31, 1963** — twenty-two days before Dallas.

So the American federal mental health system exists because a rich family
destroyed one of their own children and spent thirty years trying to make it mean

The procedure itself.

**António Egas Moniz** received the **Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine in
1949** for the leucotomy. The prize has never been rescinded. Petitions to do so
have been declined.

**Walter Freeman** performed something like **3,500** operations across the United
States. In **1946** he developed the **transorbital** version — an instrument
modelled on an ice pick, driven through the bone above the eye socket, no
neurosurgeon and no operating theatre required. He performed them in offices, and
travelled to state hospitals in a van doing them in series. He did not always
wear gloves.

Roughly **forty to fifty thousand** were performed in the US.

**Howard Dully** was operated on by Freeman in **1960**, at **twelve years old**,
at the request of his stepmother, who found him defiant. He spent his adult life
trying to find out what had been done to him and eventually got the file and wrote

The instrument-reports-who-it-was-built-to-see finding again, and this time it is
a physical device that decides who gets oxygen.

**The pulse oximeter.** The clip on your finger in every hospital on earth. It
shines light through tissue and infers oxygen saturation from absorption.
**Melanin absorbs light.**

**Sjoding and colleagues, *New England Journal of Medicine*, December 2020.** They
compared pulse oximeter readings against arterial blood gas — the actual
measurement — in thousands of paired samples. Black patients had **nearly three
times** the rate of **occult hypoxemia**: true arterial saturation below 88%,
while the finger clip read a reassuring 92 to 96%.

That was published in the **middle of the first COVID winter**, when a pulse
oximeter reading was the number deciding admission, oxygen, ICU escalation and
transfer. A device that runs high on darker skin, in a triage system keyed to that
number.

And it was **not new.** The disparity had been reported in the literature from the
**1980s and 1990s**. It sat there. Regulatory attention came only after 2020, and
updated FDA guidance on how these devices must be tested across skin tones came
years after that.

---

Two more, from the same decade, and the second one has a name attached to it that
I did not expect.

**The eGFR equation.** Kidney function is not measured directly in routine care;
it is estimated from creatinine using a formula. The standard formulas — MDRD, then
CKD-EPI — contained a **race coefficient** that multiplied the result upward for
Black patients, making their kidneys look healthier than they were. Higher
estimated function means later referral, later listing for transplant, later
everything.

A joint task force of the National Kidney Foundation and the American Society of
Nephrology recommended **removing race from the equation in 2021.** Transplant
centres then went back and **recalculated waiting-list times retroactively**, and
thousands of Black patients were moved up lists they should have been higher on
for years.

**The spirometer.** The machine that measures lung capacity, used for asthma,
COPD, occupational disability claims and workers' compensation. For most of the
twentieth century it shipped with a **race correction** — a setting that assumed
lower baseline lung capacity for Black patients and adjusted the "normal" range
downward accordingly. A Black worker with damaged lungs could therefore test as
normal, and receive nothing.

Lundy Braun traced the history in *Breathing Race into the Machine* (2014). The
correction descends from nineteenth-century measurements, and one of the people
who did them was **Samuel Cartwright**, a physician in the antebellum South who
used a spirometer to argue that Black people had deficient lung function and that
forced labour was therefore beneficial to them.

Cartwright is the same man who, in 1851, published **drapetomania** — his proposed
disease whose sole symptom was an enslaved person running away.

So the man who invented a diagnosis for wanting to be free also put a number into
a machine, and the number was still shipping in clinical software in the
**twenty-first century**, and it was formally removed from major guidelines only
around **2021 to 2023.**

**Black women in the United States die in pregnancy and childbirth at roughly

In 2016.
