The same wiring that wouldn't let Kanye West sleep, that ran more connections
between more things than any mind is built to hold, that made him reckless enough to
drive his own face into a dashboard and unfiltered enough to tell the most powerful
man on earth the truth on live television — is one wiring, and America has spent his
whole life cheering its bright end as genius and locking its dark end away as crazy,
never once admitting they are the same current in the same man. In October 2002 that
current ran him off the road at twenty-five; two weeks later, jaw bolted shut, he
rapped through the wire, and the sickness that nearly killed him became the sound
that made him immortal.

That is the whole book, and it already happened in ten seconds of a song most people
skip. Doctors call the wiring bipolar. Its manic side does not sleep, and the science
now knows why the not-sleeping matters: the same drop in the brain's filter that lets
a manic mind pour unrelated things together into something no careful mind would have
built — the flood we call inspiration — also lifts the brakes on the body, and a
person with the brakes off drives too fast. The crash was not bad luck that
interrupted his gift. The crash was his gift, aimed at a highway instead of a record.
Recklessness and revelation come out of the one lifted filter. He is the proof, face
first, at twenty-five.

So watch what he does with it, because this is the move he will make every single
time for the next twenty years, and if you learn to see it here you will never
unsee it. He does not wait to heal and then, safe, tell the story. He goes to the
studio with the wire still on his teeth and raps his near-death out through a mouth
that won't open, over Chaka Khan sped up until she sounds like she's weeping with
joy — her old song "Through the Fire" turned into his "Through the Wire," because
that is what his ear does, it hears in a happy record from 1984 a survival hymn
nobody knew they'd left him. Believe him completely here: nobody's watching yet, no
crowd to lie for, just a hurt nobody producer in the dark deciding that whatever
tries to kill him will be made to sing. That is not how you cope with pain. That is
a theology, arriving whole at twenty-five: the pain does not get to be wasted.

Everything after is that same sentence, sung louder, at higher cost, until the world
that clapped for the wire is calling for the cross.

When Kanye West says "I am a god," you have half a second to decide whether it's the
sickness talking or the truth, and America always chooses sickness — because a
country that hands godhood to dead men for free cannot survive a living Black one
claiming it out loud, in a leather kilt, on his own record. But watch the thing you
were taught to laugh at. He does not say "I am a god" to stand above you. The next
breath is a hurried prayer and a want for everyone to eat; the same mouth that claims
the throne spends the next decade bailing strangers out of jail and singing to men in
county lockups and putting on a free Sunday Service so anybody can come. His godhood
has content, and the content is not power over you — it is love with no bottom, the
god in him insisting on the god in everyone, which is the oldest and most dangerous
sentence a man can say to a world built on some people being less. That is not a god
complex. That is godliness, and we can't tell them apart from the outside, and the
not-being-able-to-tell is the whole point: believe him, and it's a prophet; doubt
him, and it's a lunatic, and the man is identical in both.

And he is prophetic about his own life, which is the strangest gift and the one that
gets him killed. He said he was the greatest before there was proof. He said they
would call him crazy. He said, in so many words, that he would be crucified — and
then he was, on schedule, by the same people who'd cheered. When a man speaks his
life before he lives it and it comes true, you are looking at one of two things and
you cannot say which: a delusion that got lucky, or a soul bending the world to his
word — and bending the world to your word is exactly what godliness would look like
from inside a single life. "You ain't got the answers, Sway" was that man, on the
radio, being told to be smaller — just do it yourself, just wait your turn — and
refusing, because he already had the whole vision entire and would not beg the
gatekeepers for a permission slip to build it. They locked a Chicago genius out of
the fashion houses the way they lock everyone Black out of the ownership he'd been
screaming about, and the internet turned his cornered roar into a punchline, and
every laugh was a small nail, and he was right. He was right the whole time. He said
so, before it was true, until it was true.

The spiritual journey is just that arc, walked out loud where we could watch: from
"I am a god" to "Jesus is king," from claiming the throne to kneeling at it, a man
converting himself in public over ten years and getting mocked at every station of
it. Believe him at each one. That's the only way to hear that it was always the same
prayer.
