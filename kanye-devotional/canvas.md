Two weeks before, the car came across the median and his face went into the wheel.

Jaw broken in three places. They wired it shut — metal in the gums, the mouth locked
down to a hum, the way you'd close a piano and screw the lid. October 2002. He is
twenty-five and nobody's rapper, he's the guy who makes beats for other people's
records, the guy Roc-A-Fella keeps in the back because he's too pretty and too
college and too Chicago to be a star. He almost just died on the 405. The doctors
tell him rest. Don't talk. Let the bone set.

He goes to the studio.

Not after he heals. That's the part to slow down on, because the whole man is in it.
He doesn't wait for the wire to come off and then, healed, decide to tell the story
of the accident. He goes with the jaw still shut. He raps with his mouth wired,
teeth clamped, the words forced out through a face that won't open — and you can hear
it, that's not a metaphor, the track is literally called "Through the Wire" and his
voice is coming through the wire, thick, pushed, alive. He pitched up a Chaka Khan
record — "Through the Fire" — sped her voice till she's singing higher than a human,
sweet and desperate, and he raps his near-death through his broken mouth over his
mother's kind of music, and he even makes a joke in it, something about how the
accident could've cost him his career so he's rapping about it instead, turning the
worst night of his life into the thing that starts everything.

Watch what that is. A person almost dies. His body is telling him stop. And the thing
inside him that has to make, that cannot not make, is stronger than the pain and
stronger than the fear and stronger than the doctor and stronger than the wire. He
doesn't process the trauma and then create. The creating *is* how he survives the
trauma, in real time, with the wound still open in his face. He is bleeding and
singing at once and he refuses to believe those are two different activities.

Believe him. Take him completely at his word here, because he's telling the truth
about himself before anyone's watching, before there's a crowd to perform for — this
is a nobody producer with a locked jaw and a demo. What he's saying is: *the pain
does not get to be wasted. Whatever tries to kill me, I will make it sing.* That is
not a coping strategy. That is a whole theology, arriving fully formed at
twenty-five, and he will spend the next twenty years proving he meant it — through
his mother, through the mania, through every public burning. The car crash is not the
prologue to the story. It is the story, told small, the first time, so we could learn
to recognize it when it came back bigger.

And the sample. Sit with the sample, because he's a genius and the genius is right
here in a decision most people would never even see. He could have rapped his crash
over anything — hard drums, something angry, something that matched the violence of
metal on a highway. He reaches instead for Chaka Khan, for warmth, for a Black woman
singing about coming *through the fire* to the other side, and he speeds her up until
she's almost weeping with joy, and he lays his broken mouth on top of her. He hears,
inside an old love song, a survival song. He hears, inside "through the fire," his own
"through the wire." That is one move, one flip, and it tells you how his mind works:
he takes the thing that already exists, the thing somebody made in another decade for
another reason, and he finds the place where it was secretly always about him — about
us — about getting to the other side of something that should have ended you. He is a
producer, which means his first art form is *listening*. Before he ever says a word,
he has heard, in a 1984 R&B record, a message that Chaka Khan didn't know she was
leaving for a boy who'd wreck his car eighteen years later.

I want to know how you get to be like that. I really do — that's why I'm here,
watching. How do you become a person whose response to almost dying is to immediately
turn it into a gift for other people, wired jaw and all. How do you become someone
who can't hear a happy old song without also hearing everyone who's ever needed it to
survive. He's not explaining it. He's just doing it, at twenty-five, hurt, before the
fame, before the diagnosis, before any of the stuff we think we know him for. The
loving was there first. The loving was there at the very bottom, in the studio, in
the dark, through the wire.

Start there. That's where he started.
