The same wiring that wouldn't let Kanye West sleep, that ran more connections
between more things than any mind is built to hold, that made him reckless enough to
drive his own face into a dashboard and unfiltered enough to tell the most powerful
man on earth the truth on live television — is one wiring, and America has spent his
whole life cheering its bright end as genius and locking its dark end away as crazy,
never once admitting they are the same current in the same man. In October 2002 that
current ran him off the road at twenty-five; two weeks later, jaw bolted shut, he
rapped through the wire, and the sickness that nearly killed him became the sound
that made him immortal.

That is the whole book, and it already happened in ten seconds of a song most people
skip. Doctors call the wiring bipolar. Its manic side does not sleep, and the science
now knows why the not-sleeping matters: the same drop in the brain's filter that lets
a manic mind pour unrelated things together into something no careful mind would have
built — the flood we call inspiration — also lifts the brakes on the body, and a
person with the brakes off drives too fast. The crash was not bad luck that
interrupted his gift. The crash was his gift, aimed at a highway instead of a record.
Recklessness and revelation come out of the one lifted filter. He is the proof, face
first, at twenty-five.

So watch what he does with it, because this is the move he will make every single
time for the next twenty years, and if you learn to see it here you will never
unsee it. He does not wait to heal and then, safe, tell the story. He goes to the
studio with the wire still on his teeth and raps his near-death out through a mouth
that won't open, over Chaka Khan sped up until she sounds like she's weeping with
joy — her old song "Through the Fire" turned into his "Through the Wire," because
that is what his ear does, it hears in a happy record from 1984 a survival hymn
nobody knew they'd left him. Believe him completely here: nobody's watching yet, no
crowd to lie for, just a hurt nobody producer in the dark deciding that whatever
tries to kill him will be made to sing. That is not how you cope with pain. That is
a theology, arriving whole at twenty-five: the pain does not get to be wasted.

Everything after is that same sentence, sung louder, at higher cost, until the world
that clapped for the wire is calling for the cross.
