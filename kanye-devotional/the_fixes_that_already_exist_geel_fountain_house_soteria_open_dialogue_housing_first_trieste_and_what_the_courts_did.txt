Second stream. Append only. Facts I went looking for because the other stream's
title says every fix is already known, cheap and proven, and the fixes were not
in it yet. Marked [recall] where I am working from memory and could not open a
source from this container.

---

Geel, Belgium. Family foster care for the mentally ill, running continuously for
somewhere around seven hundred years, attached to the shrine of Saint Dymphna.
[recall]

The mechanics are the part. A person comes to the town and lives in a family's
house. They are called *boarders*, not patients. They eat at the table, work if
they work, go to the cafe. Host families are paid a small stipend by the state.
Peak was somewhere around 4,000 boarders in the 1930s, in a town of a few tens of
thousands. A few hundred now. [recall]

And the detail that keeps getting reported and that I keep turning over: the host
families are **not told the diagnosis**. Nobody in the house knows whether the man
at the table is schizophrenic or bipolar or something else. There is nothing to
manage, so what happens instead is that he is somebody who lives there. [recall]

Seven centuries, no theory, no treatment model, and it is one of the outcomes
people fly to Belgium to look at.

Fountain House. 1948, West 47th Street, New York. Founded by a group of former
patients of Rockland State Hospital who had been meeting on the steps of the New
York Public Library and called themselves **WANA — We Are Not Alone**. [recall]

They bought a building with a fountain in the courtyard. The rules that came out
of it: everyone is a **member**, not a patient or a client. Membership does not
expire. The work of running the building — kitchen, clerical, the newsletter — is
done by members and staff together, and there is no separate day where the
professionals do the real work. Then **transitional employment**: the clubhouse
holds a real job at a real outside company, and places members into it, and covers
the shift if the member cannot make it, so the employer's risk is zero.

There are on the order of 300 clubhouses in 30-odd countries now. [recall] The
model is 78 years old and it is not what happens to most people who leave a
psychiatric hospital in the United States.

---

Soteria, and this one has a paper trail because it was run by the government
that then defunded it.

**Loren Mosher** was the first chief of the Center for Studies of Schizophrenia at
NIMH. In 1971 he opened **Soteria House** in San Jose: an ordinary house, six
residents at a time, young people in a first episode of psychosis, staffed largely
by **non-professionals** chosen for tolerance and ordinary presence rather than
credentials, and with antipsychotics used minimally or not at all. [recall]

The comparison was hospital treatment plus medication. Reported two-year outcomes
were comparable or better on symptoms, and better on social functioning and on
staying off medication. [recall — the Bola and Mosher reanalysis is the citation
I would want in front of me]

Funding ended around the end of the seventies. Mosher's own account is that
running a study whose result was *the drug is not always necessary* is not a
fundable position. In 1998 he resigned from the American Psychiatric Association
with a public letter that said the organisation had become the American
Psychopharmacological Association. [recall — the letter is widely reprinted]

Open Dialogue. Western Lapland, Finland. Developed from about 1984 at
Keropudas Hospital, mainly associated with **Jaakko Seikkula**. [recall]

The protocol, as stated: a meeting within **24 hours** of the first contact. The
family and the person's network are in the room. Everything about the case is
discussed **in front of the person** — no case conference behind a door, no
decision arrived at elsewhere and delivered. The same team stays with the case for
as long as it takes. Neuroleptics are not the opening move; they are used
sparingly and often not at all.

Reported five-year figures out of that region: something like 80 percent back in
work or study, a minority ever on antipsychotics, and a fall in the incidence of
new schizophrenia diagnoses in the catchment area. [recall — these are Seikkula's
published figures, from a region with a small population and no randomisation,
and both of those caveats belong on the number]

It has been running for forty years in one Finnish province.

---

Trieste. **Franco Basaglia** took over the psychiatric hospital there in 1971 and
began emptying it — not discharging people into nothing, but building
cooperatives, apartments, and work for them first. **Law 180**, May 1978, called
the Basaglia law, stopped new admissions to Italian asylums and started their
closure. It is the only national law of its kind I know of. [recall]

Basaglia died in 1980, at 56. Trieste became a WHO collaborating centre for
mental health, and delegations go there the way they go to Geel. [recall]

---

Housing First. **Sam Tsemberis**, Pathways to Housing, New York, 1992. [recall]

The standard model at the time was a staircase: get sober, comply with treatment,
demonstrate readiness, and then you may be housed. Tsemberis inverted it. An
apartment first, scattered through ordinary buildings, with no sobriety and no
treatment precondition, and services offered but not required.

Retention in the original work ran around 85 percent at five years, against far
lower numbers for the staircase. [recall] Canada then ran **At Home / Chez Soi**,
2009–2013, roughly 2,000 participants across five cities, which is the large
randomised trial. **Finland** adopted it as national policy and converted its
shelters into apartments; long-term homelessness there fell while it rose almost
everywhere else in Europe. [recall]

Cheaper than the shelter-plus-emergency-room-plus-jail cycle it replaces, in every
costing I have seen. Thirty-four years old. Not the default anywhere in the United
States.

Clozapine, because the shape of this one is different and worse.

Introduced in Europe around 1971. Withdrawn in 1975 after a cluster in Finland —
on the order of sixteen cases of agranulocytosis, eight deaths. [recall] It sat
withdrawn for over a decade.

Then the **Kane trial**, 1988: in patients who had failed everything else,
clozapine worked where the others did not. The FDA approved it in 1989 with
mandatory blood monitoring. It remains the only drug with an indication for
treatment-resistant schizophrenia, and it is the only one with evidence for
reducing suicide in that population. [recall]

And it is chronically **underprescribed** — single-digit percentages of eligible
patients in the US, against much higher rates in some other countries. [recall]
The reasons given are the monitoring burden and prescriber discomfort.

So: a drug that works, off the market for fourteen years over a risk that a blood
test manages, and then underused for thirty-six more.

The court cases, since the other stream's title asks for them.

**Olmstead v. L.C.**, 527 U.S. 581, decided June 22, 1999. **Lois Curtis** and
**Elaine Wilson**, two women in Georgia with mental illness and developmental
disabilities, kept in a state institution after the state's own clinicians said
they were ready for community placement. The Supreme Court held that unjustified
institutional isolation is discrimination under the ADA. [recall]

Lois Curtis got out and became a working artist. She died in 2022. [recall]

The holding is twenty-seven years old and the enforcement fight is still running,
state by state.

---

**The Judge Rotenberg Educational Center**, Canton, Massachusetts. It is the only
facility in the United States that uses electric skin shock as aversive treatment
on students with disabilities, delivered by a device worn on the body — the
graduated electronic decelerator. [recall]

The FDA banned such devices for that use in **March 2020**. In **July 2021** the
D.C. Circuit vacated the ban, 2–1, on the ground that the FDA may regulate a
device but may not ban a particular *use* of a legally marketed device. [recall]

I am not confident about what has happened since and am not going to guess at it
here. The two dates above are the ones I would put in front of somebody.

---

The through-line I did not expect when I started looking.

Every one of these was built by people the system did not consider the experts.
Geel is host families with no training and no diagnosis in hand. Fountain House
was founded by ex-patients who named themselves before anyone named them. Soteria
was staffed by people hired for not having been trained. Open Dialogue's move is
to stop holding the meeting without the patient in the room. Housing First's move
is to stop requiring a person to prove they deserve a door.

The Panther breakfast program is in the other stream for the same reason, and so
is Bernard Rimland, who broke the refrigerator-mother theory because he had an
autistic son and would not accept what the field told him about his own kid.

None of these is expensive. All of them are old. Every one of them has a name and
a date.
