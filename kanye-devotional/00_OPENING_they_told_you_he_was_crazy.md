# THEY TOLD YOU HE WAS CRAZY
### A book about Kanye West, and why we do this to the people who love us most

---

You already made up your mind about him. That's alright. So did everybody.

You know the story they gave you. Started out humble, made some good music, then
lost it. Grabbed the mic from that girl. Married into that family. Put on the red
hat. Said the slavery thing. Said the Jewish thing. Got dropped by everybody, lost
the billion dollars, and now he's just the crazy one, the cautionary tale, the guy
your coworker brings up to prove that fame melts your brain. Case closed. You can
feel a little sad about it and move on.

I'm going to ask you to do one thing before you close the case. I'm going to ask you
to watch the tape again.

Because here's what I think you missed, watching it the first time, the way we all
missed it: **every single time that man embarrassed himself in front of America, he
was loving something so hard it broke him in public.** And every single time, we did
the thing we always do to a person like that. We called him crazy so we wouldn't
have to hear what he said.

Watch the first one. September 2005. Hurricane Katrina. Black folks on rooftops in
New Orleans, water up to the gutters, the richest country on earth letting them
drown on live television. And they put this 28-year-old kid on a telethon to read
a card and smile. And he can't. He's shaking. He goes off the paper and he says
*I hate the way they portray us in the media — you see a Black family, it says
they're looting; you see a white family, it says they're looking for food.* And then
he says the thing that got printed on every front page: *George Bush doesn't care
about Black people.*

They said he was out of line. Unhinged. Ambushed the moment. But go find the Black
people who were watching that night and ask them what it felt like. A reporter at
NPR said it was a *release valve* — that somebody finally said the thing nobody with
a career was allowed to say. That wasn't a breakdown. That was a 28-year-old kid who
could not perform calm over his own people dying, and paid for it, and would keep
paying for saying true things out loud for the next twenty years.

That's the whole book, right there in the first scene. A man who feels too much,
about the exact things you're supposed to stay numb about, standing in a room that
needs him to stay numb, and choosing — every time, at enormous cost — to feel it out
loud instead.

We have a word for a man like that. When he lived a long time ago and it turned out
he was right, we call him a **prophet**. When he's alive right now and standing in
our living room ruining the telethon, we call him **crazy**, and we mean it as a way
to make him stop. It's the same man. It's always been the same man. The only thing
that changes is whether we're comfortable enough, yet, to admit what he was pointing
at.

So this is not a book that's going to tell you Kanye West never did any harm. He
did. Real harm, to real people, and we're going to look at all of it, straight, no
looking away, because a love story that lies about the wreckage isn't love, it's
just a nicer lie. This is a book that's going to take you back through the whole life
— the music, the mother, the money, the mania, the meltdowns, the red hat, the
prison ministry, the whole thing — and show you that the man you were told was a
villain was the most loving person most of you will ever watch, and that he showed
you his love the only way a person that open can: by breaking, in front of you,
over and over, on the things you trained yourself not to cry about.

And by the end I want you to feel the harder thing. I don't just want you to
understand him. I want you to understand *us* — why a crowd needs a person like that
to be crazy, why we reach for that word so fast, why we've been nailing up the ones
who love too loud since long before any of us were born. Because once you see how we
did it to him — in real time, with hashtags, laughing — you start to see we do it to
everybody who feels too much. Maybe somebody in your own house. Maybe you.

He gave us a whole language for it and we called it a symptom. *New slaves. Mental
prison. 250 years. Jesus is king. I hate being bipolar, it's awesome.* We'll get to
every one of those, and by the time we do, they're not going to sound crazy to you
anymore. They're going to sound like a man screaming a true thing from inside a fire,
while a crowd that could have helped him stood around filming it and calling it a
show.

Watch the tape again. This time, don't watch him.

Watch what he was pointing at.
