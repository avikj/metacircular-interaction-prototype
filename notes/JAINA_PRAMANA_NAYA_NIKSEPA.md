# Jaina epistemology: pramāṇa, naya, nikṣepa, saptabhaṅgī

**Set down 2026-08-18.** This document contains no application, no mapping to
any code in this repository, and no argument of my own. It is the doctrine in
its own order and its own vocabulary.

**Provenance, stated first because it limits everything below.** I could not
fetch any primary text: this container's egress proxy permits only package
registries, and every attempt at a text archive returned `EGRESS_BLOCKED`. Web
*search* works, so sūtra numbering and wording for 1.5, 1.6, 1.9, 1.15, 1.33,
5.29 and 5.31 and the saptabhaṅgī list are anchored against search results
(sources at the end). Everything else — the Sanskrit of the remaining sūtras,
the commentarial attributions — rests on training and is marked where I am
uncertain. **This is not a substitute for reading the Tattvārthasūtra with a
commentary.**

**Recension.** Sūtra numbers follow the Digambara recension as transmitted
with Pūjyapāda's *Sarvārthasiddhi*. The Śvetāmbara recension transmitted with
the *Tattvārthādhigamabhāṣya* numbers some sūtras differently — the naya list
in particular is 1.33 in the first and split across 1.34–1.35 in the second.
Where I know the numbers diverge I say so.

## I. Umāsvāti, *Tattvārthasūtra*, Adhyāya 1

Umāsvāti (Umāsvāmī in the Digambara transmission), c. 2nd–5th century CE. The
only text accepted as authoritative by both Śvetāmbara and Digambara.

### 1.1
> **samyagdarśanajñānacāritrāṇi mokṣamārgaḥ**
>
> Right view, right knowledge and right conduct are the path to liberation.

Three, jointly; not severally. The compound is dvandva and the predicate
singular — *mārgaḥ*, one path.

### 1.2
> **tattvārthaśraddhānaṃ samyagdarśanam**
>
> Belief in the tattvas as they are is right view.

### 1.3
> **tannisargād adhigamād vā**
>
> That [right view arises] by nature, or by acquisition.

### 1.4
> **jīvājīvāsravabandhasaṃvaranirjarāmokṣās tattvam**
>
> Soul, non-soul, influx, bondage, stoppage, shedding, and liberation are the
> tattvas.

Seven.

### 1.5 — NIKṢEPA / NYĀSA, the fourfold placing
> **nāmasthāpanādravyabhāvatas tannyāsaḥ**
>
> The placing (nyāsa) of these [tattvas] is by name, by representation, by
> substance, and by state.

The four *nikṣepas*. A term is deposited in one of four ways, and to argue
about a term without fixing which deposit is meant is to argue about nothing:

- **nāma** — the name alone. A boy called Indra is Indra by nāma-nikṣepa,
  with no further qualification.
- **sthāpanā** — installation, representation. An image or token *set up as*
  the thing: a piece of wood established as Indra is Indra by sthāpanā.
- **dravya** — the substance-deposit: that which *was* or *will be* the thing,
  taken in the mode where it is not now. One who will become Indra, or has
  ceased to be, is Indra by dravya-nikṣepa.
- **bhāva** — the state-deposit: the thing actually in the condition the word
  names, now. Indra ruling is Indra by bhāva-nikṣepa.

This sūtra precedes 1.6. The order is not incidental: the fourfold placing is
stated before the means of knowledge are.

### 1.6 — PRAMĀṆA AND NAYA
> **pramāṇanayair adhigamaḥ**
>
> [Comprehension of the tattvas is] by means of knowledge (pramāṇa) and by
> standpoints (naya).

Two instruments, named together, neither reducible to the other.

### 1.7
> **nirdeśasvāmitvasādhanādhikaraṇasthitividhānataḥ**
>
> [Also] by designation, ownership, instrument, substratum, duration, and
> classification.

Six *anuyogadvāras* — doors of disquisition.

### 1.8
> **satsaṃkhyākṣetrasparśanakālāntarabhāvālpabahutvaiś ca**
>
> And by existence, number, field, occupation, time, interval, state, and
> relative magnitude.

Eight more.

### 1.9 — THE FIVE KNOWLEDGES
> **matiśrutāvadhimanaḥparyayakevalāni jñānam**
>
> Sensory, scriptural, clairvoyant, mind-reading, and omniscient — [these
> five] are knowledge.

### 1.10
> **tat pramāṇe**
>
> Those are the two pramāṇas.

The five knowledges are distributed into two means of knowing.

### 1.11
> **ādye parokṣam**
>
> The first two are indirect.

### 1.12
> **pratyakṣam anyat**
>
> The rest are direct.

**This is the inversion that matters and that is routinely flattened.** For
the Jainas, *mati* — knowledge through the senses and the mind — is
**parokṣa**, *indirect*, precisely because it is mediated: the senses and the
mind stand between the soul and the object. *Pratyakṣa*, direct, means what
the soul knows *without* an instrument — avadhi, manaḥparyaya, kevala. This is
the opposite assignment from the Nyāya and Buddhist schemes, where pratyakṣa
is sense-perception. The Jaina position is that sense-perception is the
paradigm of *mediated* knowing.

### 1.13
> **matiḥ smṛtiḥ saṃjñā cintābhinibodha ityanarthāntaram**
>
> Sensory knowledge, memory, recognition, induction, and inference — these are
> not different in meaning.

All five are *mati*.

### 1.14
> **tadindriyānindriyanimittam**
>
> That [mati] is caused by the senses and the non-sense (the mind).

### 1.15 — THE FOUR STAGES OF MATI
> **avagrahehāvāyadhāraṇāḥ**
>
> Apprehension, speculation, judgement, retention.

- **avagraha** — the first grasping: *that there is something*.
- **īhā** — the striving toward specification: *is it this or that?*
- **avāya** — the determination: *it is this*.
- **dhāraṇā** — the holding, such that it can be recalled.

The classical illustration is the rope and the snake: something is grasped
(avagraha); one strives, is it rope or snake (īhā); it is settled as rope
(avāya); it is retained (dhāraṇā). **Cognition is four-membered and
successive**, not a single act.

### 1.16
> **bahubahuvidhakṣiprāniḥsṛtānuktadhruvāṇāṃ setarāṇām**
>
> [Each of the four operates] on the many, the many-sorted, the quick, the
> unexpressed, the unuttered, the lasting — and their opposites.

Twelve objects.

### 1.17
> **arthasya**
>
> [The four stages, of] the object.

### 1.18
> **vyañjanasyāvagrahaḥ**
>
> Of the indistinct, [there is] only apprehension.

### 1.19
> **na cakṣuranindriyābhyām**
>
> Not by the eye and the mind.

The eye and the mind do not take the indistinct (vyañjana) — they do not
contact their object. This is why the counting of mati's varieties comes out
as it does (the traditional total is 336, obtained by multiplying the stages,
objects and instruments with these exclusions).

### 1.20
> **śrutaṃ matipūrvaṃ dvyanekadvādaśabhedam**
>
> Scriptural knowledge is preceded by sensory knowledge, and is of two kinds,
> of many kinds, of twelve kinds.

*Śruta* presupposes *mati*. Nothing articulate without something apprehended
first.

*(Sūtras 1.21–1.32 treat avadhi, manaḥparyaya, kevala, their ranges, and the
possibility of erroneous knowledge — mati, śruta and avadhi can be
mithyā/viparyaya, the last two cannot. I do not set them out here because I
am not confident of their exact wording.)*

### 1.33 — THE SEVEN NAYAS
> **naigamasaṃgrahavyavahārarjusūtraśabdasamabhirūḍhaivaṃbhūtā nayāḥ**
>
> The figurative, the generic, the practical, the straight, the verbal, the
> etymological, and the actualized — [these are] the standpoints.

*(Śvetāmbara recension: split across 1.34–1.35.)*

1. **naigama** — the figurative. Takes the purpose, or the not-yet-distinguished
   whole. A man carrying wood and water, asked what he is doing, says "I am
   cooking rice." He is not; he is fetching. The naigama takes the intention
   as the thing.
2. **saṃgraha** — the generic. Takes the class without its divisions: "the
   existent" (sat), with everything gathered under it.
3. **vyavahāra** — the practical. Divides what saṃgraha gathered, down to what
   can actually be used and transacted with.
4. **ṛjusūtra** — the straight. Takes only the *present* mode, discarding past
   and future. A thing is what it is now.
5. **śabda** — the verbal. Distinguishes by grammatical form: tense, number,
   gender, case. Words differing in these do not denote one thing.
6. **samabhirūḍha** — the etymological. Distinguishes synonyms by their roots:
   *indra* (ruling), *śakra* (being able), *purandara* (destroying strongholds)
   are three different things, because the roots differ.
7. **evaṃbhūta** — the actualized, "so-become". The word applies **only when
   the thing is actually performing the function the word names.** One is
   *indra* only while ruling; a *gopāla* only while herding cows; a pot is a
   *ghaṭa* only while performing the action of holding.

The first three (with naigama) are *arthanaya* — standpoints on the object;
the last three are *śabdanaya* — standpoints on the word. The three
substrata classically named are *upacāra* (convention), *artha* (meaning),
*śabda* (word).

**The ordering is a progression from coarse to fine**, each later naya
admitting fewer things as the same thing.

## II. Naya and pramāṇa: the difference

The formula transmitted in the commentaries:

> **Naya comprehends one aspect of the object; pramāṇa comprehends the object
> in its fullness.**

Mallisena (*Syādvādamañjarī*, 1292 CE) states this as:

- **sakalādeśa** — the total statement, which is *pramāṇa*.
- **vikalādeśa** — the partial statement, which is *naya*.

A naya is not an error. It is a partial and correct statement. It becomes an
error only in one way, and Siddhasena states the condition:

**Siddhasena Divākara**, *Sanmatitarka* (Prakrit *Sammai-suttaṃ*), c. 5th c.:

> A naya taken **nirapekṣa** — without regard to the others — is *mithyā*.
> Taken **sāpekṣa** — with regard to the others — it is *samyak*.

Hence **durnaya**: not a false standpoint, but a standpoint that has denied
the other standpoints. What makes it *dur-* is the denial, not the partiality.

And *Sanmatitarka* 1.28:

> **jāvaiyā vayaṇapahā tāvaiyā ceva hoṃti ṇayavāyā**
>
> As many as are the ways of speaking, so many are the naya-statements.

The seven of 1.33 are a classification, not an enumeration of all there are.

## III. Saptabhaṅgī — the sevenfold predication

Applied to any predication about any object, with the particle **syāt** —
"in some respect", "from a standpoint" — prefixed to each.

**The standard seven, in the order given by Samantabhadra (*Āptamīmāṃsā*,
c. 6th c.) and Akalaṅka (c. 720–780):**

1. **syād asti** — in some respect, it is.
2. **syād nāsti** — in some respect, it is not.
3. **syād asti nāsti ca** — in some respect, it is and it is not.
4. **syād avaktavyam** — in some respect, it is inexpressible.
5. **syād asti ca avaktavyaṃ ca** — in some respect it is, and is inexpressible.
6. **syād nāsti ca avaktavyaṃ ca** — in some respect it is not, and is inexpressible.
7. **syād asti nāsti ca avaktavyaṃ ca** — in some respect it is, is not, and is inexpressible.

**A discrepancy in the sources, reported rather than resolved.** A search
result I consulted lists *syād avaktavyaḥ* as the **seventh** and shifts the
combinations up. Both orderings circulate. The Digambara sequence as I have it
from Samantabhadra and Akalaṅka puts avaktavya **fourth**, immediately after
the third, because of the reason given below. I do not know which ordering the
particular source I found is following, and I am not going to adjudicate it.

**Why exactly seven** — Akalaṅka's argument: three primary (*mūla*)
predicates — asti, nāsti, avaktavya — and their combinations: 3 singly, 3 in
pairs, 1 all together. 3 + 3 + 1 = 7.

**The distinction between the third and the fourth**, which is the whole of
avaktavya. Both are built from asti and nāsti. They differ in the *mode of
assertion*:

- **krama** — successive. Asserted one after the other: this yields the third
  bhaṅga, *syād asti nāsti ca*. Two utterances in succession.
- **yugapat** — simultaneous. Asserted at once: this yields the fourth,
  *syād avaktavyam*.

*Avaktavya* is therefore **not** "unknown", **not** "undefined", **not**
"neither". It is a positive predication: that the object's joint aspect,
taken simultaneously, **outruns the one-aspect-at-a-time medium in which
predication happens.** Akalaṅka's terms for the two modes are *kramārpaṇa*
and *sahārpaṇa*.

**What asti and nāsti are relative to** — the classical fourfold:

- **sva-dravya / para-dravya** — own substance / other substance
- **sva-kṣetra / para-kṣetra** — own field / other field
- **sva-kāla / para-kāla** — own time / other time
- **sva-bhāva / para-bhāva** — own state / other state

A pot **is**, with respect to its own substance, place, time and state; and
**is not**, with respect to another's. *Nāsti* is never absolute negation. It
is negation relative to a stated fourfold ground. This is why the first two
bhaṅgas are not contradictory.

## IV. Anekāntavāda and the metaphysics it rests on

### Tattvārthasūtra 5.29
> **utpādavyayadhrauvyayuktaṃ sat**
>
> That which is, is endowed with origination, cessation, and persistence.

All three, of the same thing, at once. Not in succession.

### 5.30
> **tadbhāvāvyayaṃ nityam**
>
> The permanent is that which does not abandon its own nature.

### 5.31
> **arpitānarpitasiddheḥ**
>
> [Contradictory attributes are established] through the distinction of the
> asserted (arpita) and the unasserted (anarpita).

*(Numbered 5.31 in the Digambara recension, 5.32 in some Śvetāmbara
printings.)*

This sūtra is the standpoint-index stated as doctrine. Every predication
carries an aspect asserted and the rest left unasserted; two predications that
appear to contradict do not, if their asserted aspects differ.

**The older formulation of 5.29 is the *tri-padī*** — the three-fold utterance
of the *Bhagavatī Sūtra* (*Viyāha-pannatti*), fifth Aṅga of the Śvetāmbara
canon, whose oldest strata are pre-Common-Era:

> **uppannei vā, vigamei vā, dhuvei vā**
>
> It arises, it perishes, it endures.

Prakrit, and earlier than the Sanskrit sūtra.

## V. Where these come from

- **Bhagavatī Sūtra** (Viyāha-pannatti / Vyākhyāprajñapti) — fifth Aṅga,
  Śvetāmbara canon. Oldest strata pre-Common-Era; redacted at the Valabhī
  council, c. 5th c. CE. Contains the tri-padī and applies a sevenfold
  predication to the jīva.
- **Umāsvāti / Umāsvāmī**, *Tattvārthasūtra*, c. 2nd–5th c. CE.
- **Pūjyapāda**, *Sarvārthasiddhi*, c. 5th–6th c. — the Digambara commentary
  whose recension the numbering above follows.
- **Siddhasena Divākara**, *Sanmatitarka*, c. 5th c. — nirapekṣa/sāpekṣa,
  durnaya, and "as many nayas as ways of speaking".
- **Samantabhadra**, *Āptamīmāṃsā*, c. 6th c. — saptabhaṅgī as a fixed
  seven-membered scheme, each member prefixed *syāt*.
- **Akalaṅka**, *Laghīyastraya* / *Aṣṭaśatī*, c. 720–780 — the argument that
  the number is exactly seven; kramārpaṇa and sahārpaṇa.
- **Mallisena**, *Syādvādamañjarī*, 1292 — sakalādeśa and vikalādeśa.

Mathematical texts of the same tradition, named because they are not separate
from it: *Anuyogadvāra*, *Sthānāṅga*, *Bhagavatī* — combinatorics, the laws of
indices, and the taxonomy of the unbounded (*saṃkhyāta*, *asaṃkhyāta*,
*ananta*, each with orders), arising from cosmology and karma theory.
