# पाटीगणित — the *Līlāvatī* calls itself board-work, every sum in it comes out, and the quadrilateral is refused an area

**Bhāskara II, *Līlāvatī* and *Bījagaṇita*, parts one and two of the
*Siddhāntaśiromaṇi*, Śaka 1072 = 1150 CE, read in T. Hayashi's Sanskrit e-texts
(entered 20 April 1993 and June 1993, TEI conversion 2020-07-31) collated from
V. G. Apaṭe's *Līlāvatī* with Gaṇeśa's *Buddhivilāsinī* and Mahīdhara's
*Līlāvatīvivaraṇa*, Ānandāśrama Sanskrit Series 107, Poona 1937; K. V. Sarma's
*Līlāvatī* with Śaṅkara's and Nārāyaṇa's *Kriyākramakarī*, Vishveshvaranand
Indological Series 66, Hoshiarpur 1975; and Apaṭe's *Bījagaṇita*, ĀSS 99, Poona
1930 — against H. T. Colebrooke's *Algebra, with Arithmetic and Mensuration,
from the Sanscrit of Brahmegupta and Bháscara*, John Murray, London, 1817.
Verse numbers below are Apaṭe's, which the e-text follows; Colebrooke's differ.**

---

## Every number in this chapter has been recomputed

Two hundred and seventy-two verses. Between them the prose carries the word
*nyāsaḥ* — "the setting-down" — **158 times**, and each *nyāsa* prints the data
of a worked example and its answer in figures.

**Roughly sixty of those answers were recomputed here from the data in the
verses — every one reported below, and a scan across all thirteen
*vyavahāra*s. Three did not come out.** The three are reported in place, with
what the arithmetic says the reading should be; all three are single wrong
digits, and in each case the other numbers standing in the same *nyāsa* convict
them. I did not check the remaining hundred.

## The dates

**Golādhyāya, Praśnādhyāya 58**, the author's own:

> rasa-guṇa-pūrṇa-mahī-sama-śaka-nṛpa-samaye 'bhavan mama utpattiḥ |
> rasa-guṇa-varṣeṇa mayā siddhānta-śiromaṇī racitaḥ ||

*rasa* 6, *guṇa* 3, *pūrṇa* 0, *mahī* 1; read right to left, **Śaka 1036 = 1114
CE** for the birth, and composed in the *rasa-guṇa* year, thirty-six.
Śaka 1036 + 36 = **Śaka 1072 = 1150 CE**. *(I have not opened the Golādhyāya. I
take the verse and its numerals from secondary report and mark it as such; what
I have opened is Colebrooke's statement of its content, next.)*

Colebrooke, 1817, Dissertation p. iii and Notes p. xxxiv:

> "He completed his great work, the *Sidd'hānta-śiromaṇi*, as he himself informs
> us in a passage of it, in the year 1072 Śaca. … it was completed by him, being
> thirty-six years of age; and that his birth was in 1036 Śaca."

and the corroboration he offers is a second work with its own epoch: the
*Karaṇakutūhala*, "a practical astronomical treatise, the epoch of which is 1105
Śaca; 33 years subsequent to the completion of the systematic treatise."
Two dates, thirty-three years apart, from two of the author's own books.

## The book names its own genre in its first verse, and the name is a surface

**Līlāvatī 1**, *śārdūlavikrīḍita*:

> prītiṃ bhakta-janasya yo janayate vighnaṃ vinighnan smṛtas
> taṃ vṛndāraka-vṛnda-vandita-padaṃ natvā mataṅga-ānanam |
> **pāṭīṃ sad-gaṇitasya vacmi** catura-prīti-pradāṃ prasphuṭāṃ
> saṃkṣipta-akṣara-komala-amala-padair **lālitya-līlāvatīm** ||

Having bowed to the elephant-faced one — *mataṅgānana* — I declare the **pāṭī**
of good calculation, giving delight to the clever, plain, in soft and spotless
words of few syllables, *lālitya-līlāvatī*: charming and playful.

The colophon at the other end, **272**, closes the ring:

> iti śrī-bhāskara-ācārya-viracite siddhānta-śiromaṇau **līlāvatī-saṃjñaḥ
> pāṭī-adhyāyaḥ** saṃpūrṇaḥ

— the **pāṭī chapter, named Līlāvatī**, of the *Siddhāntaśiromaṇi*. The book's
own designation of what it is, in its first line and its last, is *pāṭī*: the
arithmetic done on the board. *Līlāvatī* is what the *pāṭī* is called. (The
derivation of *pāṭī* from the writing-board is the usual one and I have not
verified it from a source; that the word is Bhāskara's own name for the genre,
in both the opening verse and the colophon, is on the page.)

The closing verse, **272**, is built on the double sense of three technical
words:

> yeṣāṃ su-**jāti**-**guṇa**-**varga**-vibhūṣita-aṅgī
> śuddhā akhila-vyavahṛtiḥ khalu kaṇṭha-saktā |
> līlāvatī iha sa-rasa-uktim udāharantī
> teṣāṃ sadā eva sukha-saṃpad upaiti vṛddhim ||

*jāti* is the reduction of fractions to a common form and it is birth; *guṇa* is
multiplication and it is virtue; *varga* is the square and it is a class;
*vyavahṛti* is a chapter-heading of practical arithmetic and it is conduct;
*kaṇṭha-sakta* is "clasped to the throat" and "learnt by heart." The verse reads
in one direction as a woman and in the other as the book.

## The units, and the money comes out to the last cowrie

**Verses 2–8** fix the measures before anything else happens. In cowries:

| | |
|---|---|
| 20 *varāṭaka* (cowrie) | 1 *kākiṇī* |
| 4 *kākiṇī* | 1 *paṇa* |
| 16 *paṇa* | 1 *dramma* |
| 16 *dramma* | 1 *niṣka* |

so **1 niṣka = 16 × 16 × 4 × 20 = 20,480 cowries**, and every money answer in
the book is a base-20/4/16/16 expansion of a fraction. Three of them, recomputed:

**75.** If 63 *pala* of camphor cost 104 *niṣka*, what do 12¼ *pala* cost?
104 × (49/4) / 63 = 5096/252 = 20 + 2/9 niṣka. The text: *niṣkāḥ 20, drammāḥ 3,
paṇāḥ 8, kākiṇyaḥ 3, varāṭakāḥ 11, varāṭaka-bhāgaḥ 1/9.* Recomputed: 2/9 of
20,480 = 4551 + 1/9 cowries; 3 dramma = 3840, 8 paṇa = 640, 3 kākiṇī = 60, plus
11 + 1/9 — **4551 + 1/9. Exact.**

**76.** 2 *dramma* buy 1⅛ *khārikā* of husked rice; what do 70 *paṇa* buy? 2
dramma = 32 paṇa; (9/8)(70/32) = 2.4609375 khārī. The text: *khāryau 2, droṇāḥ
7, āḍhakaḥ 1, prasthau 2.* With 16 droṇa to the khārī, 4 āḍhaka to the droṇa, 4
prastha to the āḍhaka: 0.4609375 × 16 = 7.375 droṇa = 7 droṇa, 1 āḍhaka, 2
prastha. **Exact.**

**85**, the seven-quantity rule: eight cloths 3 wide and 8 long fetch 100; what
does one 3½ long and ½ wide fetch? 100 × 1.75/192 = 175/192 niṣka = 18,666⅔
cowries. The text: *niṣkāḥ 0, drammāḥ 14, paṇāḥ 9, kākiṇī 1, varāṭakāḥ 6,
varāṭaka-bhāgau 2/3* = 17,920 + 720 + 20 + 6 + ⅔ = **18,666⅔. Exact.**

**86** and **87** run the same machinery at nine and eleven quantities — cloths
by thickness × width × length × count, then the same with a cartage distance
appended — and both come out (16⅔ niṣka; 8 dramma).

## The problems, and what the vocative is doing

**50**, the *viloma-kriyā*, the reverse process. A number, tripled, increased by
three quarters of itself, divided by seven, diminished by its own third, squared,
diminished by fifty-two, its root taken, increased by eight, divided by ten,
gives **two**. *Vetsi hi cañcala-akṣi vimalāṃ bāle viloma-kriyām* — if you know,
restless-eyed girl, the clear reverse process. The *nyāsa* lists the operations
in order — *guṇaḥ* 3, *kṣepaḥ* ¾, *bhājakaḥ* 7, *ṛṇam* ⅓, *vargaḥ*, *ṛṇam* 52,
*mūlam*, *kṣepaḥ* 8, *haraḥ* 10, *dṛśyam* 2 — and answers **28**. The additive ¾
is *tribhiḥ sva-caraṇaiḥ*, three of its own quarters, so the step is ×7/4, not
+¼. Recomputed forwards: 28 × 3 = 84; × 7/4 = 147; ÷ 7 = 21; × ⅔ = 14; squared,
196; − 52 = 144; root, 12; + 8 = 20; ÷ 10 = **2**. Exact. Every one of the ten
steps lands on an integer.

**54**, the pilgrim. Half at Prayāga, two ninths of the remainder at Kāśī, a
quarter of that remainder as toll, six tenths of that at Gayā, **63 niṣka** home.
The text prints the compounded fraction **7/60** and the answer **540**.
Recomputed: ½ · 7/9 · ¾ · 4/10 = 7/60; 63 × 60/7 = **540**. Exact.

**55**, the bees. A fifth of the swarm to the *kadamba*, a third to the
*śilīndhra*, three times the difference of those two to the *kuṭaja*, and one bee
left, called this way and that between the *ketakī* and the *mālatī*. Answer
**15**. Recomputed: $\frac{x}{5}+\frac{x}{3}+3\left(\frac{x}{3}-\frac{x}{5}\right)
= \frac{14x}{15}$; the remaining fifteenth is the one bee. Exact.

**70**, Arjuna. *pārthaḥ karṇa-vadhāya mārgaṇa-gaṇaṃ kruddho raṇe saṃdadhe* —
with half his arrows he warded off Karṇa's arrows; with four times the square
root of the whole, the horses; with six, Śalya; with three, the parasol, the
flag, the bow; with one, the head. **100.** Recomputed: $x = \tfrac{x}{2} +
4\sqrt{x} + 10$ gives $\sqrt{x} = 10$. Exact.

**71**, the bee again, and the sign goes the other way. The square root of **half**
the swarm went to the *mālatī*; eight ninths of the **whole** elsewhere; one bee
was shut in a lotus at night, calling, and one answered outside. The *nyāsa*
reads *mūla-guṇakaḥ −½, bhāgaḥ −8/9*, and solves for the half: $\sqrt{y} +
\tfrac{16y}{9} + 2 = 2y$ at $y = 36$, giving **72**. Recomputed: 6 + 64 + 2 = 72.
Exact — and the "visible" two is two bees, not one.

**101–102**, the four jewel merchants. Eight rubies, ten sapphires, a hundred
pearls, five diamonds; each gives one of his own stones to each of the other
three, out of *saṅga-sneha*, and all four end **equal in wealth**. Find the
prices.

The rule: subtract from each count the number of men times the gift — 8 − 4 = 4,
10 − 4 = 6, 100 − 4 = 96, 5 − 4 = 1 — and the prices are inversely as those
remainders. The text takes 96 as the convenient numerator: prices **24, 16, 1,
96**, and *sama-dhanam* **233**. Recomputed: each man keeps his count less three
and gains one of each of the others —

- 5 × 24 + 16 + 1 + 96 = **233**
- 7 × 16 + 24 + 1 + 96 = **233**
- 97 × 1 + 24 + 16 + 96 = **233**
- 2 × 96 + 24 + 16 + 1 = **233**

The text then gives the second, fraction-free route: the price of each is the
product of the *other three* remainders — 6·96·1 = 576, 4·96·1 = 384, 4·6·1 = 24,
4·6·96 = 2304 — and the common wealth **5592**. Recomputed: 5 × 576 + 384 + 24 +
2304 = 5592 ✓, and all four agree. **The e-text prints the first of those four
prices as 596. It is 576**; 5592 and the other three prices require it.

**A third slip, same kind.** At **228–231**, heaps of grain. Circumference 60,
depth = circumference/11 for fine grain, so the volume is $(60/6)^2 \times 60/11
= 6000/11 = 545\tfrac{5}{11}$ *khārī*, as printed. Split among a heap against a
wall (half), in an inner corner (quarter) and on an outer corner (three
quarters), the text prints **272 8/11, 136 4/1, 409 1/11**. The middle
denominator is 11: $\tfrac14 \times 6000/11 = 1500/11 = 136\tfrac{4}{11}$.

## Zero, twice, in two different books

**Līlāvatī 45–46**, two *āryā*:

> yoge khaṃ kṣepa-samaṃ varga-ādau khaṃ kha-bhājito rāśiḥ |
> kha-haraḥ syāt kha-guṇaḥ khaṃ kha-guṇaś cintyaś ca śeṣa-vidhau ||
> śūnye guṇake jāte khaṃ hāraś cet punas tadā rāśiḥ |
> avikṛta eva jñeyas tathā eva khena ūnitaś ca yutaḥ ||

In addition, *kha* leaves the addend as it is. In squaring and rooting, *kha*.
A quantity divided by *kha* is a **khahara** — a "having-*kha*-for-divisor."
Multiplied by *kha* it is *kha*, but as a *kha*-multiple it is **to be kept in
mind if a further operation impends**. And: when the multiplier has become zero
and the divisor afterwards becomes zero, the quantity is to be known as
**unaltered**.

**47** is the test of that last clause:

> khaṃ pañca-yug bhavati kiṃ vada khasya vargaṃ
> mūlaṃ ghanaṃ ghana-padaṃ kha-guṇāś ca pañca |
> khena uddhṛtā daśa ca kaḥ **kha-guṇo nija-ardha-yuktas
> tribhiś ca guṇitaḥ sva-hataḥ triṣaṣṭiḥ** ||

The last clause: what number, multiplied by zero, increased by half of itself,
multiplied by three, divided by zero, is **sixty-three**? The *nyāsa* answers
**14**. Recomputed under the rule of 46 — the zero multiplier and the zero
divisor annihilate each other and what survives is the intervening arithmetic:
(x + x/2) × 3 = 9x/2 = 63, **x = 14.** The rule is not decoration; the problem
cannot be solved without it, and the prose adds *asya gaṇitasya graha-gaṇite
mahān upayogaḥ* — this calculation is of great use in planetary computation.

Ten divided by zero the text sets down, in the same *nyāsa*, as **10/0**.

The other statement is in the other book. **Bījagaṇita 20**, *upajāti*:

> asmin vikāraḥ kha-hare **na** rāśāv api praviṣṭeṣv api niḥsṛteṣu |
> bahuṣv api syāl laya-sṛṣṭi-kāle 'nante 'cyute bhūta-gaṇeṣu yadvat ||

Colebrooke, *Vīja-gaṇita* §16:

> "In this quantity consisting of that which has cipher for its divisor, there is
> no alteration, though many be inserted or extracted; as no change takes place
> in the infinite and immutable God, at the period of the destruction or creation
> of worlds, though numerous orders of beings are absorbed or put forth."

**A textual note that the arithmetic decides.** The GRETIL segmentation of this
verse reads *kha-hareṇa rāśau* — instrumental — which dissolves the negative
particle *na* into the sandhi and turns "there is **no** alteration in this
khahara quantity" into "there is alteration by the khahara." Colebrooke's
translation, Kṛṣṇa Daivajña's commentary on it, and the sense of the simile all
require the locative plus *na*: *khahare na rāśau*. The word-splitter lost a
negation.

Kṛṣṇa Daivajña's illustration of the *khahara*, reported by Colebrooke at the
same place, is the gnomon: the shadow at sunrise and sunset is infinite, and is
equally so whatever height is given to the gnomon and whatever number is taken
for radius, so that with radius 3438 and gnomon 12, or radius 120, 100, 90, the
expressions 12·3438/0, 12·120/0, 12·100/0, 12·90/0 "are all alike infinite."

## The series, and a number that is 2³¹ − 2

**Verses 117–134**, the *śreḍhī-vyavahāra*.

**117** gives $\sum_{1}^{n} k = n(n+1)/2$ and the sum of those sums,
$\times (n+2)/3$. **119** gives the sum of squares as
$\frac{2n+1}{3}\cdot\frac{n(n+1)}{2}$ — *ku*, earth, is the 1 in "*dvi-ghna-padaṃ
ku-yutaṃ tri-vibhaktaṃ saṃkalitena hatam*" — and the sum of cubes as **the
square of the sum**: *saṃkalitasya kṛteḥ samam eka-ādi-aṅka-ghana-aikyam*. The
tables at **118** and **120** run to n = 9 and are correct: 45, 165, 285, 2025.

**131.** A man gives two cowries on the first day and doubles daily for a month.
How many *niṣka*?

*nyāsaḥ: ādiḥ 2, cayo guṇaḥ 2, gacchaḥ 30, labdhā varāṭakāḥ* **2147483646**.

$2(2^{30}-1) = 2{,}147{,}483{,}646 = 2^{31}-2$. Then the text converts it:
*niṣkāḥ 104857, drammāḥ 9, paṇāḥ 9, kākiṇyau 2, varāṭakāḥ 6.* Recomputed against
20,480: quotient 104,857, remainder 12,286; 12,286 = 9 × 1280 + 766; 766 = 9 ×
80 + 46; 46 = 2 × 20 + 6. **Every digit.**

**133–134** counts metres. In a metre of *n* syllables to the *pāda*, the number
of *sama* (all four *pāda*s alike) forms is $2^n$; of *ardhasama*,
$2^{2n}-2^n$; of *viṣama*, $2^{4n}-2^{2n}$. For *anuṣṭubh*, n = 8:

- *sama* 256 = 2⁸
- *ardhasama* 65,280 = 2¹⁶ − 2⁸
- *viṣama* 4,294,901,760 = 2³² − 2¹⁶

all three as printed. This is Piṅgala's enumeration (*Chandaḥśāstra*, ~300 BCE)
arriving as a worked example inside an arithmetic.

**The first of the three slips.** **122**: first day 4 *dramma*,
daily increase 5, a fortnight of 15 days. *nyāsaḥ: ā 4, ca 5, ga 15, madhya-dhanam
39, antya-dhanam 74, sarva-dhanam* **575**. Last term 4 + 14×5 = 74 ✓; middle
(4+74)/2 = 39 ✓; total 39 × 15 = **585**, not 575. Colebrooke's §123 prints
"First term 4. Middle term 39. Last term 74. **Sum 585.**" The e-text's 575 is a
slip, and the two terms it stands beside are the ones that convict it.

## Combinatorics arrives twice, from two different practical questions

**Verses 112–116**, inside the *miśraka* chapter, on mixtures.

**112–113** give the binomials by the multiplicative recurrence: write
$n, n-1, n-2, \dots$ over $1, 2, 3, \dots$; each next term is the previous one
multiplied by the upper and divided by the lower — $\binom{n}{k} =
\binom{n}{k-1}\frac{n-k+1}{k}$.

**114** names where else this is used, and the list is the load-bearing verse:

> mūṣā-vahana-bheda-ādau **khaṇḍa-merau** ca śilpake |
> vaidyake rasa-bhedīye tan na uktaṃ vistṛter bhayāt ||

varieties of crucible-carriage, the **khaṇḍa-meru**, architecture, medicine, and
the division of flavours — "it is not stated here for fear of prolixity."
The *meru-prastāra* is the array of Piṅgala's *Chandaḥśāstra* 8, worked out in
Halāyudha's *Mṛtasañjīvanī* (10th c.). **Whether Bhāskara's *khaṇḍa-meru* — the
"broken" or "sectioned" meru — is that array or a variant of it I have not
established**, and no commentary I read here says.

**115.** How many forms has a *pāda* of the *gāyatrī*? Six syllables, so
6, 15, 20, 15, 6, 1, plus the all-short one: **64 = 2⁶**. And the whole
four-*pāda* metre: **16,777,216** — recomputed, $64^4 = 2^{24}$, as printed.

**116** asks two questions in one verse: eight crucibles carried one, two,
three… at a time — 8, 28, 56, 70, 56, 28, 8, 1, summing to **255 = 2⁸ − 1** — and
the combinations of six tastes, sweet, pungent, astringent, sour, saline,
bitter: 6, 15, 20, 15, 6, 1, summing to **63 = 2⁶ − 1**. Both as printed.

**Then the *aṅkapāśa*, verses 261–271, "the net of digits."**

**261** states the count and, in the same breath, the *sum*: the arrangements of
n given digits number $n!$; and the sum of all the numbers so formed is
$\frac{n! \times (\text{digit sum})}{n}$ multiplied by the repunit of n places.

**262.** With digits 2 and 8: 2 arrangements, sum 110 (28 + 82). With 3, 9, 8:
6 arrangements, sum 4440. With **2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, 8, 9**: 8! = **40,320**
arrangements, and the sum is printed in words as *caturviṃśati-nikharvāṇi
triṣaṣṭi-padmāni navanavati-koṭayaḥ navanavati-lakṣāṇi pañcasaptati-sahasrāṇi
śata-trayaṃ ṣaṣṭiś ca* — and in figures as **2463999975360**.

Recomputed: $\frac{40320 \times 44}{8} = 221{,}760$; $221{,}760 \times 11111111
= \frac{221760}{9}\times 99999999 = 24640 \times 99999999 =
\mathbf{2{,}463{,}999{,}975{,}360}$. **Exact, to the last of thirteen digits.**

**263** applies it:

> pāśa-aṅkuśa-ahi-ḍamarūka-kapāla-śūlaiḥ
> khaṭvā-aṅga-śakti-śara-cāpa-yutair bhavanti |
> anyonya-hasta-kalitaiḥ kati mūrti-bhedāḥ
> śaṃbhoḥ harer iva gadā-ari-saroja-śaṅkhaiḥ ||

Noose, goad, serpent, drum, skull, trident, club-staff, spear, arrow, bow, moved
between the hands: how many forms of Śaṃbhu? And of Hari with mace, discus,
lotus, conch? *jātā mūrti-bhedāḥ* **3628800** — 10! — *evaṃ hareś ca* **24** = 4!.

**264–265**, repeated digits: divide by the factorials of the repetitions. Digits
2, 2, 1, 1 give $4!/(2!\,2!) = 6$ and the text prints all six —
2211, 2121, 2112, 1212, 1221, 1122 — and their sum, **9999**, which recomputes
(6 × 6/4 × 1111). Digits 4, 8, 5, 5, 5 give $5!/3! = 20$, all twenty printed, sum
**1199988** = 20 × 27/5 × 11111. Both exact.

**266–267**, distinct nonzero digits over six places: 9·8·7·6·5·4 = **60,480**.

**268–270** is the one with the condition. How many n-place numbers have a given
digit sum S? The rule: take S − 1, and form
$\frac{S-1}{1}\cdot\frac{S-2}{2}\cdots$ through n − 1 factors — that is
$\binom{S-1}{n-1}$. The example: five places, digit sum 13, gives
$\frac{12}{1}\cdot\frac{11}{2}\cdot\frac{10}{3}\cdot\frac{9}{4} =
\binom{12}{4} = \mathbf{495}$.

And **269** states the range of validity, in one line:

> **nava-anvita-sthānaka-saṃkhyāyāḥ ūne aṅka-yoge** kathitaṃ tu vedyam |

what has been said is to be understood when the digit sum is **less than the
number of places increased by nine**.

That bound is exact. $\binom{S-1}{n-1}$ counts compositions of S into n parts each
≥ 1; a part can reach 10 exactly when $S - (n-1) \ge 10$, i.e. when $S \ge n+9$.
The rule is valid iff $S < n + 9$. Here n = 5, S = 13 < 14. The verse states the
condition under which its own formula holds, and the condition is the sharp one.

**269** continues: *saṃkṣiptam uktaṃ pṛthutā-bhayena na anto 'sti yasmād
gaṇita-arṇavasya* — briefly stated, for fear of extent, since there is no end to
the ocean of calculation. **271** closes the chapter:

> na guṇo na haro na kṛtir na ghanaḥ pṛṣṭas tathāpi duṣṭānām |
> garvita-gaṇaka-baṭūnāṃ syāt pātaḥ **avaśyam** aṅka-pāśe 'smin ||

No multiplication, no division, no square, no cube has been asked; and still the
conceited calculating-boys will certainly fall in this net of digits.

## The quadrilateral is refused an area, and the refusal is the content

**165–168**, the triangle. Given base and two sides, the two *ābādhā* (the
segments of the base cut off by the perpendicular) are
$\frac{1}{2}\left(b \pm \frac{(c_1+c_2)(c_1-c_2)}{b}\right)$, the *lamba* is
$\sqrt{c^2 - \bar{a}^2}$, the area is half base times perpendicular.

**167.** base 14, sides 13 and 15: *ābādhe* 5 and 9, *lamba* 12, area **84**.
Recomputed: 13² − 5² = 15² − 9² = 144 ✓, 14 × 12/2 = 84 ✓.

**168** is the case where the rule produces something impossible and the text
takes the sign seriously. Sides 10 and 17, base 9. The rule gives
(10+17)(17−10)/9 = 21, and *anena bhūr ūnā na syāt* — the base cannot be
diminished by this. So the base is subtracted from **it**, and half the remainder
is *ṛṇa-gatā ābādhā*, a **negative** segment, *diś-vaiparītyena* — by reversal of
direction. The *ābādhā* come out 6 and 15; recomputed, $10^2-6^2 = 17^2-15^2 =
64$, *lamba* 8 both ways, area 9 × 8/2 = **36**, as printed. The foot of the
perpendicular falls outside the base and the arithmetic says so by changing sign.

**169** gives the four-sided "gross" area
$\sqrt{(s-a)(s-b)(s-c)(s-d)}$ — Brahmagupta's, *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* 12 (628); I have not
established the verse number and do not give one — and calls it *asphuṭa*,
unclear. **170** instances it: base 14, face 9,
sides 13 and 12, perpendicular 12. Recomputed, s = 24, product 10·15·11·12 =
**19,800**, and the text says the root is *kiṃcid-nyūnam ekacatvāriṃśacchatam*,
somewhat less than 141 — $\sqrt{19800} = 140.712$. Then: *idam atra kṣetre na
vāstavaṃ phalam*, this is not the real area here; the real area, by
*lambena nighnaṃ ku-mukha-aikya-khaṇḍam*, half the sum of base and face times
the perpendicular, is **138**.

Then **171–172**, in Colebrooke's rendering:

> "Since the diagonals of the quadrilateral are indeterminate, how should the
> area be in this case determinate? The diagonals, found as assumed by the
> ancients, do not answer in another case. With the same sides, there are other
> diagonals; and the area of the figure is accordingly manifold. … How can a
> person, neither specifying one of the perpendiculars, nor either of the
> diagonals, ask the rest? or how can he demand a determinate area, while they
> are indefinite?
>
> **Such a questioner is a blundering devil. Still more so is he, who answers the
> question.** For he considers not the indefinite nature of the lines in a
> quadrilateral figure."

The Sanskrit is *sa pṛcchakaḥ **piśāco** vā vaktā vā nitarāṃ tataḥ | yo na vetti
catur-bāhu-kṣetrasya **aniyatāṃ sthitim***. Sūryadāsa's gloss on *piśāca*,
reported by Colebrooke: "so termed, because he blunders." Gaṇeśa's gloss on *the
ancients*: "By Śrīdhara and the rest." The prose between the verses states the
mechanism: *catur-bhuje hi eka-antara-koṇāv ākramya antaḥ-praveśyamānau bhujau
tat-saṃsaktaṃ sva-karṇaṃ saṃkocayataḥ, itarau tu bahiḥ-prasarantau sva-karṇaṃ
vardhayataḥ* — press two opposite corners inward and their diagonal shortens
while the other lengthens. The four-bar linkage is not rigid, and this is the
statement of it.

**175** is the worked figure that follows the refusal: face 11, base 22, sides 13
and 20, perpendicular 12. Gross area $\sqrt{22 \cdot 11 \cdot 20 \cdot 13} =
\sqrt{62920} = 250.84$, printed as **250**. True area **198**. The text then
gives the dissection: a 5-12-13 right triangle (area 30), a 6 × 12 rectangle
(72), a 12-16-20 right triangle (96), summing to 198.

Reconstructed from those three pieces: place the base from (0,0) to (22,0); the
13-side runs from (0,0) to **(−5,12)** — outside the base — the top from (−5,12)
to (6,12), the 20-side from (6,12) to (22,0). Cut at x = 0 and x = 6:

- x from −5 to 0: triangle, legs 5 and 12, **area 30**
- x from 0 to 6: rectangle 6 × 12, **area 72**
- x from 6 to 22: triangle, legs 16 and 12, **area 96**

Total **198**; base 22 ✓, face 11 ✓, sides 13 and 20 ✓, perpendicular 12 ✓, and
$(22+11)/2 \times 12 = 198$ ✓. The 13-side leans backwards, exactly as in the
negative-*ābādhā* triangle of 168. The three pieces the text names determine the
figure completely, and the figure they determine is the one the two verses say
it is.

**186–188** then does something else again. Given sides 25, 39, 52, 60 the text
first says the diagonals are *aniyata*, then reports two of them, 56 and 63,
"determined by the earlier writers, Brahmagupta and the rest" — *evam aniyatatve
'pi niyatāv eva karṇāv ānītau brahmagupta-ādyaiḥ* — and gives Brahmagupta's rule
for them, and works it: 25·39 + 52·60 = 4095, 25·52 + 39·60 = 3640, 52·39 +
25·60 = 3528; then $\sqrt{3640 \times 3528 / 4095} = \sqrt{3136} = 56$ and
$\sqrt{4095 \times 3528/3640} = \sqrt{3969} = 63$. Recomputed, both exact.
**189–190** then supplies a shorter route to the same two numbers and says so:

> bāhvor vadhaḥ koṭi-vadhena yuk syād ekā śrutiḥ koṭi-bhujā-vadha-aikyam |
> anyā **laghau saty api sādhane 'smin pūrvaiḥ kṛtaṃ yad guru tan na vidmaḥ** ||

Take two right triangles, cross-multiply their legs by each other's hypotenuses,
and the four products are the sides; then one diagonal is (product of the
uprights + product of the bases) and the other is the cross sum. With the 3-4-5
and 5-12-13 triangles: sides 25, 60, 52, 39; and 3·12 + 4·5 = 56, 3·5 + 4·12 =
63. **"This being an easy method, we do not know what the earlier writers were
doing with a heavy one."**

**147** is the rule that generates those triangles: choose two numbers, and
$2mn$, $n^2-m^2$, $n^2+m^2$ are upright, side and hypotenuse. The text's own
instances: (2,1) → 4, 3, 5; (2,3) → 12, 5, 13; (2,4) → 16, 12, 20. Śaṅkara's
*Kriyākramakarī*, at this verse, proves it from **Mādhava's** rule for the Rsine
and Rcosine of a sum of angles, applied to a triangle and itself (Wagner 2018, in its appendices, citing the
Kriyākramakarī at 293–97; the paper does not say whether those are pages or
verses, and neither do I).

## The circle

**199:**

> vyāse **bha-nanda-agni**-hate vibhakte **kha-bāṇa-sūryaiḥ** paridhiḥ sa **sūkṣmaḥ** |
> **dvāviṃśati**-ghne vihṛte 'tha **śailaiḥ** sthūlo 'thavā syād vyavahāra-yogyaḥ ||

The numbers are in *bhūtasaṃkhyā*, words for digits, running **from the lowest
place upward** — *aṅkānāṃ vāmato gatiḥ*: *bha*, the 27 lunar mansions, fills the
two lowest places; *nanda*, 9, the hundreds; *agni*, 3, the thousands —
**3927**. And *kha*, void, 0 in the units; *bāṇa*, the five arrows of Kāma, in
the tens; *sūrya*, the twelve suns, in the next two — **1250**.

Diameter × 3927/1250 is the *sūkṣma*, the fine
circumference; × 22/7 is the *sthūla*, the gross, fit for practical work.

$3927/1250 = 3.1416$, which is $62832/20000$ divided by 16 — Āryabhaṭa's ratio,
*Āryabhaṭīya* Gaṇitapāda 10 (499), reduced. Āryabhaṭa marked his **āsanna**,
approached. Bhāskara marks the same number **sūkṣma**, fine, and puts 22/7 beside
it under a different word. Two authors, one ratio, and three different words for
what the number is.

$3927/1250 - \pi = +7.35 \times 10^{-6}$. $22/7 - \pi = +1.26 \times 10^{-3}$.

**200** runs it both ways. Diameter 7 → circumference 7 × 3927/1250 =
27489/1250 = **21 + 1239/1250**, as printed; circumference 22 → diameter
27500/3927 = **7 + 11/3927**, as printed.

**201–202.** Area = circumference × diameter / 4; the sphere's surface is that ×
4, "like a net all round a ball"; the sphere's solid content is the surface ×
diameter / 6. With diameter 7:

| | text | recomputed |
|---|---|---|
| circle area | 38 + 2423/5000 | 192423/5000 = 38.4846 ✓ |
| sphere surface | 153 + 1173/1250 | 769692/5000 = 153.9384 ✓ |
| sphere volume | 179 + 1487/2500 | 153.9384 × 7/6 = 179.5948 ✓ |

Surface = four times the great circle; volume = surface × diameter / 6, which is
$\tfrac{4}{3}\pi r^3$ written as $\tfrac{4\pi r^2 \cdot 2r}{6}$.

**203** gives the shortcuts: area = $d^2 \times 3927/5000$; roughly $d^2 \times
11/14$; and *ghanī-kṛta-vyāsa-dalaṃ nija-ekaviṃśa-aṃśa-yuk*, the **cube of the
diameter, halved, plus its own twenty-first part** — $\frac{d^3}{2}\cdot
\frac{22}{21} = \frac{88}{21}r^3$. $88/21 = 4.190476$ against
$\tfrac{4}{3}\pi = 4.188790$: **+0.04%**. (Parsing the compound the other way, as
the cube of the *half*-diameter, gives $\tfrac{22}{21}r^3$, which is not a sphere
volume. The arithmetic decides which parse is meant.)

**204–205**, arrow and chord. Arrow $= \tfrac{1}{2}\left(d -
\sqrt{(d+c)(d-c)}\right)$; chord $= 2\sqrt{(d-\text{ś})\text{ś}}$; diameter $=
\frac{(c/2)^2}{\text{ś}} + \text{ś}$. Instanced at d = 10, c = 6: arrow 1, and
both inversions return 6 and 10. Exact, all three.

multipliers of the diameter over 120,000. Recomputed against

**210–211**, the chord from the arc without a table:

> cāpa-ūna-nighna-paridhiḥ prathama-āhvayaḥ syāt pañca-āhataḥ paridhi-varga-caturtha-bhāgaḥ |
> ādya-ūnitena khalu tena bhajet catur-ghna-vyāsa-āhataṃ prathamam āptam iha jyakā syāt ||

With C the circumference, a the arc, d the diameter, and $P = (C-a)a$:
$$\text{chord} = \frac{4dP}{\tfrac{5}{4}C^2 - P}.$$

Put $a = 2x$ degrees on $C = 360$, $d = 2R$: the expression reduces exactly to
$$\sin x = \frac{4x(180-x)}{40500 - x(180-x)},$$
which is the rule of **Bhāskara I, *Mahābhāskarīya* VII.17–19, c. 629** — five
hundred and twenty years earlier, and the same relation to the digit. Bhāskara II
gives it in the chord form and does not name a source here. *(I have not opened
the Mahābhāskarīya; I take the attribution from the standard reports of it and
mark it as unopened.)*

## कुट्टक — the pulveriser, worked

**Verses 242–260.** **242–246** state it: reduce dividend, divisor and additive by
a common factor first, and *if the additive is not divisible by what divides the
other two, the problem as posed is corrupt* — *yena chinnau bhājya-hārau na tena
kṣepaś ca etad duṣṭam uddiṣṭam eva*. Then mutual division until the remainder is
1; the quotients written **one below another**, the additive below them, zero
below that. Then, in the words of **245**, *sva-ūrdhve hate antyena yute
tad-antyaṃ tyajet muhuḥ* — the entry **above** the penultimate is multiplied by
the penultimate, the last is added to it, and the last is then dropped; and again,
until two numbers remain. And *evaṃ tadaiva atra yadā samās tāḥ syur
labdhayaś ced viṣamās tadānīṃ yathā-āgatau labdhi-guṇau viśodhyau* — if the
number of quotients is even take them as they come, if odd subtract each from its
own divisor.

**247.** 221x + 65 divisible by 195. gcd(221,195) = 13; reduced, 17x + 5 ≡ 0
(mod 15). The *vallī*: 17 = 1·15 + 2, 15 = 7·2 + 1 — quotients 1, 7. The column
printed is **1, 7, 5, 0**; folding upward, 5·7 + 0 = 35, 35·1 + 5 = 40; the pair
printed is **40, 35**; reduced mod 17 and 15, **labdhi 6, guṇa 5**. Recomputed:
(17·5 + 5)/15 = 6 ✓. Two quotients, even, taken as they come ✓. The alternatives
printed, 23/20 and 40/35, both check.

**249.** 100x + 90 divisible by 63. Quotients printed **1, 1, 1, 2, 2, 1** —
recomputed from 100 = 1·63 + 37, 63 = 1·37 + 26, 37 = 1·26 + 11, 26 = 2·11 + 4,
11 = 2·4 + 3, 4 = 1·3 + 1 ✓; six, even; the folded pair **2430, 1530**, reduced
to **labdhi 30, guṇa 18**; (100·18 + 90)/63 = 30 ✓. The text then re-solves the
same problem three more ways — reducing dividend and additive by 10, reducing
divisor and additive by 9, reducing both — and gets 18 and 30 each time.

**251.** 60x + 16 divisible by 13. Quotients 4, 1, 1, 1, 1 — **five, odd**. The
raw pair is 2, 8, and (60·2 + 16)/13 is not an integer. Subtracted from their
divisors: guṇa 13 − 2 = **11**, labdhi 60 − 8 = **52**, and (60·11 + 16)/13 =
676/13 = **52** ✓. The parity rule is what makes it come out.

**259–260**, the *saṃśliṣṭa-kuṭṭaka*, one divisor and two multipliers: add the
multipliers, add the remainders, solve once. 5x ≡ 7 and 10x ≡ 14, both mod 63:
solve 15x ≡ 21 (mod 63), guṇa **14**. Recomputed: 5·14 = 70 ≡ 7 ✓; 10·14 = 140
≡ 14 ✓. Both simultaneously, from one pulverisation.

**258** says what the machine is for, and it is not a puzzle: given the residue
of a planet's seconds, take 60 as dividend, the civil days in a *kalpa* as
divisor, the residue as the additive; the quotient is the seconds and the
multiplier is the residue of minutes. Then repeat with that as the new additive
for minutes, then 30 for degrees, then 12 for signs, then the revolutions in a
*kalpa* — and the last multiplier is the *ahargaṇa*, the count of days elapsed.
Then the same again for intercalary months and omitted days. The pulveriser
inverts the planetary theory: from where a planet is, back to what day it is.

## चक्रवाल — and Bhāskara says who named it

**Bījagaṇita 70–75**, the *varga-prakṛti*, the square-nature. **70** defines the
triple: a *hrasva* (least root), the *prakṛti* (the multiplier of its square), a
*kṣepa* (additive), and a *jyeṣṭha* (greatest root). **71** is the *bhāvanā*,
Brahmagupta's composition (*Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta* 18.64–65, 628):

> vajra-abhyāsau jyeṣṭha-laghvos tad-aikyaṃ hrasvaṃ laghvor āhatiś ca prakṛtyā |
> kṣuṇṇā jyeṣṭha-abhyāsa-yuj jyeṣṭha-mūlaṃ tatra abhyāsaḥ kṣepayoḥ kṣepakaḥ syāt ||

cross-multiply the greater and the lesser and add — that is the new lesser; the
product of the lessers times the *prakṛti*, plus the product of the greaters —
that is the new greater; and the product of the two additives is the new
additive. **71cd** gives the subtractive version.

**75**, four *anuṣṭubh*, is the cycle:

> hrasva-jyeṣṭha-pada-kṣepān bhājya-prakṣepa-bhājakān kṛtvā kalpyo guṇas tatra tathā prakṛtitaś cyute |
> guṇa-varge prakṛti-ūne 'thavā **alpaṃ śeṣakaṃ yathā** | tat tu kṣepa-hṛtaṃ kṣepo vyastaḥ prakṛtitaś cyute |
> guṇa-labdhiḥ padaṃ hrasvaṃ tato jyeṣṭham ato 'sakṛt |
> tyaktvā pūrva-pada-kṣepān **cakra-vālam idaṃ jaguḥ** ||

Set the least root as dividend, the additive as the additive, the greatest as
divisor; find a multiplier by the pulveriser such that the multiplier's square
minus the *prakṛti* leaves **as small a remainder as possible**; that remainder
divided by the additive is the new additive, sign reversed if the subtraction was
from the *prakṛti*; the quotient answering to the multiplier is the new least
root, whence the greatest; and so again and again, **abandoning the previous
root and additive — this they called the cakravāla.**

***Jaguḥ*, third person plural, past: "they called it." Bhāskara names the method
by a name he attributes to others and does not claim.** In this repository the
priority already stands with **Jayadeva, c. 950**, whose statement survives only
through Udayadivākara's *Sundarī* (~1073).

**76** poses the two cases:

> kā saptaṣaṣṭi-guṇitā kṛtir eka-yutā kā ca ekaṣaṣṭi-nihatā ca sakhe sa-rūpā |
> syān mūla-dā …

What square, multiplied by **sixty-seven** and increased by one, and what square
multiplied by **sixty-one** and increased by one, yields a square?

Colebrooke's translation prints the two runs from the commentaries. Recomputed
here, every step:

**N = 67.** The starting triple is (1, 8, −3), because $8^2 - 67\cdot 1^2 = -3$
— the nearest square above 67 taken as the greatest root, and the shortfall taken
as the additive.

| step | least | greatest | additive | check |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | 1 | 8 | −3 | 64 − 67 = −3 ✓ |
| | 5 | 41 | 6 | 1681 − 1675 = 6 ✓ |
| | 11 | 90 | −7 | 8100 − 8107 = −7 ✓ |
| | 27 | 221 | −2 | 48841 − 48843 = −2 ✓ |
| compose with itself | 11934 | 97684 | 4 | ✓ |
| divide by 2 | **5967** | **48842** | **1** | $48842^2 - 67\cdot5967^2 = 1$ ✓ |

**N = 61.** Start (1, 8, 3).

| step | least | greatest | additive | check |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| | 1 | 8 | 3 | 64 − 61 = 3 ✓ |
| | 5 | 39 | −4 | 1521 − 1525 = −4 ✓ |
| through the −4 and −1 rules | 3805 | 29718 | −1 | 883159524 − 883159525 = −1 ✓ |
| compose with itself | **226153980** | **1766319049** | **1** | ✓ |

$$1766319049^2 - 61 \times 226153980^2 = 1,$$

recomputed exactly. Colebrooke's page 175 prints the same triple: *L 226153980,
G 1766319049, A 1.*

**79, 80, 85, 86, 87** run the same machine on $13x^2-1$, $8x^2-1$, $6x^2+3$,
$12x^2+\dots$, $9x^2+52$, $4x^2+33$, $13x^2\pm13$, and on **negative** *prakṛti*:
*ṛṇa-gaiḥ pañcabhiḥ kṣuṇṇaḥ ko vargaḥ sa-ekaviṃśatiḥ vargaḥ syāt* — what square,
multiplied by **negative five** and increased by twenty-one, is a square? $x=1$:
$-5+21 = 16$; $x=2$: $-20+21 = 1$. Both squares. **81** says the additive can be
carried in either direction and that the *bhāvanā* with the unit-additive roots
makes the family infinite.

**88** closes the section: *uktaṃ bīja-upayogi idaṃ saṃkṣiptaṃ gaṇitaṃ kila* —
this much calculation, useful for algebra, has been stated in brief.

**The Kerala commentary, and where it stops.** Śaṅkara Vāriyar (fl. 1500–1560)
wrote the *Kriyākramakarī* on the *Līlāvatī* and left it unfinished; Nārāyaṇa
Mahiṣamaṅgalam (c. 1500–1575) completed it. **Śaṅkara's portion ends at verse
199** — the *sūkṣma*/*sthūla* circumference verse — and Nārāyaṇa picks it up
there; the pages that follow carry "various approximations of π" and
justifications "very close to the text of the *Yuktibhāṣā*" (Roy Wagner, "The
Kriyākramakarī's Integrative Approach to Mathematical Knowledge," *History of
Science in South Asia* 6, 2018, pp. 84–126, in the table there mapping
Līlāvatī verses to Kriyākramakarī pages in K. V. Sarma's 1975 edition; I have
not opened the edition itself).

The authorities the *Kriyākramakarī* quotes into the *Līlāvatī* include
**Āryabhaṭa, Govindasvāmin, Śrīdhara's *Triśatikā*, Śrīpati, Brahmagupta,
Parameśvara's own *Līlāvatī* commentary, and Mādhava.**

**And it argues the rule of three by Nyāya.** At Sarma 1975, pp. 204–6, the
commentary sets the *trairāśika* of *Līlāvatī* 73 inside the Naiyāyika schema of
**anumāna** — *pakṣa*, *sapakṣa*, *dharma*, *sādhana*, *sādhya*, and the
pervasion *vyāpti* — with the standard *dṛṣṭānta*: smoke on the mountain, fire in
the kitchen. The *icchā* and the unknown *icchāphala* are the *pakṣa*; the
*pramāṇa* and its *phala* are the correlate side, pervaded by the same *dharma*
"because the *pramāṇa* and the *icchā* are of the same kind but on different
sides" (Wagner 2018, Appendix E). *(The Kerala authors are drawing Nyāya's
inferential apparatus; a Jaina logician's account of the same move would not be
this one, and I have not looked for one.)*

Bhāskara's own claim for the *trairāśika* stands at **239–241**, at the end of
the shadow chapter, and Colebrooke renders it:

> "As the being, who relieves the minds of his worshippers from suffering, and
> who is the sole cause of the production of this universe, pervades the whole,
> and does so with his various manifestations, as worlds, paradises, mountains,
> rivers, gods, demons, men, trees, and cities; so is all this collection of
> instructions for computations pervaded by the rule of three terms."
>
> "Whatever is computed either in algebra or in this [arithmetic] by means of a
> multiplier and a divisor, may be comprehended by the sagacious learned as the
> rule of three terms. Yet has it been composed by wise instructors in
> miscellaneous and other manifold rules, teaching its easy variations, thinking
> thereby to increase the intelligence of **such dull comprehensions as ours**."

**Persian.** Colebrooke, Note A, p. xxvii:

> "The first, by **Faizi**, undertaken by the command of the Emperor **Acber**,
> was executed in the 32d year of his reign; **A.H. 995, A.D. 1587**. The
> translation of the *Vīja-gaṇita* is later by half a century, having been
> completed by **Ata Ullah Rashīdī**, in the 8th year of the reign of Shāh
> Jahān; **A.H. 1044, A.D. 1634**."

The same repository's commit history already records the other direction of that
traffic: Krṣṇadāsa's *Pārasīprakāśa*, a Sanskrit grammar of Persian written with
the *vibhakti* and *dhātu* apparatus.

**English.** John Taylor, *Lilawati, or a Treatise on Arithmetic and Mensuration*,
Bombay, 1816 — the *Līlāvatī* alone, a year before Colebrooke. H. T. Colebrooke,
1817 — the *Bījagaṇita* and *Līlāvatī* together with the twelfth and eighteenth
chapters of Brahmagupta, collated against three copies of the text and the
commentaries listed above.

**Print, in Sanskrit.** V. G. Apaṭe, ĀSS 99 (*Bījagaṇita*, 1930) and ĀSS 107
(*Līlāvatī* with Gaṇeśa and Mahīdhara, 1937); K. V. Sarma, VIS 66 (*Līlāvatī*
with the *Kriyākramakarī*, 1975).

**A count I made from the file.** Hayashi's e-text marks the places where Apaṭe's
reading and Sarma's differ: **22 marked as Apaṭe's, 30 as Sarma's, 52 in all**
across 272 verses. Almost all are single syllables — *aguṅlam*/*aṅgulam*,
*dhaṣṭena*/*dhṛṣṭena*, *khala*/*khalu*, *-uddhṛteḥ*/*-uddhṛtaḥ*. Two are
substantive: at **206–208** Sarma reads the polygon constants' word-numerals
differently in three places (*dvi-dvi-* for *tri-dvi-*, *śaila-ṛtu-* for
*bāṇa-iṣu-*, *tri-veda-* for *ku-rāma-*), which is a disagreement about the
digits themselves, not the spelling.

## The daughter

The story: Bhāskara cast his daughter's horoscope, found the one auspicious hour
for her marriage, set a pierced cup floating in a vessel of water so it would
sink at that moment, and a pearl from her dress fell into the cup and stopped the
hole; the hour passed; she never married, and he named the book for her.

**What I can establish, and I counted it.** The word *līlāvatī* occurs **five
times in the 272 verses**, and only twice as a vocative — at **13** (*aye bāle
līlāvati mati-mati*, in the addition problem) and at **17** (*bāle
bāla-kuraṅga-lola-nayane līlāvati*, in the multiplication problem). The other
three: verse **1**, where it is an adjective of the *pāṭī*, *lālitya-līlāvatīm*;
verse **202**, *vimalāṃ ced vetsi līlāvatīm*, accusative, where what is known is
the book; and verse **272** with the colophon, where it is the book's name.

The counts of the vocatives across the whole text, from the same file:

| | |
|---|---|
| *sakhe*, friend (m.) | 22 |
| *gaṇaka*, calculator (m.) | 16 |
| *gāṇitika*, mathematician (m.) | 3 |
| *bāle*, girl | 5 |
| *mitra*, friend (m.) | 5 |
| *vatsa*, child (m.) | 4 |
| *kovida* / *vidvan*, learned (m.) | 5 |
| *līlāvati* | 2 |
| *sumate* / *kānte* / *mṛga-akṣi* / *cañcala-akṣi* / *kalyāṇini* | 1 or 2 each |

**The masculine vocatives outnumber the feminine ones about four to one and are
spread over the whole book. Every feminine one but a single instance falls in
the first seventy-two verses** — the eight operations, fractions, the inverse
process, and the square-root problems — **and the one exception is at 202**, in
the circle chapter, where what is addressed is *līlāvatīm* the book.

The story is reported to stand in **Fayzī's Persian preface of 1587**.
Colebrooke, who read Fayzī's version and used it to authenticate the text, does
not repeat the story anywhere in the 1817 volume; the words *daughter*,
*horoscope* in that sense, and *nuptial* do not occur in it. The Sanskrit
*Līlāvatī* nowhere says who Līlāvatī is.

**What I cannot.** I have not read Fayzī's preface, in Persian or in translation.
I do not know whether the story is his, or older than him, or whether he found it
in a commentary. **I do not know who Līlāvatī was.**

## Not established here

- **I opened no manuscript.** Everything above is from two Sanskrit e-texts
  (Hayashi's, collated from Apaṭe 1930/1937 and Sarma 1975) and from the scanned
  text of Colebrooke 1817. The Sanskrit quoted here is Hayashi's word-split
  transliteration with the split undone by hand; the *Devanāgarī* of Apaṭe and
  Sarma I have not seen.
- **Taylor 1816 I could not read.** The Internet Archive's scan
  (`lilavatiatreatiseonarithmeticandmenustrationjohntaylor1816_96_I`) has been
  OCR'd into Devanāgarī characters throughout, including the Latin-script title
  page and the English body; the file is not usable as text. I have its title,
  place and date and nothing else, and I do not know what is in it.
- **The Golādhyāya date verse is unopened.** I quote it from secondary report
  and mark it. What I opened is Colebrooke's statement of its content and of the
  *Karaṇakutūhala*'s epoch.
- **The Mahābhāskarīya is unopened.** That *Līlāvatī* 210 reduces algebraically
  to $\sin x = 4x(180-x)/(40500 - x(180-x))$ I verified here; that this relation
  is Bhāskara I's, at *Mahābhāskarīya* VII.17–19 (c. 629), I take from standard
  report and have not checked against the text.
- **The origin of 52055 and 41031 is unknown to me.** The seven-gon and nine-gon
  chords are the only two of the seven in verses 206–208 that miss, and they miss
  by nearly the same amount, about eleven parts in 120,000, in the same
  direction. I have no account of where the numbers came from.
- **The *Gaṇitakaumudī* on the *Līlāvatī* that Colebrooke names as lost** is not
  necessarily Nārāyaṇa Paṇḍita's *Gaṇitakaumudī* of 1356, which survives. Whether
  the two are the same work I have not established.
- **Bhāskara's dates of death, his lineage, and the Pāṭaṇa/Chāḷisgaon inscription
  said to be erected by his grandson Cāṅgadeva** I could not source to a text or
  an edition of the epigraph. I did not find the inscription's edition and I do
  not restate its content.
- **The *bhūtasaṃkhyā* readings at 206–208 are disputed between the two printed
  editions** and I have taken Apaṭe's, which are the ones the worked example is
  computed with. Whether Sarma's readings give different constants I have not
  checked.
- **The perpendicular in verse 175 I reconstructed**, from the three dissection
  pieces the text names, to a figure with the 13-side leaning outside the base.
  The text prints figures (`[fig.7]`, `[fig.8a–c]`) which the e-text does not
  carry. I have not seen them.

  `Cakravala.agda`, `CakravalaDescent.agda`, `KuttakaValli.agda`,

  a dozen more, all written before anyone read this text. Whether the *vallī* in
  `KuttakaValli.agda` is the *vallī* of verse 244 — quotients written one below
