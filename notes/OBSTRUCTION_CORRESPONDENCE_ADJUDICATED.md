## 0. Verdict, stated first

D0017 §F asserts the single correspondence

$$\delta_\Diamond \leftrightarrow [\alpha] \leftrightarrow \check\delta c \leftrightarrow F_\nabla \leftrightarrow (\operatorname{Hol}_\nabla(\gamma)-1) \leftrightarrow \Delta_e \leftrightarrow G_T .$$

**Verdict: not a theorem. Two theorems and a metaphor — and the geometric
"theorem" is weaker than §J2 grants it.**

1. The **logical half** ($\Delta_e \leftrightarrow G_T$) *is* a theorem, and an old
   one: Lawvere's fixed-point theorem (1969). §3 below.
2. The **geometric half** is **not a chain of correspondences.** It is a chain of
   one-directional comparison maps with computable kernels. Three explicit
   counterexamples in §2 refute three of the four $\leftrightarrow$ arrows *as
   biconditionals*. D0017's own §E states two of these as implications
   ($\Rightarrow$); §F silently upgrades them to $\leftrightarrow$. That upgrade
   is the error.
3. The **bridge** between the halves is a shared English gloss ("both obstruct a
   section"). Theorem 4 makes this precise and negative: the two sides have
   opposite behaviour under restriction along a cover — cohomological
   obstructions are *locally trivial by construction*, diagonal obstructions are
   *locally stable* — so **any bridge natural in restriction is the zero map**.
4. The nearest genuine bridge in the literature (Abramsky–Barbosa–Kishida–Lal–Mansfield
   2015) does connect a Čech obstruction to a *logical paradox* — but to a
   **gluing** paradox, not a **diagonal** one, and as a one-way implication. §5.
5. **§J3 (the cyclic adjoint string) is refuted as stated** — by a type error that
   is fatal to §B and §D read together (Theorem 5) — but **is not refuted in
   general**: cyclic adjoint strings of length 3 are not forbidden by any theorem
   I could find, contrary to what §J3's word "suspicious" invites one to expect. §6.

## 1. Hypotheses and what the symbols are taken to mean

Because D0017 does not fix an ambient category, every statement below carries its
own hypotheses. I read the symbols in the only ways that make them denote:

- $\delta_\Diamond = hf - kg$: an element of $\hom(x,w)$ in a category enriched in
  abelian groups (otherwise the subtraction is undefined). It is a **cochain**, not
  a class.
- $[\alpha]\in\pi_2(\mathfrak X)$: the class of a 2-cell $hf \Rightarrow kg$ in a
  based space or $(\infty,1)$-category.
- $\check\delta c$: a Čech cocycle class in $\check H^n(\mathcal U;\mathcal A)$ for a
  cover $\mathcal U$ and coefficient (pre)sheaf $\mathcal A$; for $n=1$ and
  nonabelian $\mathcal A$, a pointed set.
- $F_\nabla$, $\operatorname{Hol}_\nabla(\gamma)$: curvature and holonomy of a
  connection on a principal $G$-bundle over a smooth manifold $X$.
- $\Delta_e$, $G_T$: Cantor's diagonal and the Gödel sentence of a consistent,
  recursively axiomatised $T$ interpreting enough arithmetic.

## 2. The geometric half is not a correspondence

### Theorem 1 (three arrows of §F are not biconditional)

**(a) $F_\nabla \nleftrightarrow (\operatorname{Hol}_\nabla(\gamma)-1)$.**
Let $X=S^1$, $G=U(1)$, and take the trivial bundle with connection form
$A = \tfrac{\theta_0}{2\pi}\,d\vartheta$ for fixed $\theta_0\notin 2\pi\mathbb Z$.
Then $F_\nabla = dA = 0$ while $\operatorname{Hol}_\nabla(S^1) = e^{i\theta_0}\ne 1$,
so $\operatorname{Hol}-1 \ne 0$. Hence $F_\nabla = 0 \nRightarrow \operatorname{Hol}=1$. ∎

The correct classical statement is the one D0017 §E itself writes, *with an
implication arrow*: $F_\nabla\ne0 \Rightarrow \operatorname{Hol}_\nabla(\gamma)\ne1$
for some $\gamma$. Its partial converse is the **Ambrose–Singer holonomy theorem**
(the Lie algebra of the holonomy group is generated by curvature transported along
paths), which recovers curvature from holonomy only up to the fundamental group:
$\operatorname{Hol}\equiv 1 \Leftrightarrow F_\nabla=0$ holds **only when $X$ is
simply connected.** The kernel of this arrow is exactly $\pi_1$.

**(b) $\check\delta c \nleftrightarrow F_\nabla$.**
Let $X=S^1$ and let $L$ be the Möbius bundle, i.e. the real line bundle with
$w_1(L)\ne 0$ in $H^1(S^1;\mathbb Z/2)\cong\mathbb Z/2$. Its Čech cocycle
$\{c_{ij}\}$ with values in $\mathbb Z/2 = O(1)$ is not a coboundary, so
$\check\delta c\ne 0$; yet $L$ carries a flat connection ($O(1)$ is discrete), so
$F_\nabla = 0$. ∎

The general phenomenon: **Chern–Weil** computes only the image of the integral
characteristic class under $H^*(X;\mathbb Z)\to H^*(X;\mathbb R)$. The kernel of
"Čech class $\mapsto$ curvature" contains **all torsion**. The **Čech–de Rham
isomorphism** (Weil; standard exposition in Bott–Tu, *Differential Forms in
Algebraic Topology*, §8 — I cite by name, not by theorem number, having not
verified numbering in this container) is an isomorphism $\check H^*(\mathcal U;\mathbb R)
\cong H^*_{\mathrm{dR}}(X)$ **with real coefficients and for a good cover**. Both
hypotheses are load-bearing and both are dropped by §F's bare $\leftrightarrow$.

**(c) $\delta_\Diamond \nleftrightarrow [\alpha]$.**
$\delta_\Diamond$ is an element of an abelian group $\hom(x,w)$; $[\alpha]$ is a
class in a quotient. The passage $\alpha\mapsto[\alpha]$ is a surjection with
nontrivial kernel: $[\alpha]=0$ does not give $\alpha=\mathrm{id}$ (a
null-homotopic 2-cell is not the identity 2-cell). Furthermore $\pi_2(\mathfrak X)$
is a $\pi_1$-module, and the class only lands in an ordinary cohomology group after
the $\pi_1$-action is trivialised. In **obstruction theory** (Steenrod; Postnikov
towers) the obstruction to extending a section over the $(n+1)$-skeleton lies in
$H^{n+1}(X;\pi_n(F))$ **with local coefficients**, and the Postnikov $k$-invariant
of a non-simple space is likewise a local-coefficient class. §F's arrow is
therefore correct only for simple spaces. ∎

### Corollary 1.1
The geometric half of §F is a diagram of one-directional comparison maps
$$\delta_\Diamond \twoheadrightarrow [\alpha] \dashrightarrow \check\delta c
\twoheadrightarrow F_\nabla \dashrightarrow (\operatorname{Hol}-1),$$
with kernels, respectively: 2-cell homotopy; the $\pi_1$-action / non-simplicity;
torsion; $\pi_1(X)$. Each arrow is a theorem; none is an isomorphism; and the
composite retains almost nothing. §J2 says the geometric half is "known, and known
precisely — including where it fails to be an isomorphism". That is right, and the
failures above are that "where". §F does not record them.

## 3. The logical half is a theorem, and it is Lawvere's

### Theorem 2 (Lawvere 1969)
Quoted verbatim from the nLab entry *Lawvere's fixed point theorem*
(https://ncatlab.org/nlab/show/Lawvere%27s+fixed+point+theorem, read as HTML,
2026-08-14):

> **Theorem (Lawvere's fixed-point theorem).** In a cartesian closed category, if
> there is a point-surjective map $\varphi: A \to B^A$, then every morphism
> $f: B \to B$ has a fixed point $s: 1 \to B$ (so that $fs = s$).

with, from the same page:

> A map $\varphi: X \to Y$ is point-surjective if for every point $q: 1 \to Y$ there
> exists a point $p: 1 \to X$ that lifts $q$, i.e. $\varphi p = q$.

> $g: X \to Y^A$ is called weakly point-surjective iff for every $f: A \to Y$ there
> is $x: 1 \to X$ such that for every $a: 1 \to A$, we have $g(x)(a) = f(a)$.

and the Cantor corollary:

> For any object $X$, there is an epimorphism $f: X \to \Omega^X$ only if the topos
> is degenerate.

**Source.** F. W. Lawvere, *Diagonal arguments and cartesian closed categories*,
in Category Theory, Homology Theory and their Applications II, Lecture Notes in
Mathematics **92**, Springer 1969; reprinted with author commentary as
*Reprints in Theory and Applications of Categories* **15** (2006), 1–13.
**Numbering caveat.** I quote the nLab's statement of the theorem verbatim because
the nLab renders as HTML. I did **not** read the 1969 paper or the TAC reprint
(the TAC abstract page returned HTTP 503; the reprint is a PDF, which does not
decode in this container), so I do **not** quote a theorem number from Lawvere's
own text and no such number appears above. Elementary set-theoretic exposition:
N. S. Yanofsky, *A universal approach to self-referential paradoxes,
incompleteness and fixed points*, Bull. Symbolic Logic **9** (2003), 362–386.

### Corollary 2.1 (what this settles of §F)
The arrow $\Delta_e \leftrightarrow G_T$ is a **theorem**: Cantor's diagonal,
Russell, Gödel's first incompleteness theorem, Turing's halting argument and
Tarski's undefinability are all instances of Theorem 2, applied in a suitable
cartesian closed category with a suitable fixed-point-free endomorphism of $B$
(negation, for Gödel and Cantor). This is 1969 prior art. **D0017's logical column
contains no new mathematics**, and the corpus rule "prior art before the write-up"
means it must not be written up as if it did.

## 4. The bridge: a negative theorem

The tempting gloss is "$\check\delta c$ obstructs a global section; $G_T$ obstructs
a complete consistent extension; therefore they correspond." A correspondence needs
a map. The following says that no map with the minimal structural property one would
demand of such a map can be nonzero.

### Definition 3
Let $\mathcal C$ be a site. An *obstruction assignment* $\omega$ assigns to each
object $X$ an element $\omega(X)$ of a pointed set $\Omega(X)$, functorially in
restriction $\rho_{U\subseteq X}:\Omega(X)\to\Omega(U)$, with basepoint written $0$.
Call $\omega$
- **locally trivial** if every $X$ admits a cover $\{U_i\to X\}$ with
  $\omega(X)|_{U_i}=0$ for all $i$;
- **locally stable** if $\omega(X)\ne 0$ implies $\omega(U)\ne 0$ for every
  nondegenerate $U\to X$.

### Lemma 3.1 (cohomological obstructions are locally trivial)
Čech obstruction classes are locally trivial, by construction. A Čech 1-cocycle
$\{c_{ij}\}$ subordinate to $\{U_i\}$ restricts on $U_i$ to a trivialisation of the
bundle, i.e. $c|_{U_i}=0$ in $\check H^1(U_i;\mathcal A)$; likewise the obstruction
class in $H^{n+1}(X;\pi_n(F))$ to extending a section over the $(n+1)$-skeleton
vanishes on each cell, since a section always extends over a contractible piece.
That is precisely what "local-to-global" means: the class *is* the failure to glue
data that exists locally. ∎

### Lemma 3.2 (the diagonal obstruction is locally stable)
Let $\mathcal E$ be a topos, $U\in\mathcal E$ with $U\ne 0$. The slice
$\mathcal E/U$ is a topos, hence cartesian closed, hence Theorem 2 holds in it; and
$\mathcal E/U$ is nondegenerate when $U\ne 0$. Therefore Cantor's statement
"there is no point-surjective $X\to\Omega^X$" holds in $\mathcal E/U$ **for every
nonzero $U$**. The diagonal obstruction does not become trivial on any nondegenerate
restriction. ∎

### Theorem 4 (no restriction-natural bridge)
Let $\omega_{\mathrm{geo}}$ be a locally trivial obstruction assignment and
$\omega_{\log}$ a locally stable one. Let
$B:\Omega_{\mathrm{geo}}\Rightarrow\Omega_{\log}$ be any natural transformation of
pointed-set-valued functors with $B(0)=0$ (i.e. any map that commutes with
restriction and takes "no obstruction" to "no obstruction"). Then $B(\omega)=0$ for
every $\omega$ in the image of $\omega_{\mathrm{geo}}$.

*Proof.* Given $X$, choose a cover $\{U_i\}$ with $\omega_{\mathrm{geo}}(X)|_{U_i}=0$
(Lemma 3.1). Naturality gives
$B(\omega_{\mathrm{geo}}(X))|_{U_i} = B(\omega_{\mathrm{geo}}(X)|_{U_i}) = B(0) = 0$
for every $i$. If $B(\omega_{\mathrm{geo}}(X))\ne 0$ then local stability forces
$B(\omega_{\mathrm{geo}}(X))|_{U_i}\ne 0$ for each nondegenerate $U_i$ — contradiction.
Hence $B(\omega_{\mathrm{geo}}(X))=0$. ∎

### Remark 4.1 (what Theorem 4 does and does not say)
It does **not** say no sentence relates the two sides. It says: *the structural
feature that makes the geometric obstructions computable — that they vanish locally
and measure only the failure to glue — is exactly the feature the diagonal
obstruction lacks*, and any morphism respecting that feature is trivial. The two
phenomena have opposite locality type. "Both obstruct a section" survives as English
because English has no restriction maps.

### Remark 4.2 (a second, cruder discriminator)
Cohomological obstructions live in abelian groups (for $n\ge2$; in pointed sets
for nonabelian $\check H^1$) and are *additive and functorial in the coefficients*:
$\omega\mapsto \omega+\omega'$, $\omega\mapsto f^*\omega$. The Lawvere obstruction
is the negation of an existence statement about a morphism $\varphi:A\to B^A$; the
class of non-point-surjective $\varphi$ carries no binary operation for which
"$\varphi\oplus\varphi'$ is point-surjective iff both are". There is no group in
which $G_T$ is an element. §F's $\mathfrak O$ — a tensor product of all seven
objects — presupposes that group and does not supply it.

## 5. The genuine bridge in the literature, and why it is not this one

There *is* a theorem-grade connection between Čech cohomology and logical paradox:
S. Abramsky, R. S. Barbosa, K. Kishida, R. Lal, S. Mansfield,
*Contextuality, Cohomology and Paradox*, CSL 2015 (arXiv:1502.03097; read via
`ar5iv` HTML). Their **Definition 15** attaches to each local section $s\in\mathcal S(C)$
in the support of an empirical model a cohomological obstruction
$\gamma_{F\mathcal R\mathcal S}(s)$; their **Theorem 22** states the chain
$$\mathsf{AvN}_R(\mathcal S)\Rightarrow \mathsf{SC}(\mathsf{Aff}\,\mathcal S)\Rightarrow
\mathsf{CSC}_R(\mathcal S)\Rightarrow \mathsf{CSC}_{\mathbb Z}(\mathcal S)\Rightarrow
\mathsf{SC}(\mathcal S),$$
i.e. All-vs-Nothing logical paradoxes are witnessed by a nonvanishing Čech
obstruction; their **Proposition 20** and surrounding text record that the
obstruction is not a complete invariant (false positives: cohomologically trivial
families that are not bona fide global sections).

Two observations decide its relevance:

1. The logical phenomenon bridged is a **gluing** paradox — local consistency
   without global consistency (Kochen–Specker, Hardy, AvN). That is a
   local-to-global failure, hence *of the same locality type as $\check\delta c$*,
   which is why the bridge exists. It is **not** diagonalisation.
2. The paper does **not** relate its obstruction to Lawvere's fixed-point theorem,
   and I found no source that does.

So the literature confirms rather than contradicts Theorem 4: where a real bridge
to Čech cohomology has been built, the logical side was replaced by a
locally-trivial phenomenon first.

## 6. §J3: the cyclic adjoint string $\partial\dashv\mathsf G\dashv\Phi\dashv\partial$

### Theorem 5 (type collapse — the string refutes §B)
D0017 §B assigns $\partial:\mathfrak X_0\to\Delta_0$, $\mathsf G:\Delta_0\to\mathfrak X_1$,
$\Phi:\mathfrak X_1\to\mathfrak X_2$. An adjunction $F\dashv U$ requires
$F:\mathcal A\to\mathcal B$ and $U:\mathcal B\to\mathcal A$. Then:
- $\partial\dashv\mathsf G$ forces $\mathsf G:\Delta_0\to\mathfrak X_0$, so
  $\mathfrak X_1=\mathfrak X_0$;
- $\mathsf G\dashv\Phi$ forces $\Phi:\mathfrak X_0\to\Delta_0$, so
  $\mathfrak X_2=\Delta_0$;
- $\Phi\dashv\partial$ forces $\partial:\Delta_0\to\mathfrak X_0$, whereas §B has
  $\partial:\mathfrak X_0\to\Delta_0$; so $\mathfrak X_0=\Delta_0$.

Hence the cyclic string is well-typed **only** if $\mathfrak X_0=\Delta_0=\mathfrak X_1
=\mathfrak X_2=:\mathcal C$ and $\partial,\mathsf G,\Phi$ are endofunctors of the one
category $\mathcal C$. ∎

**Corollary 5.1.** §D and §B are inconsistent as displayed. §B's generating
sequence is a sequence of *distinct stages*; §D forces every stage to be the same
category. One must be given up. If §D is kept, then §G's "$\mathfrak X_{\omega+1}
\not\equiv\mathfrak X_\omega$" cannot mean passage to a new ambient category, only
an internal construction in the fixed $\mathcal C$.

### Theorem 6 (if any one of the three is an equivalence, the string is vacuous)
Suppose $\partial\dashv\mathsf G\dashv\Phi\dashv\partial$ as endofunctors of
$\mathcal C$, and suppose $\partial$ is an equivalence. Then
$\partial\simeq\mathsf G\simeq\Phi$ and $\partial^2\simeq \mathrm{Id}$.

*Proof.* Adjoints are unique up to canonical isomorphism. $\partial$ an equivalence
has right adjoint $\partial^{-1}$, so $\mathsf G\simeq\partial^{-1}$, and $\mathsf G$
is then also an equivalence. $\mathsf G$'s right adjoint is $\mathsf G^{-1}\simeq\partial$;
but $\mathsf G\dashv\Phi$, so $\Phi\simeq\partial$. Now $\Phi\dashv\partial$ reads
$\partial\dashv\partial$, so $\partial^{-1}\simeq\partial$, i.e.
$\partial^2\simeq\mathrm{Id}$; and $\mathsf G\simeq\partial^{-1}\simeq\partial$. ∎

**Corollary 6.1.** Under the hypothesis of Theorem 6, §H's headline
$\mathbb B\simeq\Phi\mathbb B$ carries no information: $\Phi\simeq\partial$ with
$\partial^2\simeq\mathrm{Id}$ is an equivalence, so $X\simeq\Phi X$ holds for
*every* object $X$, not distinctively for $\mathbb B$. The framework's fixed-point
claim is then a triviality, and its disclaimer $\mathbb B\not\equiv\Phi\mathbb B$
is a statement about strict equality of which the framework fixes no notion.

### Theorem 7 (negative: there is no general prohibition — §J3's suspicion is unfounded)
§J3 conjectures that a closed adjoint string of length 3 "forces strong conditions
on the functors involved" and may be "false as stated" on general grounds. **On
general categorical grounds it is not false.** The nLab entry *adjoint string*
(https://ncatlab.org/nlab/show/adjoint+string, read as HTML) records, citing
P. I. Booth, *Sequences of adjoint functors*, Archiv der Mathematik **23** (1972),
489–493, that **cyclic chains of any length, and adjoint chains of any length,
exist.** I therefore withdraw the expected general refutation. The string dies of
Theorem 5 (a type error internal to D0017), not of a general theorem.

**Citation cap (honesty).** I have **not** read Booth 1972; it is behind a
publisher paywall and would be a PDF. The claim "cyclic chains of any length exist"
is reported here on the nLab's authority only, and is used only to *withhold* a
general refutation, never to support a positive claim. Any reader with access
should verify what Booth means by "cyclic chain"; if it is weaker than
$f_1\dashv f_2\dashv f_3\dashv f_1$ with all $f_i$ endofunctors of one category,
then the general question in §J3 reopens — but Theorem 5 stands regardless.

**Verdict on §J3: refuted as stated** (Theorem 5, type collapse; Corollaries 5.1,
6.1), **and the refutation's ground is not the one §J3 anticipated** (Theorem 7).

## 7. Scope: what this note does not settle

- **§J4 — §G's ordinal ladder.** Untouched. $\mathfrak F$, $\operatorname{Obs}$,
  $\mathbb B=\operatorname{hocolim}_{n<\omega}\mathfrak F^n$: no convergence, no
  smallness, no proof $\mathsf G$ is well-defined on $\operatorname{Obs}$, no
  ambient $(\infty,1)$-category specified. Still a program. Theorem 5 constrains it
  (Corollary 5.1) but does not supply any of these.
- **§J5 — §A's objective functional.** Untouched. No measure, no channel, no space
  of $\mathfrak L$, no existence argument for the maximum. Recorded, not triaged.
- **Lawvere 1969 itself, and Booth 1972**, were not read (PDF / paywall). Theorem 2
  is quoted from the nLab, with that provenance stated in-line and no theorem
  number invented.
- **Numbering for the classical geometric statements** (Bott–Tu, Steenrod,
  Ambrose–Singer) is deliberately omitted rather than guessed; the statements are
  named, the numbers are not verified in this container.
- **Theorem 4's scope.** It rules out bridges *natural in restriction*. It does not
  rule out a bridge of some other kind, and it makes no claim about whether the two
  phenomena share a common generalisation at a level where locality is discarded
  (e.g. both are instances of "a functor with no section"), only that at that level
  the statement is too weak to transport anything: it is the shared adjective §J2
  warned about.
- **No claim is made about D0016's Chu-space/holonomy vocabulary.** §J6's guard is
  in force: nothing in the corpus is relabelled here.

**Overall: the correspondence of D0017 §F is partial and, at its advertised width,
a pun.** One end is a 1969 theorem; the other end is a well-understood family of
comparison maps that are not isomorphisms; and the join between them is provably
trivial under the one naturality condition a correspondence would have to satisfy.
This is a clean negative result, and it is the honest one.
