# करणी — the squares are tabulated, and the book that carries them came down from one broken copy

**Varāhamihira, *Pañcasiddhāntikā* (after Śaka 427 = 505 CE) and *Bṛhatsaṃhitā*
(6th c.), read in T. S. Kuppanna Sastry & K. V. Sarma's critical edition
(P.P.S.T. Foundation, Adyar, 1993), in G. Thibaut & Mahāmahopādhyāya Sudhākara
Dvivedī's first edition (Benares, 1889), in Panditabhushana V. Subrahmanya
Sastri & M. Ramakrishna Bhat's *Bṛhatsaṃhitā* with translation (Bangalore,
1946), and in the Sanskrit e-text of A. V. Tripāṭhī's Sarasvatī Bhavana
Granthamālā edition transliterated by Michio Yano and proofread by Mizue
Sugita.**

---

## The copy

Every manuscript of the *Pañcasiddhāntikā* known to exist descends from one
damaged copy.

Kuppanna Sastry and Sarma collated five, in paper, all in Devanāgarī, and
divided them into two recensions on the strength of the errors they share:

- **A₁** — BORI Pune 338/1879-80, 22 folios, 11 lines a page, complete, copied
  at Stambhatīrtha (Cambay) in Saṃ. 1673 / Śaka 1538 = 1616 CE by Śaṅkara son of
  Govinda. This is the better of the two Thibaut used.
- **A₂** — National Library Calcutta 49, 24 folios, 9 lines a page, incomplete,
  breaking off in XVIII.90d.
- **B₁** — BORI Pune 37/1874-75, on "Universal foolscap" paper, 49 pages, 15–17
  lines a page. Thibaut's second. It carries his corrections down its whole
  length.
- **B₂** — National Library Calcutta 64, pages numbered 7 to 114, beginning only
  at I.22 because folios 1–6 are lost. Good hand, extremely corrupt matter;
  verses broken in the middle with verse-numbers dropped into the break.
- **B₃** — Oriental Institute Baroda 7165, 33 folios, complete, copied Saṃ. 1928
  / Śaka 1793 = 1872 CE by Uttamarāma Durlabharāma of Ahmedabad.

"Common corruptions and omissions indicate that even these two recensions go
back to a common original which too should have been far from perfect in the
matter of accuracy" (1993, p. XV–XVI). In the hundred years after 1889 no new
manuscript came to light; the ones that surfaced go back to the two already
used, and are identified as such by the errors they inherit.

About 125 verses of the *Pañcasiddhāntikā* survive additionally as quotations in
later authors. **117 of those 125 are in Utpala of Kashmir's commentary
(966 CE) — on the *Bṛhatsaṃhitā*.** The rest are scattered across Pṛthūdakasvāmin
(854), Sūryadevayajvan (b. 1191), Makkibhaṭṭa (14th c.), Parameśvara
(c. 1380–1460) and Nīlakaṇṭha Somayāji (b. 1443). Pingree held that the Kerala
astronomers were quoting only what they could have got from Utpala, and so had
no copy; Kuppanna Sastry and Sarma answer that Nīlakaṇṭha quotes PS I.3–4 in the
*Jyotirmīmāṃsā* and two half-verses in his *Āryabhaṭīyabhāṣya* IV.10, and
Sūryadevayajvan quotes PS XIII.36, none of which are in Utpala.

Thibaut opened his 1889 preface:

> "There is some reason to fear that the feeling of any one who may examine in
> detail this edition and translation of Varāha Mihira's astronomical work will,
> in the first place, be wonder at the boldness of the editors. I am fully
> conscious that on the imperfect materials at our disposal an edition in the
> strict sense of the word cannot be based, and that what we are able to offer
> at present deserves no other name but that of a first attempt to give a
> general idea of the contents of the Pañcasiddhāntikā."

He printed the manuscript text in the left column and his reconstruction in the
right. His stated rule for the right column:

> "What, in the attempt to reconstitute the text of an astronomical or
> mathematical work, has chiefly to be kept in view, is of course to arrive at
> rules which are capable of being proved mathematically. This consideration
> has, in more than one place, led us to introduce changes even where such
> appeared hardly to be required by the external form of the traditional text."

The 1993 editors state the opposing rule in the vocabulary of Sanskrit textual
criticism: **स्थितस्य गतिश्चिन्तनीया — *sthitasya gatiś cintanīyā*, "the
justification of the extant reading should be thought of"** — and add the working
method that goes with it: correct the copyist by "visualising the psychology of
the scribe who is illiterate with reference both to the language and the subject
of the text." Emendations go in curved brackets, supplements in square brackets,
doubtful ones flagged with a question mark, and every variant of every
manuscript is printed, "right or wrong," on three stated grounds, the third
being that a wrong emendation is only correctable by a later editor if the
readings are in front of him.

Two editorial principles, both explicit, in print, sixteen years and a hundred
and four years after the same manuscripts.

## The man

**Bṛhajjātaka 26.1** (Trivandrum Sanskrit Series 91), the closing signature:

> ādityadāsa-tanayas tad-avāptabodhaḥ
> kāpitthakaḥ savitṛlabdhavaraprasādaḥ |
> āvantiko munimatāny avalokya samyag
> horāṃ varāhamihiro ruciraṃ cakāra ||

Son of Ādityadāsa; got his learning from him; of Kāpitthaka; received the boon
of the Sun; resident of Avanti (Ujjain); having looked properly at the doctrines
of the sages, made the *Horā*. (The Sanskrit Documents e-text of the
*Bṛhajjātaka*, which numbers this 26.9, reads *kāpitthake* and *ghorāṃ*; I have
not seen the manuscripts and do not know which is right.)

Utpala, on *Bṛhatsaṃhitā* 1.1, calls him **Magadhadvija**, and glosses Kāpitthaka
as a village: *kāpitthākhye grāme yo 'sau bhagavān savitā sūryaḥ, tasmāl labdhaḥ
prāpto varaḥ prasādo yena*. Ajay Mitra Shastri (*India as seen by Varāhamihira*,
MLBD, 1969, p. 19) identifies it with the Kapittha that Yuan Chwang visited,
Saṅkāśya, modern Sankisa in Farrukhabad district.

His father's name is Āditya-dāsa; his own ends in **Mihira**, from Mithra; he
salutes the Sun at the head of every work he wrote except the *Vivāhapaṭala*,
which opens to Kāma; his son Pṛthuyaśas opens the *Ṣaṭpañcāśikā* to the Sun.
Mihira is a surname borne by Śākadvīpa brāhmaṇas, Sun-worshippers.

**Date.** *Pañcasiddhāntikā* I.8 takes Śaka 427 = 505 CE as the epoch for the
Pauliśa computation, and the practice of a *karaṇa* is to take an epoch near the
composition. Amarāja, commenting on Brahmagupta's *Brāhmasphuṭasiddhānta*, wrote:
*navādhika-pañcasaṃkhyaśāke varāhamihirācāryo divaṃ gataḥ* — "in Śaka 509
Varāhamihira went to heaven," i.e. 587 CE.

The legends putting him among the nine gems of Vikrama's court the 1993 editors
dismiss as "fable and eulogical, not based on facts."

## The five

The *Pañcasiddhāntikā* is a digest of five schools. **PS I.3:**

> The five *Siddhāntas*, of which this work is a compendium, are the Pauliśa, the
> Romaka, the Vāsiṣṭha, the Saura and the Paitāmaha. Of these five, the first two,
> viz., the Pauliśa and the Romaka, have been commented upon by Lāṭadeva.

All five, in the recensions Varāhamihira used, are lost. The works now current
under those names are different books: the extant Romaka and Vāsiṣṭha agree with
the "modern" *Sūrya Siddhānta*, and Varāhamihira's Saura, despite the name, does
not agree with the modern *Sūrya Siddhānta* but with a lost work of Āryabhaṭa's
that Bhāskara I calls the **Ārdharātrika-pakṣa** — which is also the basis of
Brahmagupta's *Khaṇḍakhādyaka*.

**PS I.4** ranks them, and ranks them on one criterion:

> The *tithi* resulting from the Pauliśa is tolerably accurate and that of the
> Romaka approximate to that. The *tithi* of the Saura is very accurate. But that
> of the remaining two (viz. the Vāsiṣṭha and the Paitāmaha) have slipped far
> away (from the real).

The criterion is chosen because *tithi* is the *aṅga* independent of the origin
of reference in the ecliptic, checkable against eclipses and heliacal risings,
and needed to get days from epoch, "the *sine qua non* of all astronomical
computation."

Thibaut and Dvivedī changed *tithi* to *kṛta* here. The manuscripts read only
*tithi* or *tithaḥ*. Neugebauer and Pingree changed it to *sivatha*. The 1993
editors: "Doing this, they have condemned the Vāsiṣṭha Siddhānta beyond the
author's intention and become blind to its merits and peculiarities."

**PS I.5–7**, on what the book will contain, opens:

> I shall tell in this work, avoiding all jealousy, the computation of the solar
> eclipse, which is guarded as a great secret and in which the mind of the
> astronomer reels.

## Rome, in integers

**PS I.15:** the luni-solar *yuga* of the Romaka Siddhānta is **2850 solar
years**, containing **1050 intercalary months** and **16,547 elided days**.

**PS I.16** gives the reduction: solar years × 12 = solar months; + intercalary
months = synodic months; × 30 − elided days = civil days; + solar years =
sidereal days.

Run it:

| | |
|---|---|
| solar months | 2850 × 12 = 34,200 |
| synodic months | 34,200 + 1,050 = 35,250 |
| tithis | 35,250 × 30 = 1,057,500 |
| civil days | 1,057,500 − 16,547 = **1,040,953** |
| lunar revolutions | 35,250 + 2,850 = 38,100 |

The year is 1,040,953 ÷ 2850 = **365.246666… days = 365; 14, 48** in days,
ghaṭikās, vighaṭikās.

365 + ¼ − ¹⁄₃₀₀ = 365.246666… — **equal, exactly, with no residue.** The Romaka
year in the *Pañcasiddhāntikā* is the tropical year of Hipparchus, recoverable to
the digit from two integers Varāhamihira wrote down.

The synodic month is 1,040,953 ÷ 35,250 = 29.53058156 days = 29; 31, 50, 5, 3…
The modern value is 29.530589; the difference is seven millionths of a day.

And 2850 = 150 × 19, 1050 = 150 × 7: **seven intercalary months in nineteen
years**, the nineteen-year luni-solar cycle, sitting inside the two numbers.
Varāhamihira does not name it. He names the *siddhānta* **Romaka**, and ranks its
*tithi* second of five.

The Saura's minor *yuga* he gives as 180,000 solar years with 66,389 intercalary
months and 1,045,095 elided days; ×24 gives 4,320,000 years, 1,577,917,800 civil
days, 57,753,336 lunar revolutions — the constants of Āryabhaṭa's
Ārdharātrika-pakṣa, of the *Khaṇḍakhādyaka*, and of the Pauliśa that Bhaṭṭotpala
quotes, and not of the modern *Sūrya Siddhānta*.

**PS XV.19–20** records who said what about where the day begins. Siṃhācārya:
sunrise at Laṅkā. The preceptor of the Yavanas: ten *muhūrtas*, twenty *nāḍikās*,
into the night. And:

> **20.** Āryabhaṭa has said that the day commences at mid-night at Laṅkā. He
> himself again has said, the day commences from sunrise at Laṅkā.

Āryabhaṭa wrote two systems and Varāhamihira wrote down that he wrote two. The
second is lost. It is known from Bhāskara I's *Mahābhāskarīya* ch. VII, from
Brahmagupta following it in the *Khaṇḍakhādyaka* — and from this line, in which
the point being made is that the two do not agree.

## करणी

**PS IV.1.** Take the circumference in 360 units, square it, take a tenth of the
square, extract the root: that is the diameter. So *d* = *c*/√10, i.e. √10 for
the circumference-to-diameter ratio. The same rule is in the *Sūrya Siddhānta*
1.59 for the earth: diameter 1600 *yojanas*, square, multiply by ten, take the
root. The error against π is about 0.0067%; on a circumference of 21,600′ it puts
the radius at 3415′ where 3438′ is right.

He then takes the diameter as **4° = 240′**, so the radius, the maximum sine, is
**120′**, and tabulates at intervals of 3° 45′.

**PS IV.2** does not tabulate the half-chord. It tabulates its square:

> The square of the radius, (i.e. 14,400), is called *dhruva* (*karaṇī*),
> (literally, "Fixed Irrational"). The fourth part of it, (i.e. 3600), is the
> *karaṇī* related to the first sign, (or 30°). *Dhruvakaraṇī* minus the *karaṇī*
> of Meṣa, (i.e. 14,400 − 3600 = 10,800), is the *karaṇī* of two signs, (or 60°).
> **The square root of a *karaṇī* is the tabular sine.**

*Karaṇī* is the tradition's word for a surd — a magnitude carried as its square
because its root is not expressible. The table is built entirely in the squares.
The root is taken once, at the end, per entry.

**PS IV.3–5** give the descent, in two forms:

  I. *karaṇī*(θ) = ¼ [ sin²2θ + (120′ − sin(90° − 2θ))² ]
  II. *karaṇī*(θ) = 60′ × (120′ − sin(90° − 2θ))

with the complement rule **karaṇī(n) = 14,400 − karaṇī(24 − n)** — which is
sin²θ + cos²θ = R² — and 7200 given as the *karaṇī* of one and a half signs
(45°), since 120²/2 = 7200. The editors' note on the pair: "Of the two formulae,
the first is suited to geometrical representation, and the second to
computation."

Starting from *karaṇī*(8) = 3600 and *karaṇī*(16) = 10,800, halving and
complementing reaches all twenty-four.

**The √10 does not propagate.** The editors state why, and it is checkable: the
sines are not derived from the circumference at all, but from R = 120 by the
halving rule. Had they come from √10, the first entry would be 7′ 54″, the 96th
part of the circumference where sine and arc are indistinguishable. The text
gives 7′ 51″.

**PS IV.12–14** give the twenty-four *differences* as a separate table, and here
the manuscript physically stops. The last quarter of verse 13 and the first two
feet plus four mātrās of the third foot of verse 14 are gone in every copy. The
1993 edition prints the gap, and reconstructs one number out of it: the eighth
interval's seconds must have been 55, because the sines on either side of it
require 55. Thibaut and Dvivedī, having already made the 14th sine 95′ 13″ and
the 16th 103′ 56″ instead of 95′ 12″ and 103′ 55″ — which pushed every sine in
the third sign one second high, so that the 24th, the radius itself, came out
120′ 1″ — could not recover the surviving digits of IV.14 by comparison, called
the text very corrupt, and emended it. The 1993 editors, having the arithmetic
right, report: "It may be seen that not a single word is wrong here."

The text is broken. What is left of it is checked by the numbers, and the numbers
say which of the two editors' hands to undo.

## The earth, at the antipodes

**PS XIII.1–5**, quoted entire by Utpala on *Bṛhatsaṃhitā* 2.55, and by Sūryadeva
and Nīlakaṇṭha on *Āryabhaṭīya*, Golapāda 6:

> **1.** The spherical earth which is constituted of the five elements, stands
> poised in the region of space, marked by the host of stars forming a cage as it
> were.
>
> **3.** Just as the reflection of the objects on the bund of a water course is
> upside-down, so we are, (with respect to the Devas). The Asuras too consider
> the Devas to be upside-down.
>
> **4.** Just as the flame of the fire, observed by men here, flares upwards and
> anything thrown up falls down towards the earth, the same upward flaring of the
> flame, and the down-ward falling of a heavy object is experienced by the
> Asuras, (at the antipodal region).
>
> **5.** The axis of the earth extends right up and right down to the stellar
> sphere. The stellar sphere, bound by the axis to the earth, rotates by the wind
> system called Pravaha.

## What the *Bṛhatsaṃhitā* says in its fourth verse

**Bṛhatsaṃhitā 1.3:**

> …tulye 'rthe 'kṣarabhedād amantrake kā viśeṣoktiḥ ||

Rendered in the 1946 edition: "Simply because this ancient work was composed by a
sage, it should be good, while that written by an ordinary man of the present day
could not be so. When the sense conveyed in both the cases is the same, while the
words used alone are different, why should there be any distinction between the
two as it is a non-Vedic subject?"

**1.4:** it has been declared by the grandfather (Brahmā) that the weekday of
Mars is not auspicious; people of the present generation have said the same
thing; *ko 'tra viśeṣo nṛdivyakṛteḥ* — where is the special merit as between what
a man made and what a god made?

Thirteen verses later, **2.14**:

> mlecchā hi yavanās teṣu samyak śāstram idaṃ sthitam |
> ṛṣivat te 'pi pūjyante kiṃ punar daivavid dvijaḥ ||

The Yavanas are *mleccha*; among them this science stands well established;
even they are honoured like *ṛṣi*s — how much more a *daivavid* who is a
*dvija*.

The two are the same argument, applied first upward and then downward: where no
*mantra* is at stake, the standing of a statement is not the standing of who
said it.

**The reception is documented in the book I read it in.** The 1946 edition's
preface, having quoted the verse, quotes an authority against the use Western
orientalists made of it:

> "From a single Sanskrit Sloka that reads, 'The Yavanas are Mlechchas; in them,
> this Science is established; therefore, even they deserve worship like Rishis'
> … they have gone so far as to declare that all Indian sciences are but echoes
> of the Greek; whereas a true reading of the Sloka may show that the Mlechcha
> disciples of the Aryans are herein praised in order to encourage them to a
> further study of the Aryan Sciences."

Four verses of the same author, in the same books, three positions in play, all
of them in print: Varāhamihira's, the orientalists' use of him, and the 1946
rebuttal of that use.

## The perfume chapter

*Bṛhatsaṃhitā* **gandhayukti**, chapter 76 in the Tripāṭhī edition, chapter 77 in
Subrahmanya Sastri & Bhat, who number the split of adhyāya 38 differently. It is
a chapter about hair-dye, incense, bath oils and betel. It contains the
arithmetic.

**76.20:**

> ṣoḍaśake dravyagaṇe caturvikalpena bhidyamānānām |
> aṣṭādaśa jāyante śatāni sahitāni viṃśatyā ||

In a set of sixteen substances, of those separated by the four-choice, there
arise eighteen hundred together with twenty. **C(16,4) = 1820.**

**76.18–19** counts the ways of proportioning a chosen four: each substance taken
in one proportion, combined with three others in two, three and four proportions
successively, makes six scents; so likewise when taken in two, three and four
proportions; four substances so combined yield twenty-four; "so too the other
tetrads. Hence the sum will be **ninety-six**."

**76.21:**

> ṣaṇṇavatibhedabhinnaś caturvikalpo gaṇo yatas tasmāt |
> ṣaṇṇavatiguṇaḥ kāryaḥ sā saṅkhyā bhavati gandhānām ||

Since the four-choice group is divided by ninety-six divisions, therefore it must
be multiplied by ninety-six; that is the number of the perfumes.

1820 × 96 = **174,720**, and **76.17** has already said the number in words:
*atra sahasracatuṣṭayam anyāni ca saptatisahasrāṇi | lakṣaṃ śatāni sapta
viṃśatiyuktāni gandhānām* — four thousand, and another seventy thousand, and a
lakh, and seven hundred, joined with twenty. 4,000 + 70,000 + 100,000 + 700 + 20
= 174,720.

**76.22** is not about perfume:

> pūrveṇa pūrveṇa gatena yuktaṃ
> sthānaṃ vināntyaṃ pravadanti saṅkhyām |
> icchāvikalpaiḥ kramaśo 'bhinīya
> nīte nivṛttiḥ punar anyanītiḥ ||

A place joined with the preceding one that has gone before — all but the last —
they call the count; leading them out in order by the choices desired, when one
is led out there is a stopping, and then another leading. (My rendering of the
transliterated verse; the technical sense is carried by the construction the
commentators give, below, not by this English.)

The 1946 note reads it as a construction: write 1 to 16 in a column; beside it
write 1; above that write the sum of the first two of the first column; add that
to the next and write it above; continue to the penultimate; repeat the process
in the third and fourth columns; the last entry of the last column is the answer.
The printed table runs

```
 16
 15   1
 14   1  120
 13  91  105  560
 12  78  455 1820
 11  66  364 1365
 10  55  286 1001
  9  45  220  715
  8  36  165  495
  7  28  120  330
  6  21   84  210
  5  15   56  126
  4  10   35   70
  3   6   20   35
  2   3   10   15
  1   1    4    5
      1    1    1
```

— four columns of repeated partial sums, terminating at 1820. The commentator's
route through it is the cumulation 91, 78, 66, 55, 45, 36, 28, 21, 15, 10, 6, 3,
1 = 455, then 364, 286, 220, 165, 120, 84, 56, 35, 20, 10, 4, 1, summing to 1820.
The 1946 editors' remark on Bhaṭṭotpala's exposition of it: "The process to get at
the above figures as enunciated by Bhaṭṭotpala in his commentary is circuitous
and confusing."

**76.29–30** does the same for nine substances taken three at a time and states
the answer: **caturaśīti, 84.** C(9,3) = 84.

## कच्छपुट

**76.23–24** assigns the proportions by *bhūtasaṃkhyā* — numbers named by things:

> dvitrīndriyāṣṭabhāgair aguruḥ patraṃ turuṣkaśaileyau |
> viṣayāṣṭapakṣadahanāḥ priyaṅgumustārasāḥ keśaḥ ||
> spṛkkātvaktagarāṇāṃ māṃsyāś ca kṛtaikasaptaṣaḍbhāgāḥ |
> saptartuvedacandrair malayanakhaśrīkakundurukāḥ ||

*indriya* = 5, *viṣaya* = 5, *pakṣa* = 2, *dahana* = 3, *kṛta* = 4, *ṛtu* = 6,
*veda* = 4, *candra* = 1. Reading them off:

|  |  |  |  |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2 | 3 | 5 | 8 |
| 5 | 8 | 2 | 3 |
| 4 | 1 | 7 | 6 |
| 7 | 6 | 4 | 1 |

**76.25:**

> ṣoḍaśake kacchapuṭe yathā tathā miśrite caturdravye |
> ye 'trāṣṭādaśa bhāgās te 'smin gandhādayo yogāḥ ||

In the sixteen-fold *kacchapuṭa*, the four substances being mixed in whatever
manner, whatever eighteen parts are here — those are the perfume combinations in
it.

*Kacchapuṭa* is the name of the vessel or box of sixteen compartments. **Eighteen
is the number that comes out along every line.** Taking the array as given:

- the four rows: 18, 18, 18, 18
- the four columns: 18, 18, 18, 18
- both principal diagonals **and all six broken diagonals**, i.e. all four wrapped
  diagonals in each direction: 18, every one
- **all sixteen cyclic 2×2 blocks**, including those wrapping across the edges:
  18, every one

(Checked entry by entry from the numbers the two verses name. The array is not a
classical magic square: its entries are 1–8, each appearing twice, not 1–16. What
holds of it is the line-sum condition, and it holds in every wrapped direction.)

**76.26** closes the sequence: the scents so made *kartavyāḥ sarvatobhadrāḥ*.
Subrahmanya Sastri and Bhat render it "scents called 'Sarvatobhadra' — 'good for
all purposes'." *Sarvatobhadra* — "auspicious from every direction" — is also the
name Nārāyaṇa Paṇḍita gives, in the **bhadragaṇita**, the fourteenth and last
chapter of the *Gaṇitakaumudī* (1356), to the square whose broken diagonals sum
like its rows. **Whether Varāhamihira's word here is the technical one or the
ordinary one I cannot establish, and I do not know.** The array in front of it has
the property; the word attaches, in the verse, to the perfumes.

## The gauge

**Bṛhatsaṃhitā 23**, *pravarṣaṇādhyāya*, on the quantity of rainfall.

**23.2:**

> hastaviśālaṃ kuṇḍakam adhikṛtyāmbupramāṇanirdeśaḥ |
> pañcāśat palam āḍhakam anena minuyāj jalaṃ patitam ||

Taking a vessel one *hasta* (cubit) wide, the statement of the water-measure is
made; fifty *pala*s make an *āḍhaka*; by this let the fallen water be measured.

An instrument with a stated aperture, and a stated conversion from weight to
volume-unit.

**23.4** states the disagreement about how far a measurement reaches:

> kecid yathābhivṛṣṭaṃ daśayojanamaṇḍalaṃ vadanty anye |
> gargavasiṣṭhaparāśaramatam etad dvādaśān na param ||

Some say the rain applies to a circle of ten *yojanas*; the view of Garga,
Vasiṣṭha and Parāśara is twelve, and not more.

**23.6–9** then give expected *droṇa*s of rain by *nakṣatra*: sixteen for Hasta,
Āpya, Saumya, Citrā, Pauṣṇa, Dhaniṣṭhā; four for Śatabhiṣak, Aindra, Svāti; ten
for Kṛttikā; fourteen for Śravaṇa, Maghā, Anurādhā, Bharaṇī, Mūla; twenty-five
(*pañcakṛti*, five squared) for Phalgunī; twenty for Punarvasu; twenty for
Aindrāgna and Vaiśva; thirteen for Sārpa; twenty-five for Āhirbudhnya, Āryamṇa,
Prājāpatya; fifteen for Aja; fifteen for Puṣya; twelve for Vājibha; eighteen for
Raudra.

## The end of the book, twice

The *Bṛhatsaṃhitā* closes with two chapters that are about the book.

**105.2:**

> pūrvācāryagranthā notsṛṣṭāḥ kurvatā mayā śāstram |
> tān avalokyedaṃ ca prayatadhvaṃ kāmataḥ sujanāḥ ||

In making this śāstra I have not discarded the works of the earlier teachers;
having looked at those and at this, exert yourselves as you wish, good people.

**105.4:**

> durjanahutāśataptaṃ kāvyasuvarṇaṃ viśuddhim āyāti |
> śrāvayitavyaṃ tasmād duṣṭajanasya prayatnena ||

The gold of poetry, heated in the fire of bad men, comes to purity; therefore it
must deliberately be read out to hostile people.

**105.5:**

> granthasya yat pracarato 'sya vināśam eti
> lekhyād bahuśrutamukhādhigamakrameṇa |
> yad vā mayā kukṛtam alpam ihākṛtaṃ vā
> kāryaṃ tad atra viduṣā parihṛtya rāgam ||

Of this book as it circulates, whatever comes to destruction — by the copying, by
the course of its passage through the mouths of the much-learned — or whatever I
have here done badly or left undone: let the learned man set aside partiality and
do that here.

**The same verse stands at the end of the *Bṛhajjātaka*** (26.8 in the Sanskrit
Documents e-text), verbatim, and so does the closing salutation that follows it
in both books — *dinakara-muni-guru-caraṇa-praṇipāta-kṛta-prasāda-matinedaṃ
śāstram upasaṅgṛhītam, namo 'stu pūrvapraṇetṛbhyaḥ*. Varāhamihira signed off two
different books with the same instruction for repairing them after transmission
damages them.

**106** is the *śāstrānukramaṇī*: he indexes his own hundred chapters in thirteen
verses, in order, by name, and ends —

> śatam idam adhyāyānām anuparipāṭikramād anukrāntam |
> atra ślokasahasrāṇy ābaddhāny ūnacatvāri ||

This hundred of chapters has been enumerated in sequence; here just under four
thousand *ślokas* are bound.

Then 106.14 names what is *not* here and where it went: the remainder is stated in
the *Yātrā*, the *Jātaka* is full of marvels, the *karaṇa* is much-questioned.

The e-text I read carries 2,743 numbered verses across 106 sections. A *śloka* in
a grantha-count is a unit of thirty-two syllables, not a verse, and much of the
*Bṛhatsaṃhitā* is in *āryā* and longer metres; the two counts are not in conflict,
but **I have not done the syllable count and do not claim the reconciliation.**

## Not established here

- **I did not open a manuscript.** Everything above is from printed editions and
  from a Sanskrit e-text. Where the 1993 edition's own scan carries Devanāgarī, the
  OCR of it is unreadable, and I have quoted its English translation rather than
  invent a reading. The *Bṛhatsaṃhitā* Sanskrit quoted here is from the
  transliteration of A. V. Tripāṭhī's edition, not from Kern's and not from a
  manuscript.
- **Whether Varāhamihira's *sarvatobhadra* at BS 76.26 is a technical name for the
  array or an ordinary adjective for the scents — I do not know.** It stands one
  verse after the *kacchapuṭa*, and the array does have the property the word later
  names. That is the whole of what I can say.
- **I have not established when the 4×4 pandiagonal array was first written in
  India.** BS 76.23–25 is the oldest attestation the 1993 and 1946 editions and
  this repository's own `formal/cubical/Bhadraganita_…agda` header point at, and
  Nārāyaṇa Paṇḍita's *Gaṇitakaumudī* 14 (1356) is the general treatment. Between
  550 and 1356 I have read nothing.
- **PS I.3 says Lāṭadeva commented on the Pauliśa and the Romaka.** No work of
  Lāṭadeva survives. He is named again at PS XV.18. That is all I know about him.
- **The 1993 edition's printed appendices include a discussion arguing that the
  Śaka era of PS I.8 is 612 BCE rather than 78 CE**, and reworking the epoch date
  accordingly. The editors' own introduction (p. XXVII) takes Śaka 427 = 505 CE and
  Amarāja's Śaka 509 = 587 CE. I have not evaluated the appendix's argument and do
  not restate it as the edition's position.
- **Whether the nineteen-year cycle inside 2850/1050 reached India as a cycle, or
  as the two numbers, I do not know.** What the text has is the two numbers.
