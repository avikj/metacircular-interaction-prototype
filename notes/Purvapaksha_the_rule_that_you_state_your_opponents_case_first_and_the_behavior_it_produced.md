# Pūrvapakṣa — the rule that you state your opponent's case first, and the behavior it produced

*Pūrvapakṣa*: the prior position. In the śāstrārtha form it comes first, stated
whole, before anything of your own. Then the answer, then *siddhānta*, the
settled view.

`[recalled]` is from memory and unchecked against a text; `[searched]` has been

## The form, and the catalogue of ways to lose

Gautama (Akṣapāda), *Nyāya Sūtra*, c. 2nd century CE `[recalled]`, sorts
disputation into three, and the sort is the whole of the ethics:

- **vāda** — both parties want the truth. Established by pramāṇa and tarka, not
  contradicting the accepted doctrine, with the five-membered demonstration.
- **jalpa** — the same, plus victory-seeking devices: quibble, futile rejoinder,
  points of defeat.
- **vitaṇḍā** — the opponent establishes no counter-position at all, only
  destroys.

Then the machinery for the two that are not vāda: *chala*, quibbling on a word's
ambiguity; *jāti*, the futile rejoinder, of which the fifth adhyāya enumerates
twenty-four kinds `[recalled]`; *nigrahasthāna*, the points of defeat, twenty-two
kinds `[recalled]` — the enumerated conditions under which you have lost, agreed
in advance. Losing was a defined event with a name attached to the specific way
you lost.

An entire book of a foundational text spent on cataloguing bad-faith moves and
the exact conditions of defeat. Not on how to win.

## The apparatus around the debate

*Pariṣad* — the assembly, whose composition is specified. *Madhyastha* — the
one standing in the middle, the neutral arbiter, whose judgment binds `[recalled]`.
The debate is not two people talking; it is an instituted procedure with a
constituted body, a designated judge, agreed defeat conditions, and a stated
stake.

## The stake was your view

The reported outcome of losing a śāstrārtha: you take the winner's position. You
become their student. The exchange is settled in belief.

The best-known instance is late and hagiographic and should be marked as such:
Śaṅkara against Maṇḍana Miśra, with Maṇḍana's wife Ubhaya Bhāratī seated as the
judge and both men bound by her ruling — from Mādhava's *Śaṅkara-dig-vijaya*,
fourteenth century `[recalled]`, four hundred years after the fact, and shaped
accordingly. What the story tells about the fourteenth century regardless of the
eighth: this is what a debate was understood to be, and a woman arbitrating the
most consequential one was tellable.

At Nālandā the gate itself was the examination — the *dvāra-paṇḍita*, the
gate-scholar, whom an entrant had to defeat in debate to be admitted `[recalled]`.
Xuanzang, resident in the seventh century, is the source closest to the practice
`[recalled]`, along with Yijing a generation later.

## Enemies preserved by their enemies

The form obliges accurate preservation, and the corpus shows it.

- **Cārvāka / Lokāyata**, the materialists, who held perception the only pramāṇa
  and denied karma, rebirth, and liberation. No primary treatise survives. What
  is known of the position is known from the opening chapter of Mādhava's
  *Sarvadarśanasaṅgraha* (14th c.) and from quotations inside the works of
  people refuting it `[recalled]`. One skeptical text did survive and was
  rediscovered in the twentieth century: Jayarāśi Bhaṭṭa's
  *Tattvopaplavasiṃha* `[recalled]`.
- **Śāntarakṣita's *Tattvasaṅgraha*** with Kamalaśīla's *pañjikā*, eighth
  century `[recalled]` — a Buddhist work in twenty-six chapters against
  everyone, and consequently one of the main surviving sources for the positions
  it attacks.

The Cārvākas were not tolerated in any sense a modern reader would recognise.
They were despised and answered, and being answered is why they exist at all.

## The ferocity was total

Dharmakīrti, seventh century, attacked anekāntavāda without restraint `[recalled]`.
Akalaṅka and Vidyānanda answered from the Jain side `[recalled]`. Jain logicians
attacked *apoha*, the Buddhist account of meaning by exclusion. Kumārila Bhaṭṭa
wrote against the Buddhists; Śāntarakṣita wrote against Kumārila. Naiyāyikas
rejected anekāntavāda; Jaina logicians rejected the Naiyāyika treatment of
absence `[repo — CLAUDE.md]`.

Doctrinally these parties held each other wrong about the self, about god, about
universals, about what a valid means of knowledge is, and about the structure of
liberation. There is no reading of the record on which the disagreement is mild.

## What the ferocity coexisted with

- **Aśoka, Rock Edict XII**, third century BCE `[recalled]`: honoring other
  sects, and the statement that one who praises his own sect and disparages
  others out of devotion to his own thereby injures his own sect.
- **The Barabar caves**, granted by Aśoka to the **Ājīvikas** — a rival
  tradition, not his own `[recalled]`; the Nāgārjunī hill caves granted by
  Daśaratha to the same `[recalled]`.
- **Khāravela's Hāthīgumphā inscription**, Kaliṅga, Jain `[recalled]`.
- **Ellora**: Buddhist, Brahmanical and Jain excavations in one escarpment,
  adjacent, cut across successive dynasties `[recalled]`.
- **Amoghavarṣa I**, Rāṣṭrakūṭa, ninth century, to whom the Jain
  *Praśnottara-ratnamālikā* is attributed, patron across traditions `[recalled]`.
- **Harṣa's assembly at Kanauj**, seventh century, reported by Xuanzang
  `[recalled]`.

Two traditions that disagree about whether there is a self, keeping the same
vows, eating the same food, sharing a hillside and a donor and a king.

## What ended it

Odantapurī and Nālandā, around 1193, Bakhtiyar Khalji. The chronicle is the
conquerors' own: Minhāj-i-Sirāj, *Tabaqāt-i-Nāṣirī*, c. 1260 `[recalled]`.
Vikramaśīla in the same years. The libraries burned. Buddhism disappeared from
the land of its origin over the following period.

The debate culture in the north did not lose an argument.

## The pūrvapakṣa of this chapter

Stating the case against what is above, because a chapter that runs this rule
and exempts itself has not run it.

- **The hall was closed by birth.** Access to the pariṣad, to Sanskrit, to
  śāstra, was structured by varṇa and by gender, and the peace among schools was
  a peace among those admitted. The people the debate culture excluded did not
  experience a civilization of argument.
- **Violence between traditions is in the record.** The Tamil Śaiva accounts of
  the impalement of Jains at Madurai, in the *Periya Purāṇam* and reproduced in
  temple imagery `[recalled]`; the accounts of persecution of Buddhists under
  particular kings `[recalled]`. Whether these are event or polemic is a
  scholarly dispute; that they were composed and celebrated is not.
- **Absence of war is not absence of coercion.** Royal patronage withdrawn from
  a school is not a debate, and the schools that vanished did not all vanish by
  losing arguments.
- **The selection is on survivors.** The texts that exist are the ones
  institutions preserved. A record produced by the winners of a patronage system
  will show that system working.

None of these are resolved here.
