## The disagreement has a name, and it is counted

Siddharṣigaṇin, opening his commentary on the *Nyāyāvatāra*, sets out the whole
argument before he begins it (*Nyāyāvatāravivṛti* 0.7):

> iha ca lakṣaṇa-saṃkhyā-gocara-phaleṣu pramāṇaṃ prati vipratipadyante pare —

*Here others disagree about the mark, the number, the object and the fruit, with
respect to the pramāṇa.* Four places. He then fills all four in, school by
school.

**On the mark:**

> pramāṇam avisaṃvādi jñānam iti saugatāḥ. anadhigatārthādhigantṛ pramāṇam iti
> mīmāṃsakāḥ. arthopalabdhihetuḥ pramāṇam iti naiyāyikādayaḥ.

Buddhists: a cognition that does not belie. Mīmāṃsakas: what apprehends an
object not already apprehended. Naiyāyikas: the cause of the apprehension of an
object.

**On the number:**

> pratyakṣānumāne dve eva pramāṇe iti saugatāḥ.
> pratyakṣānumānaśabdopamānārthāpattyabhāvāḥ pramāṇānīti mīmāṃsakāḥ.
> pratyakṣānumānaśabdopamānāni pramāṇānīti naiyāyikāḥ.
> pratyakṣānumānaśabdāni pramāṇānīti vaiśeṣikāḥ. etāny eva sāṃkhyāḥ.
> pratyakṣam evaikaṃ pramāṇam iti cārvākāḥ.

Two, six, four, three, three, one.

**On the object:** the Bauddhas say the real object is the *svalakṣaṇa*, "atoms
momentarily perishing and mutually unmixed"; the Mīmāṃsakas, a thing that is
both universal and particular; the Naiyāyika-Vaiśeṣikas, universal and
particular *mutually divided*; the Sāṃkhyas, a universal of the form of the
three *guṇas*; the Cārvākas, the four elements.

**On the fruit:** the Bauddhas say the fruit is *arthādhigati*; the Mīmāṃsakas
say each earlier item is the instrument and each later item the fruit.

Dignāga's own prose on the first verse of the *Pramāṇasamuccaya* gives the same
word as his reason for writing:

> …parapramāṇapratiṣedhāya svapramāṇaguṇodbhāvanāya ca, yasmāt
> pramāṇāyattā prameyapratipattir **bahavaś cātra vipratipannāḥ**.

*To refute others' pramāṇas and to bring out the merits of his own — since the
apprehension of the knowable depends on the pramāṇa, and many here are in
disagreement.*

Dharmottara, three centuries later, uses the same four-way count as the
skeleton of his commentary on the *Nyāyabindu*: "evam iha saṃkhyā-lakṣaṇa-
phalavipratipattayaḥ, pratyakṣapariccheda tu gocaravipratipattir nirākṛtā" —
in this chapter the disagreements about number, mark and fruit; in the
perception chapter, the disagreement about object.

Three authors, three schools, five centuries, one enumeration of what is being
argued about.

## What Gautama counted

*Nyāyasūtra* 1.1.1 lists sixteen categories and makes release turn on knowing
them:

> pramāṇa-prameya-saṃśaya-prayojana-dṛṣṭānta-siddhānta-avayava-tarka-nirṇaya-
> vāda-jalpa-vitaṇḍā-hetvābhāsa-chala-jāti-nigrahasthānānāṃ tattvajñānān
> niḥśreyasādhigamaḥ

Count them: pramāṇa, prameya, saṃśaya, prayojana, dṛṣṭānta, siddhānta, avayava,
tarka, nirṇaya, vāda, jalpa, vitaṇḍā, hetvābhāsa, chala, jāti, nigrahasthāna —
sixteen. Four of the sixteen are names for kinds of bad argument.

1.1.3: **pratyakṣānumānopamānaśabdāḥ pramāṇāni.** Four.

1.1.4: **indriyārthasannikarṣotpannaṃ jñānam avyapadeśyam avyabhicāri
vyavasāyātmakaṃ pratyakṣam.** Perception is a cognition arisen from the contact
of sense and object, not-designatable, non-deviating, of the nature of
determination. One definition, four qualifiers. Every one of the four will be
attacked by name.

1.1.5: inference is threefold — **pūrvavat, śeṣavat, sāmānyatodṛṣṭa.**

1.1.7: **āptopadeśaḥ śabdaḥ.** Verbal knowledge is the instruction of a reliable
person.

1.1.32: **pratijñā-hetu-udāharaṇa-upanaya-nigamanāni avayavāḥ.** Five members,
each defined in the next five sūtras: the statement of what is to be proved
(1.1.33); the reason, which proves by similarity to the example (1.1.34), or by
dissimilarity (1.1.35); the example (1.1.36–37); the application, "so it is" or
"so it is not," with reference to the example (1.1.38); the conclusion, the
restatement of the thesis on the strength of the reason (1.1.39).

1.2.4: **savyabhicāra-viruddha-prakaraṇasama-sādhyasama-kālātītāḥ
hetvābhāsāḥ.** Five pseudo-reasons, each given a one-line definition in 1.2.5–9;
1.2.5 is **anaikāntikaḥ savyabhicāraḥ** — the deviating reason is the
non-restricted one. Hold that word.

Vātsyāyana's *Nyāyabhāṣya* opens, before the first sūtra, with a single
sentence:

> pramāṇato 'rthapratipattau pravṛttisāmarthyād arthavat pramāṇam.

*Since effective activity follows upon the apprehension of an object through a
pramāṇa, the pramāṇa has an object.* He then unfolds the sentence: without a
pramāṇa there is no apprehension of an object; without apprehension no capacity
for activity; a man, having known the object, wants it or wants to be rid of it;
his exertion is *pravṛtti*; its capacity is its connection with the fruit; and
the object is pleasure and the cause of pleasure, pain and the cause of pain.
Then the count that governs the rest:

> tatra yasyepsājihāsāprayuktasya pravṛttiḥ sa **pramātā**, sa yenārthaṃ
> pramiṇoti tat **pramāṇam**, yo 'rthaḥ pramīyate tat **prameyam**,
> yad arthavijñānaṃ sā **pramitiḥ**, catasṛṣu caivaṃvidhāsv arthatattvaṃ
> parisamāpyate.

Four items, and reality is complete in them. And *tattva* itself is defined:
**sataś ca sadbhāvo 'sataś cāsadbhāvaḥ** — the being of what is and the
non-being of what is not.

Vātsyāyana then states, at once, how the non-being is known:

> katham uttarasya pramāṇenopalabdhir iti? saty upalabhyamāne tadanupalabdheḥ
> **pradīpavat**. yathā darśakena dīpena dṛśye gṛhyamāṇe tad iva yan na gṛhyate,
> tan nāsti… tad evaṃ sataḥ prakāśakaṃ pramāṇam asad api prakāśayati.

*As with a lamp: when what is visible is being grasped by an illuminating lamp,
whatever is not grasped like it, is not there.* The pramāṇa that reveals what is,
also reveals what is not. That is non-apprehension used as a means of knowledge,
in the fifth century, without a condition attached to it. The condition is what
the argument will be about.

The regress question is already in the sūtras. 2.1.17: if the pramāṇas are
established by pramāṇas, there follows the establishing of pramāṇas by further
pramāṇas. 2.1.18: or if that stops, the prameya is established as the pramāṇa
was. 2.1.19, the answer: **na, pradīpaprakāśasiddhivat tatsiddheḥ** — no,
because it is established as the light of a lamp is. 2.1.20:
**kvacin nivṛttidarśanād anivṛttidarśanāc ca kvacid anekāntaḥ** — in some cases
cessation is seen, in some it is not, hence *anekānta*.

*Anekānta* is here the Naiyāyika word for "not restricted, hence inconclusive."
It is also the word that names the position of the Jaina school. Both usages are
below.

## Dignāga cuts four to two, and prints the reason

*Pramāṇasamuccaya* 1.1, the salutation, in Steinkellner's reconstruction:

> pramāṇabhūtāya jagaddhitaiṣiṇe praṇamya śāstre sugatāya tāyine |
> pramāṇasiddhyai svamatāt samuccayaḥ kariṣyate viprasṛtād ihaikataḥ ||1||

The vṛtti glosses each epithet as a limb of the Buddha's attainment of cause and
fruit, and then says what the book is: **svaprakaraṇebhyo nyāyamukhādibhya iha
samāhṛtya pramāṇasamuccayaḥ kariṣyate** — a *collection*, gathered here into one
place out of his own scattered treatises, the *Nyāyamukha* and the rest. The
title is a bibliographical fact about its author's earlier books.

Then, immediately:

> tatra **pratyakṣam anumānaṃ ca pramāṇe** — te dve eva. yasmāt
> **lakṣaṇadvayam | prameyam** — na hi svasāmānyalakṣaṇābhyām anyat prameyam
> asti. svalakṣaṇaviṣayaṃ ca pratyakṣaṃ sāmānyalakṣaṇaviṣayam anumānam iti
> pratipādayiṣyāmaḥ.

Perception and inference are the pramāṇas — *those two only*. Because the
knowable has two marks: there is no knowable other than the particular and the
general. The argument is not that the other candidates fail one by one. It is
that the count of pramāṇas is fixed by the count of kinds of object, and there
are two.

What that argument disposes of is then listed, with the reason in each case:

- the cognition of the *combination* (sandhāna) of a particular with a general
  is **na pramāṇāntaram** — because the two marks were already got by the two
  pramāṇas, and putting them together adds no third route;
- repeated cognition of the same object, **na ca punaḥ punar abhijñāne
  'niṣṭhāsakteḥ** — if every cognition were counted a pramāṇa there would be no
  end to them;
- memory and the rest, **smṛtādivat** — memory and dislike and so on, having an
  object already apprehended, are not further pramāṇas.

Verbal testimony goes in the second chapter. Vācaspati Miśra, commenting on
*Nyāyasūtra* 1.1.7, reports the attack and quotes the line:

> atra śabdaṃ pramāṇāntaram asahamāno dignāgaḥ tallakṣaṇaṃ vikalpyākṣipati —
> āptopadeśa iti… yathoktaṃ bhadantena
> **āptavākyāvisaṃvādasāmānyād anumānatā** iti.

*Dignāga, not tolerating verbal testimony as a further pramāṇa, splits its
definition into alternatives and objects to it… as the reverend one has said:
"from the general fact that a reliable person's statement does not belie, it is
inference."* Dignāga's reconstruction of the route: a word is not the object and
is not the object's effect, since it is produced by the speaker's wish to speak
even without the object; so a word yields the wish to speak, the wish yields the
intention, the intention rests on a cognition, and the cognition is the object's
effect — an inference in four steps, and therefore not a fifth kind of knowing.

Perception, in 1.3, is defined in three words and the definition's key term is
then itself defined by a five-item list:

> **pratyakṣaṃ kalpanāpoḍham** — yasya jñānasya kalpanā nāsti tat pratyakṣam.
> atha keyaṃ kalpanā nāma. **nāmajātyādiyojanā** ||3||

The joining of name, universal, and so forth. The vṛtti enumerates the five
kinds of word that do the joining, with an example each:

| kind of word | example | what is joined |
|---|---|---|
| nāmaśabda | *ḍittha* | an arbitrary name |
| jātiśabda | *gauḥ* | a universal |
| guṇaśabda | *śukla* | a quality |
| kriyāśabda | *pācaka* | an action |
| dravyaśabda | *daṇḍī, viṣāṇī* | a substance possessed |

Perception is what is left when all five are absent.

## He begins the polemic by disowning his teacher's book

Before the Naiyāyika, before the Vaiśeṣika, PS 1.13 examines the *Vādavidhi*:

> **na vādavidhir ācāryasyāsāro veti niścayaḥ |**
> **anyathāvayavaproktes tenāsmābhiḥ parīkṣyate** ||13||

The vṛtti names the teacher: *na hi vādavidhir ācārya-**vasubandhor**, athavā
ācāryasya tatrāsāraniścayaḥ. katham? anyathāvayavaprokteḥ.* Either the
*Vādavidhi* is not Vasubandhu's, or, if it is, it is not of his substance —
because its statement of the members of a proof is otherwise. And having said
so, *tenāsmābhir api pramāṇādiṣu kiṃcit parīkṣaṇīyam*, he examines it anyway.

The *Vādavidhi* is lost. Its fragments were collected by Erich Frauwallner and
printed as *Vasubandhu's Vādavidhiḥ*, WZKSO 1 (1957) 104–146; Frauwallner had
already published on the Buddhist logicians' fragments in the *Nyāyavārttika* in
1933. The book survives in quotations by the man who doubted it was his
teacher's, in a commentary on those quotations, and in the polemics of the
opposing school.

## Then he quotes the *Nyāyasūtra* and takes the definition apart word by word

PS 1.16 introduces the Naiyāyikas by citing their sūtra:

> **naiyāyikānāṃ** tv indriyārthasannikarṣotpannaṃ jñānam avyapadeśyam
> avyabhicāri vyavasāyātmakaṃ pratyakṣam iti.
> atrāpi viśeṣaṇāny ayuktāni, yasmāt —
> **indriyārthodbhave nāsti vyapadeśyādisambhavaḥ** |

The qualifiers are unwarranted, because when the cognition arises from sense and
object there is no possibility of its being designatable and so on. The vṛtti
takes them in order:

- *avyapadeśya*, non-designatable: a qualifier is added where deviation is
  possible; sense-cognition has no designatability at all, since being
  designated belongs to inference's object; and a sense-cognition grasps only
  its own object, which cannot be wholly stated. **tasmād viśeṣaṇavacanaṃ naiva
  kartavyam.**
- *avyabhicārin*, non-deviating: the deviating cases are objects of mental
  error, not of sense.
- *vyavasāyātmaka*, of the nature of determination: *vyavasāyo 'pi hi niścayaḥ.
  sa sāmānyādivad gavādi no vikalpya adarśanān na sambhavati* — determination is
  ascertainment, and ascertainment of "cow" and the like is impossible without
  conceptual construction.

Then 1.17–18 attack *sannikarṣotpanna* itself: the senses do not operate outside
their physical seats — **adhiṣṭhānād bahir nākṣam**, established from the
efficacy of medical applications at the seat; if they did, there would be no
grasping across an intervening gap and no grasping of what is larger than the
organ, which is what is in fact observed. And the definition is too narrow:
pleasure and the rest would not be knowable at all, unless the mind is admitted
as a further sense — **mano vāstv indriyāntaram**.

The architecture of the chapter is five refutations, each of a *named text*:

| §  | text refuted | verses |
|---|---|---|
| Dignāga's own account | — | 1.2–12 |
| Vasubandhu, *Vādavidhi* | quoted | 1.13–16 |
| Gautama, *Nyāyasūtra* 1.1.4 | quoted verbatim | 1.16–20 |
| Kaṇāda, *Vaiśeṣikasūtra* 3.1.13 (and 8.6, 8.7, 4.1.11, 1.1.7, 1.2.8–10) | quoted | 1.20–24 |
| the Sāṃkhya *Ṣaṣṭitantra*, and **Mādhava** | quoted | 1.25–33 |
| Jaimini, *Mīmāṃsāsūtra* 1.1.4 | quoted | 1.34–44 |

The Sāṃkhya section names a dissident inside that school: *pūrveṣāṃ kāpilānām
abhimatātikramāt **sāṅkhyanāśako mādhavas** tv āha* — "Mādhava the destroyer of
Sāṃkhya, transgressing what the earlier Kāpilas held, says…". Dignāga sets out
Mādhava's theory that primal matter consists of different atoms configured by
the three constituents, refutes it, and records at 1.31 that on one point
Mādhava's view is preferable to the traditional one. The chapter closes:
**evaṃ parābhimataṃ pratyakṣam atra pramāṇaṃ nopapadyate doṣavac ca vākyam.
prathamaḥ paricchedaḥ.**

## Where the texts are

The *Pramāṇasamuccaya* has not been found in Sanskrit. It survives in two
Tibetan translations, one supervised by Vasudhararakṣita with Seṅ rgyal, one by
Kanakavarman with Dad pa'i śes rab. Masaaki Hattori edited both and translated
the perception chapter from them and from the Sanskrit fragments (Harvard
Oriental Series 47, 1968).

The only known commentary on it, Jinendrabuddhi's *Viśālāmalavatī
Pramāṇasamuccayaṭīkā*, second half of the eighth century, exists in a single
palm-leaf manuscript kept in Lhasa. Ernst Steinkellner, Helmut Krasser and Horst
Lasic edited chapter 1 from photocopies of it, published jointly by the China
Tibetology Publishing House and the Austrian Academy of Sciences in 2005 as the
first volume of a series set up under a general agreement signed 9 January 2004.
Steinkellner's reconstruction of the root text, dated Vienna, April 2005, was put
on the web, with the request that corrections be mailed to the institute; its
dedication is to Hattori on his eightieth birthday.

Steinkellner's second footnote records where another book is:

> "Luo Zhao Dignāga's Nyāyamukha is known to exist in the Potala as part of a
> bundle of paper manuscripts which also contains other unique Sanskrit texts,
> but it was not photographed, evidently because of its assumed minor value in
> addition to the fact of its being partly burnt."

The *Nyāyamukha* is otherwise known from Xuanzang's Chinese translation, Taishō
1628; Giuseppe Tucci translated that into English in 1930.

The *Nyāyapraveśa* was translated by Xuanzang as Taishō 1630 and was the first
Indian logic text rendered into Chinese. Its authorship has been argued: V.
Bhattacharya and S. C. Vidyabhusana gave it to Dignāga; the Chinese and Tibetan
traditions and the modern consensus give it to Śaṅkarasvāmin.

It survives in Sanskrit because a Jaina wrote a commentary on it. Haribhadrasūri
opens the *Nyāyapraveśapravṛtti*:

> śrīsarvajñāya namaḥ //
> samyagjñānasya vaktāraṃ praṇipatya **jineśvaram** /
> nyāyapraveśakavyākhyāṃ sphuṭārthāṃ racayāmy aham //

Salutation to the Omniscient; having bowed to the Lord of the Jinas, speaker of
right knowledge, I compose a commentary on the *Nyāyapraveśaka* with its meaning
made clear. He then expounds, sūtra by sūtra and compound by compound, a manual
of Buddhist logic, including the passage in which the Buddhist's doctrine that
space is a mere name is quoted at length as the reason a Vaiśeṣika argument fails
against him.

**And the verse that performs the reduction survives in Sanskrit in the mouth of
the man refuting it.** *Pramāṇasamuccaya* 2.5 reads, as it is standardly
reconstructed:

> āptavākyāvisaṃvādasāmānyād anumānatā |
> anumeye 'tha tattulye sadbhāvo nāstitāsati ||

Its first half is quoted entire, as *bhadanta*'s words, in Vācaspati Miśra's
*Tātparyaṭīkā* on *Nyāyasūtra* 1.1.7 — I found it at line 4085 of the GRETIL
e-text of that commentary. Its second half is cited there by its opening words,
at line 3760: *saṃprati dignāgasya svakīyalakṣaṇaprapañcārthaṃ vākyam
**anumeye 'tha tattulya ityādy** upanyasya dūṣayati* — "now, having set out
Dignāga's own sentence expanding his definition, 'in the inferable and in what
is similar to it…', he refutes it." A pratīka, not a full quotation; I say so
because the difference matters for what can be claimed to survive.

## The wheel, and what the particle in it excludes

The mark's three characteristics, in the three formulations, with the restrictive
particle *eva* marked where each text puts it:

**Dignāga, PS 2.5cd** (the reconstruction; the Sanskrit is preserved only as the
pratīka above):

> anumeye 'tha tattulye sadbhāvo nāstitāsati

Presence in the inferable and in what resembles it; absence in what lacks it.
**No *eva* at all.**

**Śaṅkarasvāmin, *Nyāyapraveśa***, as Haribhadra quotes it:

> hetus trirūpaḥ. kiṃ punas trairūpyam? **pakṣadharmatvaṃ sapakṣe sattvaṃ
> vipakṣe cāsattvam eva.**

One *eva*, on the third only. Haribhadra states what its absence from the second
buys: **sapakṣe tv ekadeśe 'pi sattvam aduṣṭam eva** — presence in only *part*
of the sapakṣa is not a defect; "and so reasons like
*prayatnānantarīyakatva*, which are absolutely excluded from the vipakṣa while
pervading only part of the sapakṣa, are thereby announced to be good reasons."

**Dharmakīrti, *Nyāyabindu* 2.5:**

> trairūpyaṃ punar liṅgasya anumeye **sattvam eva**, **sapakṣa eva** sattvam,
> asapakṣe ca **asattvam eva** niścitam.

Three *eva*s. The first after *sattvam*, the second **before** *sattvam*, the
third after *asattvam*. And **niścitam**, ascertained, at the end, which
Dharmottara says is to be carried back over all three.

The nine-celled wheel is what the second and third characteristics partition.
Take the reason's presence in the *sapakṣa* and in the *vipakṣa* each to be
total, none, or partial. Nine combinations. The *Nyāyapraveśa*'s own examples
fill them in:

| | vipakṣa: all | vipakṣa: none | vipakṣa: some |
|---|---|---|---|
| **sapakṣa: all** | sādhāraṇānaikāntika — *prameyatvān nityaḥ* | **good reason** — *kṛtakatvād anityaḥ* | vipakṣaikadeśavṛtti-sapakṣavyāpin — *anityatvāt prayatnānantarīyakaḥ* |
| **sapakṣa: none** | viruddha — *kṛtakatvān nityaḥ* | asādhāraṇānaikāntika — *śrāvaṇatvān nityaḥ* | viruddha (the *Nyāyapraveśa* gives no separate example for this cell; its four *viruddha*s are subdivided on a different axis — see below) |
| **sapakṣa: some** | sapakṣaikadeśavṛtti-vipakṣavyāpin — *anityatvād aprayatnānantarīyakaḥ* | **good reason** — *prayatnānantarīyakatvād anityaḥ* | ubhayapakṣaikadeśavṛtti — *amūrtatvān nityaḥ* |

Two cells are good; two are contradictory; five are inconclusive. I checked the
five inconclusive cells against the *Nyāyapraveśa*'s list of *anaikāntika*
reasons, which the text says is sixfold — **sa ca ṣaṭprakāraḥ ṣaḍvidhaḥ** — and
names: sādhāraṇa, asādhāraṇa, sapakṣaikadeśavṛtti-vipakṣavyāpin,
vipakṣaikadeśavṛtti-sapakṣavyāpin, ubhayapakṣaikadeśavṛtti, viruddhāvyabhicārin.

Five of the six are the five cells. The sixth is not a cell, and Haribhadra says
in as many words why it cannot be: the *viruddhāvyabhicārin* is not one reason.
It is a pair. A Vaiśeṣika says *anityaḥ śabdaḥ kṛtakatvād ghaṭavat*; a
Mīmāṃsaka answers *nityaḥ śabdaḥ śrāvaṇatvāt śabdatvavat*; and the mūla text
says of the two, quoted by Haribhadra:

> dvāv apy etāv eko 'naikāntikaḥ **samuditāv eva**. anudbhāvite tu tadabhāvaḥ.

*Both these two together are one non-restricted reason, only when combined; if
[the pair] is not brought forward, it does not exist.* The objection is then
raised that this cannot differ from the *asādhāraṇa*, since *śrāvaṇatva* was
already called *asādhāraṇa*, and the answer is that it does differ:
*parasparasāpekṣo viruddhāvyabhicārī* — the antinomic reason is one whose two
halves depend on each other; *ekakaḥ asahāyo 'sādhāraṇaḥ* — the *asādhāraṇa* is
single, unaccompanied.

Two counts of the same taxonomy, taken from two directions, differ by exactly
one item, and the item is the one the text itself marks as not being a single
reason.

The rest of the arithmetic is in the text too. Haribhadra, on the placement of
the restrictive particle in *pakṣadharma eva* (exclusion of the wrong
combination) and *sapakṣa eva* (exclusion of everything else):

> anena cāsiddhānāṃ **caturṇām** asādhāraṇasya ca vyāvṛttiḥ… anena tu
> sādhāraṇādīnāṃ **navānām** api hetvābhāsānāṃ vyāvṛttiḥ.

Four *asiddha*s plus the *asādhāraṇa* by the one; nine by the other. Four plus
one is five; five plus nine is fourteen, which is the number of *hetvābhāsa*s in
the text. Reading the nine as the remaining five *anaikāntika*s plus the four
*viruddha*s closes it. That reading is mine; the text gives the two numbers and
does not add them.

## The taxonomy argues with itself about its own size

The *Nyāyapraveśa*'s full list of defects is thirty-three: nine
*pakṣābhāsa*s (contradicted by perception, by inference, by scripture, by common
usage, by one's own statement; and the four in which the qualifier, the
qualified, both, or the relation is not established), fourteen *hetvābhāsa*s
(four *asiddha*, six *anaikāntika*, four *viruddha*), ten *dṛṣṭāntābhāsa*s (five
on the similarity side, five on the dissimilarity side —
**tatra sādharmyeṇa tāvad dṛṣṭāntābhāsaḥ pañcaprakāraḥ**).

The four *asiddha*s are *ubhayāsiddha*, *anyatarāsiddha*, *saṃdigdhāsiddha*,
*āśrayāsiddha*, with worked examples: "sound is impermanent, because it is
visible" fails for both parties, since sound is grasped by the ear; "because it
is produced" fails for one party only, against the Mīmāṃsaka or Sāṃkhya who
holds that sound is *manifested* and not made; "there is fire here, because of
smoke" fails when what is seen might be vapour or a swirl of dust; "space is a
substance, because it is a substratum of qualities" fails against the Buddhist,
for whom the subject does not exist.

Immediately after listing four, the text records an objection that there are
more, and another that there are fewer:

> nanu cānyo 'py asti asiddhaḥ, sa ca dvidhā: **pratijñārthaikadeśāsiddhaḥ**,
> yathā anityaḥ śabdo 'nityatvāt; **avyāpakāsiddhaś** ceti, yathā sacetanās
> taravaḥ svāpāt. sa kasmān noktaḥ? **ācārya āha: antarbhāvāt.** kva punar
> antarbhāva iti cet? ubhayāsiddhe.
>
> āha: yady evam **asiddhabhedadvayam evāstu** ubhayāsiddho 'nyatarāsiddhaś
> ceti, śeṣadvayasyātraivāntarbhāvāt. tathā cānyair apy uktam —
> **asiddhabhedau dvāv eva dvayor anyatarasya vā** iti.

Two more kinds are named and shown to be already covered. Then the count is
pushed the other way: if subsumption is the criterion, let there be *two*
kinds, since the remaining two also collapse into them — and an outside verse is
quoted for that position. Haribhadra's answer keeps four, on the ground that the
distinctions are established through four different channels (failure of the
subject, of the reason, doubt, and the qualifying condition), and that
instructing a pupil is the purpose of composing a treatise at all.

## Uddyotakara asks where the particle goes

Vācaspati Miśra opens the *Nyāyavārttikatātparyaṭīkā* with four verses, of which
the last states what the book is for:

> icchāmi kim api puṇyaṃ dustarakunibandhapaṅkamagnānām /
> **uddyotakaragavīnām atijaratīnāṃ** samuddharaṇāt //

*I want some merit from hauling out Uddyotakara's very aged cows, sunk in the
impassable mud of bad commentaries.* And he says why Uddyotakara wrote, in a
gloss on the first sūtra:

> yady api bhāṣyakṛtā kṛtavyutpādanam etat, tathāpi **dignāgaprabhṛtibhir
> arvācīnaiḥ kuhetusantamasasamutthāpanenācchaditaṃ śāstraṃ** na
> tattvanirṇayāya paryāptam iti **uddyotakareṇa svanibandhoddyotena tad
> apanīyata** iti prayojanavān ārambhaḥ.

Though the author of the Bhāṣya had already expounded it, the śāstra had been
covered over by the raising of a thick darkness of bad reasons by Dignāga and
the moderns who follow him, and was no longer sufficient for settling the truth;
so Uddyotakara removed it with the light of his own commentary. A few lines
later, glossing the ignorance the treatise is to dispel: **kutārkikair
dignāgaprabhṛtibhir āhitam ajñānam.**

Uddyotakara's date rests on a pun in a novel. Subandhu's *Vāsavadattā*, in the
long compound describing the heroine, has her —

> vyākaraṇeneva saraktapādena, bhāratenaiva suparvaṇā, rāmāyaṇeneva
> sundarakāṇḍacāruṇā jaṅghāyugalena virājamānāṃ, chandovicitir iva
> bhrājamānatanumadhyāṃ, nakṣatravidyām iva gaṇanīyahastaśravaṇāṃ,
> **nyāyasthitim ivodyotakarasvarūpāṃ**, bauddhasaṅgītim ivālaṅkāraprasādhitāṃ…

— "like the established Nyāya, having Uddyotakara for her form", one item in a
list of śleṣas between a woman's body and the branches of learning, next to
"like a Buddhist recital, adorned with ornaments." I read the line in the GRETIL
e-text of the *Vāsavadattā* to check the wording, and it is there as quoted.
Since Uddyotakara wrote against Dignāga, and the *Harṣacarita* fixes the other
end, the novel puts him in the later sixth century.

Vācaspati dated himself in the colophon of the *Nyāyasūcīnibandha* by a
word-numeral, *vasv-aṅka-vasu-vatsare* — 8-9-8, read in reverse as 898. In the
Vikrama era that is 841 CE; in the Śaka era, 976. The verse does not say which.

**The argument.** Uddyotakara, as Vācaspati reports him, attacks Dignāga's
formulation of the second and third characteristics by asking what the
restrictive particle is doing and where it can stand. He first objects to the
statement as given: *na hi yo 'numeye saṃś cāsaṃś ca sa śakyaḥ sann iti
vaktum* — one cannot say of what is both present and absent in the inferable
that it "is present". The reply is that this makes every reason absent-from-the-
vipakṣa into a good reason and leaves no room for the deviating one.

Then the two placements. Read as **anumeye sadbhāva eva** — "in the inferable
there is only presence" — the restriction attaches to the predicate; read as
**anumeye eva sadbhāvaḥ** — "only in the inferable is there presence" — it
attaches to the locus. Vācaspati's summary of the objection to the second:

> mā vadhāri sadbhāvo 'vadhāryatāṃ cānumeyam… anumeyaṃ khalv avadhāritaṃ
> saddhetusambhavaṃ parityajya nānyatra vartate, tataś cānumeyaṃ
> hetusambhavavyāptyāvadhāritaṃ bhavati. hetusambhavas tv anavadhāritaḥ
> sarvatraiva **tattulye ca vipakṣe ca prasṛtaḥ**.

If the restriction restricts the *locus*, then the inferable is restricted by
being pervaded by the reason, but the reason's occurrence is left unrestricted
and spreads both into what resembles the inferable and into the vipakṣa — which
is the thing the characteristic was supposed to exclude. And against the first
reading: **uttarapadabādhā ca. na hy asti sambhavo 'numeya evāsti tattulye
ceti** — there is a contradiction with the following word, since it cannot both
be "in the inferable only" and "and in what resembles it." Hence *tattulye
sadbhāva iti vyartham* — the clause about what resembles the inferable is
pointless: what has spread into the *sapakṣa* was never prohibited, so saying it
is present there adds nothing.

Vācaspati also records Uddyotakara refusing Dignāga's definition of the reason
by the word *arthaḥ*: *na hi dignāgasya mate kiñcid asti vastu yan
nāntarīyakaṃ saddhetur bhavati* — on Dignāga's own view there is no *thing* that
is an invariably-concomitant good reason, and he quotes Dignāga for it:

> sarvo 'yam anumānānumeyabhāvo **buddhyārūḍhena dharmadharmibhāvena** na bahiḥ
> sadasattvam apekṣate.

*This whole relation of inference and inferable rests on a relation of property
and property-bearer as it has climbed into the mind, and does not depend on
external existence or non-existence.*

## Dharmakīrti moves the particle, and states which fallacy each position kills

*Nyāyabindu* 2.5, again:

> anumeye **sattvam eva**, **sapakṣa eva** sattvam, asapakṣe ca **asattvam eva**
> niścitam.

The commentary preserved with it takes each word of each clause and names what
it excludes. Set out as a table, from the commentary's own sentences:

| clause | *the noun alone* excludes | *eva* excludes |
|---|---|---|
| anumeye sattvam eva | *asiddha*: "because it is visible" | *pakṣaikadeśāsiddha*: "trees are sentient, because they sleep" — since not all trees fold their leaves at night, *kecid eva* |
| sapakṣa eva sattvam | *viruddha*, which is absent from the sapakṣa | *sādhāraṇānaikāntika*, which occurs not only in the sapakṣa |
| asapakṣe cāsattvam eva | *viruddha*, which is present in the vipakṣa | *sādhāraṇa* and *vipakṣaikadeśavṛtti* |

and *niścitam* excludes, in all three, the reasons whose relation is merely
*doubtful* — **niścitagrahaṇena sandigdhāsiddhaḥ sarvo nirastaḥ**;
**sandigdhānvayo 'naikāntiko nirastaḥ, yathā sarvajñaḥ kaścid, vaktṛtvāt** —
"someone is omniscient, because he speaks", where the concomitance with the
omniscient is doubtful.

**Then the commentary says why the particle stands before the noun in the second
clause and after it in the third**, and gives the same reason both times.

On the second:

> sattvagrahaṇāt **pūrvāvadhāraṇavacanena** sapakṣāvyāpisattākasyāpi
> prayatnānantarīyakasya hetutvaṃ kathitam. **paścād avadhāraṇe** tv ayam arthaḥ
> syāt — sapakṣe sattvam eva yasya sa hetur iti — prayatnānantarīyakatvaṃ na
> hetuḥ syāt.

By putting the restriction *before* "presence", the reason
*prayatnānantarīyakatva* — being immediately consequent on effort — is stated to
be a reason even though its presence does not pervade the sapakṣa. Had the
restriction come *after*, the sense would be "that whose presence in the sapakṣa
is total", and *prayatnānantarīyakatva* would not be a reason at all.

On the third, exactly inverted:

> asattvaśabdād **hi pūrvasminn avadhāraṇe** 'yam arthaḥ syāt — vipakṣa eva yo
> nāsti sa hetuḥ. tathā ca prayatnānantarīyakatvaṃ sapakṣe 'pi sarvatra nāsti,
> tato na hetuḥ syāt. **ataḥ pūrvaṃ na kṛtam.**

Had the restriction come *before* "absence", the sense would be "that which is
absent only in the vipakṣa" — and *prayatnānantarīyakatva*, which is also absent
in part of the sapakṣa, would fail. *Therefore it was not put before.*

One example, *prayatnānantarīyakatva*, forces the particle to opposite sides of
the noun in two consecutive clauses. That is the entire content of the placement,
and the text says so.

Then the redundancy question, which is Uddyotakara's question arriving from
inside:

> nanu ca **sapakṣa eva sattvam** ity ukte, **vipakṣe 'sattvam eva** iti gamyata
> eva. tat kim artham punar ubhayor upādānaṃ kṛtam?

If the second clause is stated, the third follows; why state both? The answer is
that neither is stated for its content but for a rule about *how a proof may be
formulated*: **anvayo vyatireko vā niyamavān eva prayoktavyo nānyathā** —
positive or negative concomitance must be deployed *with the restriction*, never
without. And the counterexample if it were deployed without:

> aniyate hi dvayor api prayoge 'yam arthaḥ syāt — sapakṣe yo 'sti, vipakṣe ca
> yo nāsti sa hetur iti. tathā ca sati **sa śyāmaḥ tatputratvāt, dṛśyamāna-
> putravat** iti tatputratve hetuḥ syāt.

*He is dark, because he is her son, like the sons of hers who have been seen.* On
an unrestricted reading of both clauses this passes. And the conclusion drawn
is the practical one: since the restriction must be there, and since with the
restriction either clause implies the other, **dvayor eka eva prayoktavyo na
dvau** — in an actual proof only one of the two is to be used, not both.

## Why exactly three reasons, and not a list

*Nyāyabindu* 2.10–11 names them: **trirūpāṇi ca trīṇy eva liṅgāni. anupalabdhiḥ
svabhāvaḥ kāryaṃ ceti.** Three, and only three. 2.19–2.25 gives the derivation
rather than the list:

> svabhāvapratibandhe hi saty artho 'rthaṃ gamayet. tadapratibaddhasya
> tadavyabhicāraniyamābhāvāt. sa ca pratibandhaḥ sādhye 'rthe liṅgasya
> vastutas **tādātmyāt tadutpatteś ca**… te ca tādātmyatadutpattī
> **svabhāvakāryayor eva** iti tābhyām eva vastusiddhiḥ. pratiṣedhasiddhir api
> yathoktāyā eva **anupalabdheḥ**.

One thing makes another known only where there is a binding in the nature of
things; that binding is either identity of nature or arising-from; identity of
nature belongs to the *svabhāva* reason and arising-from to the *kārya* reason;
so those two, and no others, establish a thing; and the establishing of a
negation is by non-apprehension. Three reasons, because two relations plus one
negative route, not because three were found.

The *Svavṛtti* on *Pramāṇavārttika* 1 argues the same point against mere
non-observation. PV 1.13–14:

> na ca adarśanamātreṇa vipakṣe avyabhicāritā |
> sambhāvyavyabhicāratvāt **sthālītaṇḍulapākavat** ||13||
> yasyādarśanamātreṇa vyatirekaḥ pradarśyate |
> tasya saṃśayahetutvāc **cheṣavat** tad udāhṛtam ||14||

Non-deviation in the vipakṣa does not follow from mere non-observation, since
deviation remains possible — as with the cooking of rice in a pot; and the vṛtti:
*na hi bahulaṃ pakvadarśane 'pi sthālyantargamanamātreṇa pākaḥ sidhyati* — even
after seeing many grains cooked, being in the pot does not by itself establish
that a grain is cooked. Such an inference is *śeṣavat* — which is the name
*Nyāyasūtra* 1.1.5 gives to one of its own three kinds of inference. And PV 1.15:

> **hetos triṣv api rūpeṣu niścayas tena varṇitaḥ** |
> asiddhaviparītārthavyabhicārivipakṣataḥ ||15||

Therefore [Dignāga] described *ascertainment* in all three characteristics —
against the counter-cases of the unestablished, the contrary, and the deviating.
Three characteristics, three fallacies, matched.

## Eleven kinds of not-seeing, of which ten are one

*Nyāyabindu* 2.12 gives the negative reason its condition:

> na pradeśaviśeṣe kvacid ghaṭaḥ, **upalabdhilakṣaṇaprāptasyānupalabdheḥ** iti.

There is no pot in this place — from the non-apprehension of what *fulfils the
conditions of apprehension*. And 2.13–14 define the condition: the totality of
the other conditions of apprehension being present, together with a specific
nature — **yaḥ svabhāvaḥ satsv anyeṣu upalambhapratyayeṣu san pratyakṣa eva
bhavati sa svabhāvaviśeṣaḥ.** 2.27 states what happens without the condition:
for objects removed in place, time, or nature, the ceasing of one's own
perception does not settle their absence.

Then 2.30:

> sā ca **prayogabhedād ekādaśaprakārā.**

Eleven kinds, by difference of formulation. Sūtras 2.31 to 2.41 give them, one
per sūtra, each with an example:

| # | kind | example |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | svabhāvānupalabdhi | no smoke here — the smoke that would be perceptible is not perceived |
| 2 | kāryānupalabdhi | no unimpeded causes of smoke here — because there is no smoke |
| 3 | vyāpakānupalabdhi | no śiṃśapā here — because there is no tree |
| 4 | svabhāvaviruddhopalabdhi | no cold touch here — because of fire |
| 5 | viruddhakāryopalabdhi | no cold touch here — because of smoke |
| 6 | viruddhavyāptopalabdhi | the destruction of an existing thing is not consequent on smoke — because it depends on another cause |
| 7 | kāryaviruddhopalabdhi | no unimpeded causes of cold here — because of fire |
| 8 | vyāpakaviruddhopalabdhi | no frosty touch here — because of fire |
| 9 | kāraṇānupalabdhi | no smoke here — because there is no fire |
| 10 | kāraṇaviruddhopalabdhi | this man has no gooseflesh — because a particular fire is close by |
| 11 | kāraṇaviruddhakāryopalabdhi | this place has no man with gooseflesh — because of smoke |

Eleven, counted. Then 2.42:

> ime sarve kāryānupalabdhyādayo **daśānupalabdhiprayogāḥ svabhāvānupalabdhau
> saṃgraham upayānti.**

All these ten, beginning with *kāryānupalabdhi*, are subsumed in
*svabhāvānupalabdhi*. The list is eleven and the kinds are one. 2.43 gives the
reason for keeping the ten: the formulations differ because they affirm or deny a
*different* object by an indirect route; 2.44, because practice in seeing the
formulations produces the same exclusion in one's own case.

And 2.45–2.47 closes the whole apparatus by restating the condition and saying
where it fails: **pramāṇanivṛttāv apy arthābhāvāsiddheḥ** — from the cessation of
a means of knowing, the object's absence is not established. That is the
condition Vātsyāyana's lamp does not carry.

## Five members, three statements, one sentence, no thesis

*Nyāyasūtra* 1.1.32 gives five members. The *Nyāyapraveśa* gives
**pakṣādivacanāni sādhanam** — the statements of thesis, reason, example. The
*Nyāyabindu* gives, in full, two formulations and then removes the parts:

> tatra **sādharmyavat prayogaḥ**: yat sad upalabdhilakṣaṇaprāptaṃ san
> nopalabhyate so 'sadvyavahāraviṣayaḥ siddhaḥ, yathānyaḥ kaścid dṛṣṭaḥ
> śaśaviṣāṇādiḥ. nopalabhyate ca kaścit pradeśaviśeṣa upalabdhilakṣaṇaprāpto
> ghaṭa iti. (3.8)
>
> **kāryahetoḥ prayogaḥ**: yatra dhūmas tatrāgniḥ, yathā mahānasādau. asti ceha
> dhūma iti. (3.21)
>
> **vaidharmyavataḥ prayogaḥ**: yat sad upalabdhilakṣaṇaprāptaṃ tad upalabhyata
> eva, yathā nīlādiviśeṣaḥ. na caivam iha… (3.23)
>
> asaty agnau na bhavaty eva dhūmaḥ. atra cāsti dhūma iti kāryahetoḥ prayogaḥ.
> (3.25)

Then 3.32:

> …ity **ekenāpi vākyena** anvayamukhena vyatirekamukhena vā prayuktena
> sapakṣāsapakṣayor liṅgasya sadasattvakhyāpanaṃ kṛtaṃ bhavatīti **nāvaśyaṃ
> vākyadvayaprayogaḥ**.

One sentence does it; two are not required. And 3.34:

> dvayor apy anayoḥ prayogayor **nāvaśyaṃ pakṣanirdeśaḥ**.

In neither formulation is the statement of the thesis required — because, 3.35,
once "whatever fulfils the conditions of apprehension and is not apprehended is
an object of the usage 'absent'; and the pot which fulfils them is not
apprehended here" has been said, *sāmarthyād eva* — by sheer implication — "there
is no pot here" follows.

And 3.121 removes the example as a member:

> trirūpo hetur uktaḥ. tāvatā cārthapratītir iti **na pṛthag dṛṣṭānto nāma
> sādhanāvayavaḥ kaścid**. tena nāsya lakṣaṇaṃ pṛthag ucyate gatārthatvāt.

Five members, then three statements, then one sentence, and the thesis and the
example are not members at all.

## The counts do not move in one direction

Set the three taxonomies of defect side by side and count.

**Nyāyasūtra 1.2.4:** five *hetvābhāsa*s — savyabhicāra, viruddha,
prakaraṇasama, sādhyasama, kālātīta. A list, with definitions and no derivation.

**Nyāyapraveśa:** thirty-three defects — 9 *pakṣābhāsa* + 14 *hetvābhāsa* + 10
*dṛṣṭāntābhāsa*.

**Nyāyabindu 3:** the *hetvābhāsa*s are three, and they are *generated*. 3.109:

> evam eṣāṃ trayāṇāṃ rūpāṇām **ekaikasya dvayor dvayor vā rūpayor** asiddhau
> sandehe vā yathāyogam **asiddhaviruddhānaikāntikās trayo hetvābhāsāḥ**.

Each of the three characteristics failing, or being doubtful, singly or in pairs,
yields as appropriate the unestablished, the contrary, and the non-restricted —
three, and the sūtras before it walk the cases: first characteristic unestablished
or doubtful → *asiddha* (3.57); third unestablished → *anaikāntika* (3.66); third
doubtful → *anaikāntika* (3.68); second and third established *in reverse* →
*viruddha* (3.81–84); second unestablished with third doubtful → *anaikāntika*
(3.93); both doubtful → *anaikāntika* (3.96).

And the *pakṣābhāsa*s are four, not nine — contradicted by perception ("sound is
inaudible"), by inference ("sound is permanent"), by common recognition ("the
moon is not the moon"), and by one's own statement ("inference is not a
pramāṇa"), NB 3.49–53.

**But the *dṛṣṭāntābhāsa*s go the other way.** Dharmakīrti has no example-member
at all, and lists more defects of the example than the *Nyāyapraveśa* does. On the
similarity side, 3.124–3.127: lacking the property to be proved, lacking the
proving property, lacking both (three); the property to be proved doubtful, the
proving property doubtful, both doubtful (three); no positive concomitance,
positive concomitance not exhibited (two); positive concomitance stated in reverse
(one) — nine. The commentary counts them for me: **sādharmyeṇa nava dṛṣṭāntadoṣā
uktāḥ.** On the dissimilarity side, 3.129–3.135 runs structurally parallel and
gives nine again by my count. Eighteen against the *Nyāyapraveśa*'s ten.

The sūtras at 3.125 and 3.130 read *sandigdhasādhyadharmādayaḥ* and
*sandigdhasādhyavyatirekādayaḥ*, with *-ādi*. The text does not close those
sub-lists; the commentary's "nine" does.

**Two items get removed with a reason attached, and the reasons are not the
same.**

The *Nyāyapraveśa*'s *viruddha* is fourfold, subdivided by what the reason proves
instead: the opposite of the property's own form, of a specific property, of the
subject's own form, of a specific feature of the subject. Its example for the
second is the Sāṃkhya's argument, *parārthāś cakṣurādayaḥ saṃghātatvāt
śayanāsanādyaṅgavat* — the eye and the rest exist for another, because they are
aggregates, like the parts of a bed or seat — and Haribhadra names the parties:
*kaḥ punar evam āha? **sāṃkhyo bauddhaṃ prati**.* Dharmakīrti has two *viruddha*s
and takes the objection head on (3.86–3.92):

> nanu ca **tṛtīyo 'pi iṣṭavighātakṛd viruddhaḥ**. yathā parārthāś cakṣurādayaḥ
> saṃghātatvāc chayanāsanādyaṅgavad iti… sa iha kasmān noktaḥ? **anayor
> antarbhāvāt.** na hy ayam ābhyāṃ sādhyaviparyayasādhanatvena bhidyate. na hi
> **iṣṭoktayoḥ sādhyatvena kaścid viśeṣaḥ**.

It is included in these two, because there is no difference, in respect of being
what is to be proved, between what is *intended* and what is *stated*.

The *viruddhāvyabhicārin* is removed by a different argument (3.110–3.116):

> viruddhāvyabhicāry api saṃśayahetur uktaḥ. sa iha kasmān noktaḥ?
> **anumānaviṣaye asambhavāt.** na hi sambhavo 'sti kāryasvabhāvayor
> uktalakṣaṇayor anupalambhasya ca viruddhatāyāḥ… tasmād
> **avastudarśanabalapravṛttam āgamāśrayam anumānam āśritya** tadarthavicāreṣu
> viruddhāvyabhicārī sādhanadoṣa uktaḥ. **śāstrakārāṇām artheṣu bhrāntyā
> viparītasvabhāvopasaṃhārasambhavāt.**

It cannot occur in the domain of inference, because among the effect-reason, the
nature-reason and non-apprehension, correctly characterized, contradiction is
impossible. It was stated as a defect only for inference that rests on scripture —
inference not proceeding from the force of seeing a real thing — because the
authors of treatises can, through error, draw a conclusion of a contrary nature.

He then gives the actual pair, 3.117–3.120: a *svabhāva* reason proving that the
universal is omnipresent ("whatever is simultaneously related to its relata
standing in all places is omnipresent, as space is; and the universal is so
related"), and an *anupalabdhi* proving it is not there ("whatever fulfils the
conditions of apprehension and is not apprehended is not there; and the universal
is not apprehended in the intervals between individuals"). Two proofs of
contradictory conclusions on one subject.

So the item that will not fit the nine-celled wheel, the item Haribhadra says is
two reasons and not one, and the item Dharmakīrti says cannot arise except where
the premises come from a book — are the same item, reached three ways.

## अपोह

Dignāga's fifth chapter makes verbal knowledge a species of inference and gives
what a word says: **anyāpohena bhāṣate** — it speaks its own object by the
exclusion of others.

Dharmakīrti's *Svavṛtti* on *Pramāṇavārttika* 1.69–70:

> **abhedina iva ābhānti bhāvā rūpeṇa kenacit** ||69||
> tasyā abhiprāyavaśāt sāmānyaṃ sat prakīrtitam |
> **tad asat paramārthena yathā saṃkalpitaṃ tayā** ||70||

Things appear as if non-different, in some aspect. In accordance with that
cognition's intention the universal is declared to be real; in the highest sense
it is not real as that cognition constructs it. The vṛtti:

> vyaktivyatiriktāvyatirikta-eka-nitya-vyāpitādyākārair api naiva pratipattiḥ.
> kevalam **abhinnākārā buddhir utpadyate. tasyāḥ ka āśraya ity anyāpoha
> ucyate**… na punar vastubhūtaṃ kiñcit sāmānyaṃ nāmāsti yatheyaṃ buddhiḥ
> pratibhāti. yasmāt —
> **vyaktayo nānuyanty anyad anuyāyi na bhāsate**.

Nobody apprehends the universal as separate from the individuals, or as
inseparable, or as one, eternal and pervading. Only a cognition with an
undivided form arises. What is its ground? — that is what "exclusion of others"
is called. The individuals do not run through one another; nothing that runs
through appears.

And on who makes the distinction:

> **vyākhyātāraḥ khalv evaṃ vivecayanti na vyavahartāraḥ.** te tu svālambanam
> evārthakriyāyogyaṃ manyamānā **dṛśyavikalpyāv arthāv ekīkṛtya** pravartante.

The expositors analyse it this way, not the users of language; the users, taking
their own object to be capable of effective action, make the seen object and the
conceptualized object into one, and act.

Ratnakīrti's *Apohasiddhi*, at Vikramaśīla around the turn of the eleventh
century, opens by putting the whole doctrine at risk on a grammatical question —
the three ways of resolving the compound:

> nanu ko 'yam apoho nāma? kim idam anyasmād apohyate, asmād vānyad apohyate,
> asmin vānyad apohyata iti vyutpattyā **vijātivyāvṛttaṃ bāhyam eva**
> vivakṣitaṃ; **buddhyākāro** vā; yadi vā apohanam apoha ity **anyavyāvṛttimātram**
> iti **trayaḥ pakṣāḥ**.

Three readings: the external thing itself, as excluded from what is of another
kind; the form in the cognition; or the mere act of excluding. The objector kills
all three. The first two make *apoha* a name for something positive, which is
what the doctrine denies; the third is contradicted by what is actually
experienced —

> parvatoddeśe vahnir astīti śābdī pratītir **vidhirūpam evollikhantī** lakṣyate,
> nānagnir na bhavatīti nivṛttimātram āmukhayantī. yac ca pratyakṣabādhitaṃ na
> tatra sādhanāntarāvakāśa ity atiprasiddham.

The verbal cognition "there is fire on the hill" is observed to trace out
something *positive*, not to bring forward a mere cessation, "it is not
non-fire"; and where something is contradicted by perception there is no room
for a further proof.

Ratnakīrti's answer:

> **nāsmābhir apohaśabdena vidhir eva kevalo 'bhipretaḥ, nāpy
> anyavyāvṛttimātraṃ, kintv anyāpohaviśiṣṭo vidhiḥ śabdānām arthaḥ.**

Not the bare positive, and not mere exclusion of others, but *a positive
qualified by the exclusion of others*; and therefore no defect that attaches to
any one of the three limbs applies. He also rejects the two ways of making the
exclusion arrive *afterwards* — the *vidhivādins*' account, on which the
exclusion is settled later by implication from the positive, and the
*pratiṣedhavādins*', on which the positive is settled later from the exclusion —
on the ground that no such sequence is observed even in a first-time learner:
**na hi vidhiṃ pratipadya kaścid arthāpattitaḥ paścād apoham avagacchati.** His
model is simultaneity, with a lexical example: from the word *indīvara*, laid
down on the blue lotus, blueness flashes at the very moment the blue lotus is
cognized; likewise from *go*, laid down on what is excluded from non-cows.

Vācaspati Miśra, on the Nyāya side, raises the *anyāpoha* theorist at
*Nyāyasūtra* 2.2.66 and answers with a count:

> tad evam anyāpohavādinam utthāpya dūṣayati… **vyaktyākṛtijātayas tisro
> 'smākaṃ padārthaḥ.**

The individual, the configuration and the universal — three, ours.

## The Jainas count two, and they are not those two

The *Nyāyāvatāra*'s first verse answers both the definition-dispute and the
number-dispute in one śloka, and Siddharṣigaṇin says so:

> pramāṇaṃ **svaparābhāsi** jñānaṃ bādhavivarjitam /
> **pratyakṣaṃ ca parokṣaṃ ca dvidhā**, meya-viniścayāt // NA 1 //
>
> tatrāpi **pūrvārdhena lakṣaṇavipratipattim uttarārdhena tu
> saṃkhyāvipratipattiṃ** nirācaṣṭe.

A pramāṇa is a cognition that illuminates itself and the other and is free of
contradiction; it is twofold, direct and indirect. Two — but the cut is not
Dignāga's. Inference and verbal knowledge both fall on the indirect side, and
NAV 1.1 explains that they are still listed separately, because "others do not
even now accept that a word cannot be accounted for otherwise than by its
object", and what has not been separated cannot be spoken of as distinct from
it.

NA 5–6 takes the Buddhist definition of inference and turns the Buddhist's own
premise against the example:

> sādhyāvinābhuno liṅgāt sādhyaniścāyakaṃ smṛtam /
> anumānaṃ, **tad abhrāntaṃ pramāṇatvāt samakṣavat** // NA 5 //
> **na pratyakṣam api bhrāntaṃ** pramāṇatvaviniścayāt /
> **bhrāntaṃ pramāṇam ity etad viruddhaṃ vacanam** yataḥ // NA 6 //

Inference is non-erroneous, because it is a pramāṇa, like perception; and
perception too is not erroneous — because "an erroneous pramāṇa" is a
self-contradictory phrase. The commentary quotes the position being answered:
**sarvam ālambane bhrāntam.**

NA 27 states what direct perception properly is, and against whom:

> **kevalajñānaṃ nābhyupagamyate**, tanmatoddalanārthaṃ tallakṣaṇam
> abhidhitsur āha:
> …pratyakṣaṃ **sakalārthātmasatatapratibhāsanam** // NA 27 //

Perception is the constant appearing of the natures of all objects — that is,
omniscience — and the verse is written because that is not granted.

NA 29–30 gives the object and the mode of statement:

> **anekāntātmakaṃ vastu gocaraḥ sarvasaṃvidām** /
> ekadeśaviśiṣṭo 'rtho **nayasya** viṣayo mataḥ // NA 29 //
> nayānām **ekaniṣṭhānāṃ** pravṛtteḥ śrutavartmani /
> sampūrṇārthaviniścāyi **syādvādaśrutam** ucyate // NA 30 //

A thing of non-one-sided nature is the object of all cognitions; the object of a
*naya* is a thing qualified by one part. Because the *naya*s, each fixed on one
thing, operate on the path of scripture, the *syādvāda* scripture is called what
ascertains the complete object. The commentary sorts scripture into three:
*mithyāśruta*, which proceeds from the intention of a *durnaya* and belongs to
the outsiders; *nayaśruta*, from the *naya*s taken as reasons; and
*syādvādaśruta*.

And NA 23, the fallacy-taxonomy, keeps the Buddhist's three names and then
applies the doctrine to all of them at once:

> **asiddhas** tv apratīto yo yo 'nyathaivopapadyate /
> **viruddho** yo 'nyathāpy atra yukto **'naikāntikaḥ** sa tu // NA 23 //
>
> tatra pratipratiprāṇiprasiddhapramāṇapratiṣṭhita-**anekānta**-viruddha-
> buddhibhiḥ **kaṇabhakṣa-akṣapāda-buddhādi-śiṣyakair** upanyasyamānāḥ
> **sarva eva hetavaḥ**…

Reasons put forward by the pupils of Kaṇabhakṣa, Akṣapāda and the Buddha, whose
thinking is contradicted by the *anekānta* established by the means of knowing
evident to every living being — *all* of them. The rejection is not of a
particular inference but of the form of every inference framed one-sidedly.

**The word.** *Anaikāntika* — derived from *anekānta* — is the name of a
pseudo-reason in *Nyāyasūtra* 1.2.5, in the *Nyāyapraveśa*, in *Nyāyabindu* 3, and
in *Nyāyāvatāra* 23. *Anekānta* is the name of the doctrine in *Nyāyāvatāra* 29.
The same author's thirty-two verses carry both. And in *Nyāyasūtra* 2.1.20 the
Naiyāyika uses *anekānta* itself in the fallacy sense, of his own position's
opponent. One word, two offices, three schools.

**Whose text.** GRETIL's file, following Piotr Balcerowicz's edition, names the
author **Siddhasena Mahāmati**, not Siddhasena Divākara of the *Sanmatitarka*.
Whether the *Nyāyāvatāra* is by the earlier Siddhasena or by a later author who
knew Dharmakīrti is a live question and Balcerowicz's introduction argues it; I
have read the edited text and not the introduction, and I do not know.

## Not established here

- **I did not open a manuscript, and I do not read Tibetan or Chinese.** The
  *Pramāṇasamuccaya* text quoted above is Steinkellner's *hypothetical
  reconstruction*, published under that word, from two Tibetan translations plus
  Sanskrit fragments plus the linguistic material in Jinendrabuddhi. Wherever I
  quote PS 1, that is what is being quoted. The one place I checked Sanskrit
  against Sanskrit is PS 2.5, whose two halves I located myself in Vācaspati's
  ṭīkā — the first quoted in full, the second only by its opening words.
- **The e-texts are not proof-read.** The GRETIL headers say so in as many
  words: the *Nyāyasūtra* with the Tātparyaṭīkā "is in need of proof-reading!";
  the Ślokavārttika file, "THE TEXT IS NOT PROOF-READ!"; the *Nyāyabindu*
  database's own compiler writes "I must admit that there are a lot of errors and
  defects in this version." One place where this bears on a quotation above: the
  *Nyāyabinduṭīkā* file's lemma for NB 2.5 reads *sapakṣe eva sattvam*, which
  would put the particle in the position the same commentary spends two
  paragraphs arguing it does *not* occupy; the *Nyāyabindu* mūla file reads
  *sapakṣa eva sattvam*, and so does the ṭīkā file itself at the two later
  places where it quotes the clause back. I have printed *sapakṣa eva* and say
  so here. Elsewhere I have quoted the e-texts as they stand.
- **The 5 + 9 = 14 arithmetic in the *Nyāyapraveśa* section is my addition.** The
  text gives "four *asiddha*s and the *asādhāraṇa*" for one restriction and
  "nine, beginning with *sādhāraṇa*" for the other; it does not sum them, and it
  does not say which nine.
- **The nine cells of the wheel are reconstructed by me** from the
  *Nyāyapraveśa*'s six *anaikāntika*s with their examples, its *viruddha*
  examples, and the two good reasons *kṛtakatva* and *prayatnānantarīyakatva*. I
  did not read the *Hetucakraḍamaru*, which does not survive in Sanskrit.
- **"Nine faults of the similarity-example" is the commentary's count; "nine" on
  the dissimilarity side is mine.** And the *Nyāyabindu*'s own sūtras write
  *-ādi* twice inside those lists, so the text does not close them at nine.
- **Dharmakīrti's dates are disputed and I take no side.** Frauwallner settled
  600–660 largely on Xuanzang's silence — Xuanzang was in India c. 630–645 and
  does not mention him, while Yijing, at Nālandā in the 670s–680s, writes that he
  had become renowned in recent times. Helmut Krasser argued from Bhāviveka and
  Sthiramati for a mid-sixth-century date. I have read neither argument in the
  original.
- **Whether Uddyotakara knew Dharmakīrti** is a question in the literature — there
  is a study titled "Uddyotakara's Quotations from Dharmakīrti and Vinītadeva" —
  and if the answer is yes, the ordering assumed in the section above is wrong.
  I have not read it and I do not know.
- **The Ślokavārttika's *apohavāda* chapter I did not read.** The GRETIL text with
  Sucaritamiśra's *Kāśikā* that I opened carries only the Anumāna, Śabda,
  Upamāna, Arthāpatti, Abhāva and Citrākṣepa sections. Kumārila's argument
  against *apoha* is named in the ledger row I added and is *not* quoted anywhere
  above, because I did not have it in front of me.
- **Uddyotakara's *Nyāyavārttika* I did not read directly.** Every sentence
  attributed to him above is Vācaspati Miśra reporting or glossing him, and I have
  said so at each place.
- **I did not check *Vaiśeṣikasūtra* 3.1.13 or *Mīmāṃsāsūtra* 1.1.4 in their own
  texts.** The identifications are Steinkellner's apparatus.
- **Śāntarakṣita's *Tattvasaṅgraha*, Akalaṅka, Vidyānandin, Prabhācandra,
  Māṇikyanandin, Hemacandra, Jayanta Bhaṭṭa and Udayana are not read here.** The
  Jaina case above rests on one 32-verse text and one commentary on it, and the
  Naiyāyika rejection of *anekāntavāda* is not documented above at all — I state
  that as a gap and not as an absence.
